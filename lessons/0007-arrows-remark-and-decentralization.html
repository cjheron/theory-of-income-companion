Arrow's Remark, Decentralization, and Non-Convexities

Covers Slides 1, pp. 31–35 · About 40 min · Prerequisite: Lesson 6
By the end you can:
  1. prove Arrow's remark: expenditure minimization + "not the cheapest point" ⇒ utility maximization;
  2. exhibit the classic counterexample where a PO allocation is a quasi-equilibrium but not a CE, and state assumptions that rule it out;
  3. design endowments and ownership shares that decentralize a given PO allocation, and explain why they are not unique;
  4. draw the two pictures from slide 35 where non-convexities make the 2WT fail.
Primary source Alvarez, Slides 1, pp. 31–35. Mas-Colell, Whinston & Green §16.D, which introduces price quasi-equilibrium with transfers, gives the condition under which it is a full equilibrium, and draws the "exceptional case". The exceptional case goes back to Arrow's 1951 Berkeley Symposium paper, "An Extension of the Basic Theorems of Classical Welfare Economics".

1. Where Lesson 6 left us

The 2WT delivered a price \(p\neq0\) at which firms maximize profits and each household's \(\bar x^i\) minimizes expenditure over \(\{x\in X^i: u^i(x)\ge u^i(\bar x^i)\}\). Give household \(i\) exactly enough wealth, \(M^i=p\,\bar x^i\), to buy its PO bundle. Is \(\bar x^i\) then its utility-maximizing choice in \(\{x\in X^i: p\,x\le M^i\}\)? These are dual problems, and duality can fail at exactly one kind of point.

2. Arrow's remark S1 p.31

Arrow's remark

Let \(X^i\) be convex and \(u^i\) continuous (the slides also assume strict quasi-concavity). Suppose \(\bar x^i\) solves \(\min_{x\in X^i}p\,x\) s.t. \(u^i(x)\ge u^i(\bar x^i)\), and suppose \(\bar x^i\) is not a cheapest point of \(X^i\): there is \(\tilde x^i\in X^i\) with \(p\,\tilde x^i

Consequently, if this holds for every \(i\), the PO allocation and \(p\) form a competitive equilibrium for any ownership structure that gives each \(i\) wealth \(p\,\bar x^i\).

Proof S1 pp.32–33
  1. Suppose not. Then there is \(\hat x\in X^i\) with \(p\,\hat x\le p\,\bar x^i\) and \(u^i(\hat x)>u^i(\bar x^i)\).
  2. Mix toward the cheap point. For \(\theta\in(0,1)\) let \(x^\theta=\theta\tilde x^i+(1-\theta)\hat x\), which lies in \(X^i\) by convexity.
  3. Still good enough. As \(\theta\to0\), \(x^\theta\to\hat x\). Since \(u^i(\hat x)>u^i(\bar x^i)\) and \(u^i\) is continuous, \(u^i(x^\theta)>u^i(\bar x^i)\) for all \(\theta\) small enough. (Only continuity is needed here. Strict quasi-concavity is not.)
  4. Strictly cheaper. \(p\,x^\theta=\theta\,p\,\tilde x^i+(1-\theta)\,p\,\hat x<\theta\,p\,\bar x^i+(1-\theta)\,p\,\bar x^i=p\,\bar x^i\). The strict inequality comes from \(p\,\tilde x^i0\).
  5. Contradiction. \(x^\theta\) reaches at least utility \(u^i(\bar x^i)\) at strictly lower cost than \(\bar x^i\). That contradicts expenditure minimization.
∎
Intuition: why we need room to economize

A better bundle \(\hat x\) that costs no more than \(\bar x^i\) is not itself a contradiction, because expenditure minimization only rules out strictly cheaper ways to reach \(u^i(\bar x^i)\). We need to shave a little off \(\hat x\)'s cost without losing too much utility. Mixing in a strictly cheaper bundle does exactly that. If \(\bar x^i\) is already the cheapest point in \(X^i\), there is nothing to mix with, and the argument collapses.

3. The exceptional case: a PO allocation that is not a CE S1 p.34

Slide 34 asks for an example with \(m=2\) where \(\bar x\) minimizes expenditure but the hint warns that "the cost minimizing choice must be the cheapest point". Here is the canonical one. One household, no firms, \(X=\mathbb{R}^2_+\), endowment \(e=(0,1)\), and $$u(x_1,x_2)=\sqrt{x_1}+x_2 .$$ \(u\) is continuous, increasing and concave (so quasi-concave). Good 2 has constant marginal utility, which is the "no Inada condition" of the hint. Good 1 has infinite marginal utility at \(x_1=0\).

  • PO. The only feasible allocation (up to disposal) is \(\bar x=e=(0,1)\), so it is trivially PO.
  • The only supporting price is \(p=(1,0)\). The indifference curve through \((0,1)\), \(x_2=1-\sqrt{x_1}\), has slope \(-1/(2\sqrt{x_1})\to-\infty\) at \(x_1=0\): it is vertical at \(\bar x\). The only line through \(\bar x\) with \(A\) on one side is the vertical axis \(x_1=0\), so \(p\propto(1,0)\). At this \(p\), \(p\,\bar x=0=\min_{x\ge0}p\,x\): \(\bar x\) is a cheapest point.
  • Quasi-equilibrium, yes. Expenditure \(x_1\) is minimized at \(x_1=0\), and \((0,1)\) reaches the PO utility. ✓
  • CE, no. At \(p=(1,0)\) the budget set is \(\{x_1=0,\ x_2\ \text{anything}\}\): good 2 is free, so utility is unbounded and there is no demand. At any \(p\) with \(p_2>0\), demand is \(x_1=(p_2/2p_1)^2>0=e_1\), so good 1 is in excess demand. No CE exists, even though the 2WT's quasi-equilibrium does.
Interactive. \(q=p_1/(p_1+p_2)\) is the weight on good 1. The black line is the budget through the endowment \(e=(0,1)\); the blue curve is the indifference curve through \(e\); the orange dot is the household's demand. For every \(q<1\) the household wants positive \(x_1\), and there is none to give. At \(q=1\) the budget line becomes the vertical axis, tangent to the indifference curve at \(e\), but then good 2 is free and demand runs off to infinity.

Ruling the exceptional case out S1 p.34

Slide 34's last question: what assumptions on \(u^i\), \(X^i\) guarantee that no PO bundle is a cheapest point? A clean sufficient set:

  1. \(X^i=\mathbb{R}^m_+\), so \(0\in X^i\) and \(p\cdot0=0\).
  2. Preferences are monotone, so \(p\ge0\) (Lesson 6, Exercise 6.5). With \(p\neq0\), some \(p_k>0\).
  3. Interiority: every PO bundle is strictly positive, \(\bar x^i\gg0\). This is guaranteed if bundles on the boundary of \(\mathbb{R}^m_+\) are worse than every strictly positive bundle, e.g. Cobb–Douglas \(\prod_kx_k^{a_k}\), or any \(u\) satisfying Inada conditions in every good, as long as each agent gets positive utility at the PO allocation.

Then \(p\,\bar x^i\ge p_k\bar x^i_k>0=p\cdot0\), so \(\tilde x^i=0\) is strictly cheaper and Arrow's remark applies. Alternative routes: assume \(p\gg0\) (e.g. strictly monotone preferences together with an interiority condition) and \(\bar x^i\neq0\); or assume each household's wealth exceeds the minimum cost of \(X^i\) (a "minimum wealth" or survival condition). In the counterexample, (3) fails: \(\bar x_1=0\), and the household has no wealth to spare at \(p=(1,0)\).

4. Decentralization: who must own what? S1 p.34

For \((p,\bar x,\bar y)\) to be a CE we need each household's wealth to be exactly the cost of its PO bundle: $$p\,e^i+\sum_j\theta^i_j\,\pi^j\ =\ p\,\bar x^i\quad\text{for all }i,\qquad \pi^j=p\,\bar y^j .$$ If wealth were higher, \(i\) would buy more than \(\bar x^i\). If lower, \(i\) could not afford it.

A decentralizing ownership structure

Pick any shares \(\theta^i_j\ge0\) with \(\sum_i\theta^i_j=1\) (e.g. \(\theta^i_j=1/I\)) and set $$e^i\ \equiv\ \bar x^i-\sum_j\theta^i_j\,\bar y^j .$$ Then (i) \(\sum_ie^i=\sum_i\bar x^i-\sum_j\bar y^j\) equals the economy's aggregate endowment, by feasibility; and (ii) \(p\,e^i+\sum_j\theta^i_j\pi^j=p\,\bar x^i-\sum_j\theta^i_jp\,\bar y^j+\sum_j\theta^i_jp\,\bar y^j=p\,\bar x^i\). ✓

Is it unique? No, far from it. Only the value \(p\,e^i+\sum_j\theta^i_j\pi^j\) matters. (i) Any shares \(\theta\) work, with \(e^i\) adjusted. (ii) The composition of \(e^i\) can change in any direction \(d^i\) with \(p\cdot d^i=0\) and \(\sum_id^i=0\). (iii) Equivalently, starting from any initial endowments, lump-sum transfers \(T^i=p\,\bar x^i-p\,e^i-\sum_j\theta^i_j\pi^j\) (which sum to zero) implement the allocation. This is the precise sense in which "any efficient allocation can be reached by markets plus lump-sum transfers".

Why this is less useful in practice than it sounds

The transfers depend on \(\bar x^i\) and \(p\), i.e. on everyone's preferences. They must also be lump-sum: independent of anything the household chooses. Real tax systems condition on income or consumption, which distorts MRS ≠ MRT (Lesson 5, Exercise 5.6(d)). The 2WT is a benchmark, not a policy recipe.

5. Non-convexities: the two failures on slide 35 S1 p.35

(a) A non-quasi-concave household in the Edgeworth box

Total endowment \((1,1)\). A has \(u^A=x_1^2+x_2^2\): strictly increasing (so LNS holds) but convex, with indifference curves that bow outward (quarter circles). B has Leontief preferences \(u^B=\min\{x^B_1,x^B_2\}\). Consider \(\bar x^A=(t,t)\) and \(\bar x^B=(1-t,1-t)\).

  • It is PO. B is at least as well off only at \(x^A\in\{x_1\le t,\ x_2\le t\}\), a square whose farthest point from A's origin is \((t,t)\). So A can't gain without hurting B.
  • It is not a CE for any prices or transfers. On any budget line through \((t,t)\), A's convex utility is maximized at an endpoint of the line, never in the middle. So A never demands \((t,t)\).
Interactive. Move \(t\) along the diagonal (every such point is PO) and tilt the price line through it. The orange L is B's indifference curve, with B's better set shaded below-left. The blue arc is A's indifference curve through \((t,t)\). However you tilt the line, A's demand (violet) jumps to an intercept, which is often outside the box, where A gets strictly higher utility. The PO point is never chosen.

(b) A fixed cost in production

One consumer, one firm, two goods: leisure \(\ell\) and consumption \(c\). The consumer has one unit of time and utility \(\log c+b\log\ell\). The firm needs a fixed labour input \(n_0\) before producing anything, and then has increasing marginal cost: $$c=\begin{cases}0 & n\le n_0\\ (n-n_0)^\gamma & n>n_0\end{cases},\qquad 0<\gamma<1 .$$ The production set has a flat segment from \(n=0\) to \(n=n_0\) followed by a concave piece, so it is not convex. The PO allocation is interior (because \(\log c\to-\infty\) at \(c=0\)), at \(\bar n=(\gamma+bn_0)/(\gamma+b)\). The only candidate price is the slope there, \(w=\gamma(\bar n-n_0)^{\gamma-1}\). At that price the firm's profit from the PO plan is $$\bar\pi=(\bar n-n_0)^{\gamma}-w\bar n=(\bar n-n_0)^{\gamma-1}\big[(1-\gamma)\bar n-n_0\big],$$ which is negative iff \(n_0>(1-\gamma)\bar n\), i.e. iff \(n_0>\frac{1-\gamma}{1+b}\). In that case the firm prefers to shut down (\(n=0\), \(\pi=0\)), and the PO allocation cannot be decentralized.

Interactive. Blue: the producible set (the flat segment at \(c=0\) between \(\ell=1-n_0\) and \(\ell=1\) is the fixed cost). Orange: the PO indifference curve. Black: the supporting line at the PO point. The violet dot is the "no production" plan \((\ell,c)=(1,0)\). When it lies above the price line, that plan is worth more than the PO plan at the supporting price, so the firm shuts down. Raise \(n_0\) past \((1-\gamma)/(1+b)\) and watch it happen.

7. Exercises

Exercise 7.1 Slides p.34 Ownership structure

Let \(\{\bar x^i,\bar y^j\}\) be PO and \(p\) the price from the 2WT, with Arrow's condition holding for every \(i\). (a) Describe endowments and shares \((e^i,\theta^i_j)\) for which \((p,\bar x,\bar y)\) is a CE. (b) Is the structure unique? (c) Starting instead from given endowments \(\hat e^i\) and shares \(\hat\theta^i_j\), what lump-sum transfers work? Show they sum to zero.

Show solution

(a) Any \(\theta\) with \(\sum_i\theta^i_j=1\), and \(e^i=\bar x^i-\sum_j\theta^i_j\bar y^j\) (§4). Firms maximize profit at \(p\) by the 2WT. Households have wealth exactly \(p\bar x^i\) and maximize utility by Arrow's remark. Markets clear because the allocation is feasible.
(b) No. Any \((e,\theta)\) with the right wealth values works, e.g. perturb \(e^i\) by \(d^i\) with \(p\cdot d^i=0\) and \(\sum_i d^i=0\).
(c) \(T^i=p\bar x^i-p\hat e^i-\sum_j\hat\theta^i_j p\bar y^j\). Then \(\sum_iT^i=p\big(\sum_i\bar x^i-\sum_i\hat e^i-\sum_j\bar y^j\big)=0\) by feasibility and \(\sum_i\hat\theta^i_j=1\).

Exercise 7.2 Slides p.34 The cheapest point

Construct an example with \(m=2\) and quasi-concave \(u\) in which \(\bar x\) minimizes expenditure at given \(p\) but does not maximize utility on \(\{x:p\,x\le p\,\bar x\}\). Draw indifference curves and the budget line. Verify every claim.

Hint

The slide's hint: one good with zero price, \(u\) increasing, no Inada condition. Make the free good one with constant marginal utility.

Show solution

\(u=\sqrt{x_1}+x_2\), \(p=(1,0)\), \(\bar x=(0,1)\) (§3 and the figure at \(q=1\)). Expenditure \(p\,x=x_1\ge0=p\,\bar x\), so \(\bar x\) minimizes expenditure, and it is the cheapest point of \(\mathbb{R}^2_+\). The budget set \(\{x_1\le0\}=\{(0,x_2)\}\) contains \((0,2)\) with \(u=2>1\), so \(\bar x\) is not a utility maximizer. The budget line is the vertical axis, tangent to the indifference curve \(x_2=1-\sqrt{x_1}\) at \(\bar x\).

Exercise 7.3 Slides p.34 Proof

Give assumptions on \(u^i\), \(X^i\) ensuring that at every PO allocation and its supporting \(p\), each \(\bar x^i\) is not a cheapest point. Prove your claim, including that the relevant prices are non-negative.

Show solution

Take \(X^i=\mathbb{R}^m_+\); \(u^i\) continuous, quasi-concave and increasing; and strictly positive bundles strictly preferred to all boundary bundles (e.g. Cobb–Douglas), with each agent getting a utility above the boundary level at the PO allocation. Then \(\bar x^i\gg0\). Monotonicity gives \(p\ge0\) (Exercise 6.5: otherwise \(\bar x+te_k\in A\) and \(p\cdot(\bar x+te_k)\to-\infty\)). Since \(p\neq0\), some \(p_k>0\), so \(p\,\bar x^i\ge p_k\bar x^i_k>0=p\cdot0\). Hence \(\tilde x^i=0\) is strictly cheaper.

Exercise 7.4 Slides p.35 Non-quasi-concave Edgeworth box

Sketch an Edgeworth box with one agent whose utility is not quasi-concave, in which some PO allocation cannot be decentralized. Prove it is PO and prove no price works.

Show solution

Use §5(a): \(u^A=x_1^2+x_2^2\), \(u^B=\min\{x_1^B,x_2^B\}\), \(\bar x^A=(t,t)\). PO: any allocation with \(u^B\ge 1-t\) has \(x^A\le(t,t)\) componentwise, so \(u^A(x^A)\le 2t^2=u^A(\bar x^A)\). Not decentralizable: on any budget line \(L\) through \((t,t)\) with \(p\ge0\), \(p\ne0\), \(u^A\) is strictly convex along \(L\), so its maximum over the segment \(L\cap\mathbb{R}^2_+\) is attained only at an endpoint. Since \((t,t)\) is interior to that segment, it is not demanded. (If \(p_1\) or \(p_2\) is 0, demand is unbounded.)

Exercise 7.5 Slides p.35 Exam-style Fixed cost

In §5(b) with \(\gamma=\tfrac12\):

  1. Show that \(\bar n=(\gamma+bn_0)/(\gamma+b)\).
  2. For \(b=1\), find the critical fixed cost above which the PO allocation can't be decentralized.
  3. For \(b=\tfrac12\) (a household that cares less about leisure), find it again. Interpret the change.
  4. Sketch the production possibility set and explain, in terms of the 2WT proof, which step fails.
Show solution

(a) Maximize \(\gamma\log(n-n_0)+b\log(1-n)\). FOC: \(\gamma/(n-n_0)=b/(1-n)\), so \(\gamma(1-n)=b(n-n_0)\) and \(n=(\gamma+bn_0)/(\gamma+b)\).
(b) \(\bar\pi<0\iff n_0>(1-\gamma)\bar n\iff n_0(\gamma+b)>(1-\gamma)(\gamma+bn_0)\iff n_0\,\gamma(1+b)>\gamma(1-\gamma)\iff n_0>\frac{1-\gamma}{1+b}=\tfrac14\).
(c) \(\frac{1/2}{3/2}=\tfrac13\). When leisure matters less, the PO plan uses more labour, which spreads the fixed cost over more output, so a larger fixed cost is tolerable before profits turn negative.
(d) The set is flat between \(\ell=1-n_0\) and \(\ell=1\), then rises concavely: it is not convex. Assumption FF fails, so Step 6 of the 2WT (firms maximize profits at \(p\)) breaks. The plan "no production" lies above the supporting hyperplane.

Exercise 7.6 Exam-style True / False / Uncertain
  1. "If \(u^i\) is strictly increasing, Arrow's condition holds automatically."
  2. "A small fixed cost always makes the 2WT fail."
  3. "If a PO allocation is supported as a quasi-equilibrium with \(p\gg0\) and every \(\bar x^i\neq0\) with \(X^i=\mathbb{R}^m_+\), it is a CE with transfers."
Show solution

(a) False. \(u=\sqrt{x_1}+x_2\) is strictly increasing, yet \(\bar x\) is the cheapest point at \(p=(1,0)\). Monotonicity gives \(p\ge0\), not \(p\gg0\) or interiority. (b) False. In §5(b) decentralization survives for \(n_0\le(1-\gamma)/(1+b)\). Non-convexity is a problem only when it binds at the supporting price. (c) True. \(p\,\bar x^i>0=p\cdot0\), so Arrow's remark applies (given continuity and convex \(X^i\)).

Key takeaways

  • 2WT ⇒ quasi-equilibrium. Arrow's remark (a strictly cheaper bundle exists, plus continuity) ⇒ full CE with transfers.
  • The exceptional case: a zero-wealth agent at a boundary with a vertical indifference curve (\(\sqrt{x_1}+x_2\), \(e=(0,1)\)). There is a quasi-equilibrium but no CE.
  • Interiority + monotonicity + \(X=\mathbb{R}^m_+\) rule it out.
  • Decentralizing wealth: \(p\,e^i+\sum_j\theta^i_j\pi^j=p\,\bar x^i\). Infinitely many \((e,\theta)\) do it, or equivalently zero-sum lump-sum transfers.
  • Non-convex preferences make households go to corners; non-convex technology (IRS, fixed costs) makes firms shut down or go to corners. Either way the 2WT fails.
