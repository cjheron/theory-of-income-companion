The Economy: Commodities, Agents, Feasibility

Covers Slides 1, pp. 1–9 · About 30 min · Prerequisite: none
By the end you can:
  1. list the primitives of an economy (commodity space, households, firms, ownership, endowments) and say what each one is;
  2. apply the sign conventions without hesitating: for a household, positive means demand and negative means supply; for a firm, positive means output and negative means input;
  3. write material balance for any economy, including one with dates and states of nature, and count its commodities.
Primary source Alvarez, Slides 1, pp. 2–9. The classic, and still the cleanest, statement of "a commodity is a good at a date, location and state" is Debreu, Theory of Value (1959), ch. 2 (commodities and prices) and ch. 7 (uncertainty). For a textbook pace, see Mas-Colell, Whinston & Green, §16.B and §19.B.

This whole course rests on one template. An economy is a short list of mathematical objects. A competitive equilibrium (Lesson 2) and a Pareto optimum (Lesson 3) are properties of allocations in that economy. Later models (OLG, Blanchard–Yaari, growth) are this same template with a particular commodity space and set of agents plugged in, so it pays to be fluent in it now.

1. The commodity space

Definition: Commodity space S1 p.2

The commodity space \(L\) is a vector space whose elements are bundles of commodities. Typically \(L=\mathbb{R}^m\): there are \(m\) goods, and a bundle \(x=(x_1,\dots,x_m)\) lists a quantity of each. Sometimes \(m=\infty\), e.g. \(L=\mathbb{R}^\infty\) in the overlapping-generations model (Lesson 8).

The key modelling idea is that a commodity is not just a physical object. Two units of the same physical good count as different commodities if they differ in any of the following S1 p.2:

Distinguished byExampleUse
characteristicswheat vs. steelclassical static GE
locationwheat in Chicago vs. in Rotterdamgeography, trade
timeconsumption in 2026 vs. 2027dynamics, saving, interest rates
state of naturean umbrella if it rains vs. if it is dryuncertainty, insurance

Because "time" and "uncertainty" are just more coordinates, every theorem we prove for \(\mathbb{R}^m\) automatically covers dynamic and stochastic economies, as long as \(m\) stays finite. Whether it survives \(m=\infty\) is precisely the OLG question of Lesson 12.

The catalogue of commodity spaces S1 pp.8–9

SpaceWhat the coordinates are
\(m=2\)static consumption and leisure (labour–leisure choice)
\(\mathbb{R}^m\)\(m\) different goods
\(m=2\times 2\)two inputs (capital, labour) and two final goods
\(m=k\times\#\text{locations}\)basic trade with goods differentiated by origin (Armington)
\(\mathbb{R}^{2T}\)consumption and investment in each of \(T\) periods
\(\mathbb{R}^{k\times\infty}\)\(k\) goods every period, infinite horizon
\(\mathbb{R}^{k}\)one period, \(k\) states of nature ("now and then")
\(\mathbb{R}^{k+1}\)two periods: one good today, plus one good in each of \(k\) states tomorrow
\(\{x_t: Z^t\to\mathbb{R}^k\}_{t\ge0}\)an event tree: \(x_t(z^t)\) is the \(k\)-vector consumed after history \(z^t=(z_0,\dots,z_t)\)

The last row is the workhorse of stochastic macro. \(Z\) is a finite set of shocks, and a history grows recursively: \(z^{t+1}=(z^t,z_{t+1})\). Each node of the tree is a separate set of \(k\) commodities. Continuous time or a continuum of states can be handled the same way (sums become integrals); the slides use it "whenever is simpler" without dwelling on the measure theory S1 p.9.

Interactive: the date–event tree. Each dot is a history \(z^t\), and at each dot the household consumes a \(k\)-vector \(x_t(z^t)\). Each dot is therefore \(k\) separate commodities with \(k\) separate prices \(p_t(z^t)\). Here \(z_0\) is known (one root). Move the sliders: the number of commodities is \(k\sum_{t=0}^{T}|Z|^t\), so it explodes geometrically with the horizon. That is why we will later prefer recursive methods to listing every commodity.

2. Households

Definition: Households S1 pp.2–3

Households are indexed by \(i=1,\dots,I\). Abusing notation, \(I\) also denotes the set of households. Each household has

  • a consumption possibility set \(X^i\subset L\): the bundles it is physically able to consume;
  • a utility function \(u^i: X^i\to\mathbb{R}\).

The set of households may be infinite: countably many (\(i=1,2,3,\dots\), as in OLG) or a continuum (as in Blanchard–Yaari, Lesson 17).

\(X^i\) is where physical constraints live: non-negativity of consumption, or a cap on hours (with 24 hours a day, labour supply cannot exceed 24). It is not the budget set. \(X^i\) contains what is possible, the budget set contains what is affordable, and the budget only appears once we have prices.

Sign convention for households S1 p.3

Positive entries of \(x^i\) are quantities demanded (purchases); negative entries are quantities offered (sales). If labour is a commodity and the household supplies 8 hours, then \(x^i_{\text{labour}}=-8\). An equivalent and common alternative is to make leisure the commodity, endow the household with 24 hours of time, and keep \(x\ge 0\). Both conventions describe the same economy; just keep track of which one you are using.

3. Firms

Definition: Firms S1 p.4

Firms are indexed by \(j=1,\dots,J\) (and \(J\) also denotes the set). Firm \(j\)'s technology is a production possibility set \(Y^j\subset L\): the firm can carry out any plan \(y\in Y^j\). Positive entries of \(y\) are outputs (offered); negative entries are inputs (demanded).

A production function is a special case. A firm turning labour \(n\) into output with \(f\) has $$Y^j=\{(c,\,-n)\;:\; n\ge 0,\ c\le f(n)\}.$$ The inequality \(c\le f(n)\) allows waste (free disposal). With this sign convention, profit is simply the value of the plan, \(p\cdot y = p_c\,c + p_n\cdot(-n) = p_c c - p_n n\): revenue minus cost, with no special formula needed.

Interactive: a production set with the sign convention. The horizontal axis is the labour coordinate \(y_n=-n\le 0\), the vertical axis is output \(y_c\). The shaded set is \(Y=\{(y_c,y_n): y_n\le 0,\ 0\le y_c\le\sqrt{-y_n}\}\). With prices \(p=(1,w)\) for (good, labour), the dashed lines are iso-profit lines \(y_c + w\,y_n=\pi\). The profit-maximizing plan is where the highest iso-profit line touches \(Y\). Raise \(w\) and the firm uses less labour.

4. Ownership and endowments

Definition: Ownership structure S1 p.5
  • Household \(i\) owns a share \(\theta^i_j\ge 0\) of firm \(j\). Every firm is entirely owned by households: $$\sum_{i\in I}\theta^i_j = 1\quad\text{for all } j\in J.$$
  • Household \(i\) owns an endowment \(e^i\in L\), a bundle it brings to the market.

Ownership is what routes firm profits back to people, so that no value "leaks out" of the economy. The condition \(\sum_i\theta^i_j=1\) is used in exactly two places: in Walras' law (Lesson 2) and in the First Welfare Theorem (Lesson 4). The pair \((e^i,\theta^i)\) is a household's wealth endowment. In the Second Welfare Theorem it is the policy instrument: redistributing \(e\) and \(\theta\) lets us reach different Pareto optima (Lesson 7).

Summary: an economy is the list
$$\mathcal{E}=\Big(L,\ \{X^i,u^i,e^i,(\theta^i_j)_{j\in J}\}_{i\in I},\ \{Y^j\}_{j\in J}\Big).$$

5. Feasible allocations

Definition: Feasible allocation S1 p.6

An allocation \(\{x^i,y^j\}\) is a consumption bundle for every household and a production plan for every firm. It is feasible if

  1. each household can consume its bundle: \(x^i\in X^i\) for all \(i\in I\);
  2. each firm can produce its plan: \(y^j\in Y^j\) for all \(j\in J\);
  3. material balance (demand equals supply): $$\sum_{i\in I}x^i \;=\; \sum_{j\in J}y^j \;+\; \sum_{i\in I}e^i .$$

Material balance is a vector equation, one equation per commodity S1 p.7: $$\begin{pmatrix}x^1_1\\ \vdots\\ x^1_m\end{pmatrix}+\cdots+\begin{pmatrix}x^I_1\\ \vdots\\ x^I_m\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}y^1_1\\ \vdots\\ y^1_m\end{pmatrix}+\cdots+\begin{pmatrix}y^J_1\\ \vdots\\ y^J_m\end{pmatrix} +\begin{pmatrix}e^1_1\\ \vdots\\ e^1_m\end{pmatrix}+\cdots+\begin{pmatrix}e^I_1\\ \vdots\\ e^I_m\end{pmatrix}.$$ Read row \(s\): total use of good \(s\) by households = total net output of good \(s\) by firms + total endowment of good \(s\). Because inputs are negative, a firm's use of good \(s\) as an input is netted out on the right-hand side automatically.

Intuition: feasibility needs no prices

Feasibility is a purely physical, planner's-eye notion: is this allocation possible with the resources and technologies available? Nothing about prices, budgets or ownership enters it. That is why Pareto optimality, which is defined over feasible allocations, is also price-free. The welfare theorems are bridges between this price-free world and the price-based world of competitive equilibrium.

Feasibility vs. budget feasibility

An allocation can be feasible without each bundle being affordable at some prices, and every household can be on its budget without markets clearing. A CE requires both. Keep the words straight: feasible refers to the aggregate resource constraint, budget-feasible to an individual's budget set.

6. Check your understanding

1. A household supplies 40 hours of labour, and labour is a commodity. What is that entry of \(x^i\)?
  • It is +40, since labour is a good the household owns
  • It is −40, since sales enter household bundles negatively
  • It is zero, since labour enters only the firm's plan
  • It is +40, since positive entries mean quantities offered
For households, positive entries are purchases and negative entries are sales S1 p.3. The firm that hires the labour has −40 in its plan too (an input), and material balance nets the two.
2. Ice cream in Chicago in July and ice cream in Chicago in January are…
  • one commodity, because the physical good is identical
  • one commodity, because the location is the same city
  • two commodities, because the delivery dates differ
  • two commodities, but only if their market prices differ
A commodity is a good at a given date, location and state S1 p.2. Whether the two prices end up equal is an equilibrium outcome; it doesn't decide what counts as a commodity.
3. Which object does the definition of a feasible allocation not involve?
  • The ownership shares and the price vector
  • The consumption sets and the endowments
  • The production sets and the endowments
  • The consumption sets and production sets
Feasibility is \(x^i\in X^i\), \(y^j\in Y^j\) and \(\sum x=\sum y+\sum e\). Shares \(\theta\) and prices \(p\) only enter budget constraints, i.e. the definition of a CE.
4. Two periods, two shocks per period after date 0, one good, with \(z_0\) known. How many commodities are there?
  • Two, one for each date of the economy
  • Four, one for each terminal history only
  • Six, one for each date at each shock
  • Seven, one for each node of the tree
With dates \(t=0,1,2\): \(1+2+4=7\) histories, each with \(k=1\) good. Set \(T=2\), \(|Z|=2\), \(k=1\) in the tree figure to check.

7. Exercises

Alvarez's midterms use two formats: true/false statements with a short justification (about half the points on Midterm 1), and long derivations broken into small, hinted steps. The exercises below follow both. Lesson 1 is mostly vocabulary, so the exam questions that touch it test whether you can write an economy down correctly before solving anything.

True or false? Justify. Exam format

For each statement, answer True or False and justify in at most four lines.

  1. An apple delivered today and an identical apple delivered tomorrow are the same commodity. exam-inspired
    Answer

    False. Commodities are distinguished by physical characteristics, location, date and state of nature. Each date gets its own coordinate in \(L\) and its own price; the ratio \(p_t/p_{t+1}=1+r_t\) is precisely the interest rate. Merging them would make intertemporal trade impossible to describe.

  2. Whether an allocation is feasible depends on the equilibrium prices. exam-inspired
    Answer

    False. Feasibility is \(x^i\in X^i\), \(y^j\in Y^j\) and \(\sum_i x^i=\sum_j y^j+\sum_i e^i\): purely physical. Prices enter only the budget and profit problems, i.e. the definition of a competitive equilibrium.

  3. Changing the ownership shares \(\theta^i_j\) changes the set of feasible allocations. exam-inspired
    Answer

    False. The shares appear nowhere in feasibility. They only redistribute profit income across budget constraints, so they change which feasible allocation is a CE, never what is feasible. (This is the lever the Second Welfare Theorem uses, Lesson 7.)

  4. In the standard sign convention, a household that sells labour has a negative entry for labour in \(x^i\), and a firm that hires labour has a negative entry for labour in \(y^j\). exam-inspired
    Answer

    True. For households, positive = purchases, negative = sales; for firms, positive = outputs, negative = inputs. Material balance then reads \(\sum_i x^i_{\text{lab}} = \sum_j y^j_{\text{lab}} + \sum_i e^i_{\text{lab}}\): both sides negative when there is no labour endowment entry, i.e. sales by households equal purchases by firms.

  5. If every \(X^i\) and every \(Y^j\) is convex, the set of feasible allocations is convex. exam-inspired
    Answer

    True. Convex combinations stay in each \(X^i\) and \(Y^j\), and material balance is linear, so a convex combination of two feasible allocations is feasible (Exercise 1.4 writes it out).

  6. With one good per node, a date-event tree with \(|Z|\ge2\) branches per period and dates \(t=0,\dots,T\) (with \(z_0\) known) has \(|Z|^{T}\) commodities. exam-inspired
    Answer

    False. \(|Z|^T\) counts only the terminal nodes. Every node is a commodity: \(\sum_{t=0}^{T}|Z|^t=\frac{|Z|^{T+1}-1}{|Z|-1}\). (Exercise 1.1.)

Exercise 1.1 Slides pp.8–9 (dimension of the commodity space)

Give \(\dim L\) as a formula, and say what a single coordinate means, in each case.

  1. A static economy with \(k\) final goods and \(n\) primary inputs. (5 pts)
  2. \(T\) periods, with one consumption good and one investment good per period. (5 pts)
  3. Two periods: \(k\) goods today, and \(k\) goods tomorrow in each of \(S\) states of nature ("now and then"). (5 pts)
  4. A date-event tree: dates \(t=0,\dots,T\), \(z_0\) known, \(|Z|\ge2\) branches per period, \(k\) goods at each node. (10 pts)
  5. What happens to your answer in (d) as \(T\to\infty\), and which result in this course needs extra care because of that? One line. (5 pts)
Show solution

(a) \(k+n\). A household has positive entries for final goods and negative entries for inputs it sells; a firm the reverse.
(b) \(2T\); coordinate \((c_t, i_t)\) at each date.
(c) \(k(1+S)\): \(k\) spot goods today plus \(kS\) contingent claims \(x_{1\ell}(s)\), each delivering good \(\ell\) tomorrow only if state \(s\) occurs.
(d) There are \(|Z|^t\) histories \(z^t\) at date \(t\), so \(\dim L = k\sum_{t=0}^T|Z|^t = k\,\dfrac{|Z|^{T+1}-1}{|Z|-1}\).
(e) It becomes infinite. With infinitely many commodities, values such as \(p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i\) can be infinite, and the First Welfare Theorem proof needs that value to be finite (Lesson 12).

Exercise 1.2 Exam-inspired (write the economy down)

A two-period economy has one consumption good per period. Household 1 is endowed with one unit of the good at \(t=0\) and nothing at \(t=1\); household 2 has nothing at \(t=0\) and one unit at \(t=1\). A single firm has a storage technology: storing \(s\ge0\) units at \(t=0\) yields up to \(Rs\) units at \(t=1\), with \(R>0\). Household 1 owns a share \(\theta\in[0,1]\) of the firm and household 2 owns the rest.

  1. Write \(L\), \(X^i\), \(e^i\), \(\theta^i\) and \(Y\), using the sign conventions. (10 pts)
  2. Write material balance, one equation per commodity. (5 pts)
  3. Characterize the set of feasible consumption allocations \((x^1,x^2)\) in terms of \(s\). Show that the aggregate consumption possibilities form a line segment, and give its slope. (10 pts)
  4. Where would \(\theta\) enter if you were asked for a competitive equilibrium? One line. (5 pts)
Show solution

(a) \(L=\mathbb{R}^2\); \(X^i=\mathbb{R}^2_+\); \(e^1=(1,0)\), \(e^2=(0,1)\); \(\theta^1=\theta\), \(\theta^2=1-\theta\); \(Y=\{(y_0,y_1): y_0\le0,\ y_1\le -Ry_0\}\). The date-0 good is an input (negative) and the date-1 good an output.
(b) Date 0: \(x^1_0+x^2_0=y_0+1\). Date 1: \(x^1_1+x^2_1=y_1+1\).
(c) With \(y=(-s,y_1)\), \(y_1\le Rs\), and \(s\in[0,1]\) (non-negative consumption at date 0): aggregate consumption is \((C_0,C_1)\) with \(C_0=1-s\) and \(C_1\le 1+Rs\). The efficient frontier \(C_1 = 1+R(1-C_0)\), \(C_0\in[0,1]\), is a segment of slope \(-R\): giving up one unit today buys \(R\) tomorrow. Any split of \((C_0,C_1)\) between the two households with non-negative entries is feasible.
(d) Only in the budget constraints: household 1's income is \(p\cdot e^1+\theta\pi\) and household 2's is \(p\cdot e^2+(1-\theta)\pi\). (With constant returns, \(\pi=0\) in equilibrium, so \(\theta\) would not even matter there.)

Exercise 1.3 Slides p.3 (time endowment vs. labour sales)

A household has 1 unit of time, values consumption \(c\) and leisure \(\ell\), and works \(n=1-\ell\) hours. Describe its \(X^i\) and \(e^i\) in two ways: (i) with commodities (consumption, leisure) and a time endowment; (ii) with commodities (consumption, labour) and no time endowment. Show that the household's net trade with the market is identical in both.

Show solution

(i) \(X=\{(c,\ell): c\ge0,\ 0\le\ell\le1\}\) and \(e=(0,1)\). Net trade: \(x-e=(c,\ell-1)=(c,-n)\).
(ii) \(X=\{(c,x_n): c\ge0,\ -1\le x_n\le 0\}\) with \(x_n=-n\), and \(e=(0,0)\). Net trade \(x-e=(c,-n)\).
Same net trade, so the same budget constraints (\(p\cdot(x-e)\le\) profits) and the same equilibria. Physical limits on hours appear in \(X^i\) either way (\(\ell\le 1\), or \(x_n\ge-1\)).

Exercise 1.4 Proof

Let every \(X^i\) and \(Y^j\) be convex. Show that the set of feasible allocations is convex. (We will use convexity heavily in the Second Welfare Theorem, Lesson 6.)

Show solution

Take feasible \(\{x^i,y^j\}\) and \(\{x'^i,y'^j\}\), and \(\lambda\in[0,1]\). Let \(x^i_\lambda=\lambda x^i+(1-\lambda)x'^i\) and similarly \(y^j_\lambda\). By convexity, \(x^i_\lambda\in X^i\) and \(y^j_\lambda\in Y^j\). Material balance is linear: \(\sum_i x^i_\lambda = \lambda\sum_i x^i+(1-\lambda)\sum_i x'^i = \lambda(\sum_j y^j+\sum_i e^i)+(1-\lambda)(\sum_j y'^j+\sum_i e^i) = \sum_j y^j_\lambda+\sum_i e^i\). ∎

Exercise 1.5 Exam-inspired (an OLG economy with a tree, written as a GE economy)

This is the setup of the 2017/18 Midterm 1 ("OLG and trees"), here only written down; Lessons 8–12 solve economies like it. Time is \(t=1,2,\dots\). A measure one of agents is born each period and lives two periods. Generation \(t\ge1\) is endowed with \(e^t_t>0\) when young and nothing when old, and has strictly increasing utility \(u^t(c^t_t,c^t_{t+1})\). The initial old (generation 0) care only about \(c^0_1\). A tree produces \(d_t\ge0\) units of the good at each date; at \(t=1\) the initial old own the tree.

  1. What is the commodity space? (5 pts)
  2. Model the tree as a firm: write its production set \(Y\subset L\) and say who holds the ownership share. (10 pts)
  3. Write material balance for date \(t\). Your answer is one equation. (5 pts)
  4. In the Arrow–Debreu formulation (all trades at date 0 prices \(p_t>0\)), what is the tree's profit, and what is the initial old's budget constraint? (10 pts)
  5. Suppose \(d_t=d>0\) for all \(t\). Show that in any competitive equilibrium \(\sum_{t\ge1}p_t\lt \infty\). (15 pts)
Hint for (e)

What would the initial old demand at date 1 if the value of their firm were infinite? Compare with what is physically available at date 1.

Show solution

(a) \(L=\mathbb{R}^\infty\): one good per date.
(b) \(Y=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^\infty: 0\le y_t\le d_t \ \forall t\}\) (fruit can be discarded but not created). The initial old hold \(\theta^0=1\); everyone else holds 0. (In the sequential-markets version, the tree is sold each period; in Arrow–Debreu, ownership at date 0 is all that matters.)
(c) \(c^{t-1}_t+c^t_t = d_t+e^t_t\).
(d) With \(p_t>0\), profit maximization sets \(y_t=d_t\), so \(\pi=\sum_{t\ge1}p_td_t\). Initial old: \(p_1c^0_1\le \pi\).
(e) If \(\sum_tp_t=\infty\) then \(\pi=d\sum_tp_t=\infty\). Then the initial old's budget set contains every \(c^0_1\ge0\); with strictly increasing utility their problem has no solution (demand is unbounded), which contradicts the existence of an optimal \(\bar x^0\) with \(\bar c^0_1\le d_1+e^1_1\). Hence \(\sum_tp_t\lt \infty\). Interpretation: a positive-dividend asset forces the value of the aggregate endowment to be finite, i.e. interest rates that are positive on average. Lesson 12 shows that this is exactly the condition under which the First Welfare Theorem survives in OLG.

Key takeaways

  • An economy is \((L,\{X^i,u^i,e^i,\theta^i\},\{Y^j\})\). Commodities are distinguished by characteristics, location, date and state.
  • Signs: for a household, + is a purchase and − a sale; for a firm, + is an output and − an input. Profit is just \(p\cdot y\).
  • \(\sum_i\theta^i_j=1\): firms are fully owned by households.
  • Feasible means \(x^i\in X^i\), \(y^j\in Y^j\) and \(\sum x^i=\sum y^j+\sum e^i\), one equation per commodity, with no prices involved.
