Pareto Optimality and Local Non-Satiation

1. Pareto dominance and Pareto optimality

Definition: Pareto optimal allocation S1 p.14

A feasible allocation \(\{x^i,y^j\}\) Pareto dominates a feasible allocation \(\{\bar x^i,\bar y^j\}\) if $$u^i(x^i)\ \ge\ u^i(\bar x^i)\ \text{ for all } i\in I,\qquad u^{i'}(x^{i'})\ >\ u^{i'}(\bar x^{i'})\ \text{ for some } i'\in I .$$ A feasible allocation \(\{\bar x^i,\bar y^j\}\) is Pareto optimal (PO) if no feasible allocation Pareto dominates it.

Three remarks that are easy to forget under exam pressure:

  • PO is price-free. It uses only feasibility (Lesson 1) and utilities. A planner could check it with no markets at all.
  • PO is about efficiency, not equity. Giving everything to one person is typically PO (Exercise 3.4). There is usually a whole continuum of PO allocations.
  • The comparison set matters. PO is relative to the set of feasible allocations. Change technology, or restrict attention (e.g. to "symmetric" allocations in OLG, Lesson 10), and you change what counts as optimal.
Related: weak Pareto optimality

\(\bar x\) is weakly PO if there is no feasible \(x\) with \(u^i(x^i)>u^i(\bar x^i)\) for every \(i\). PO ⇒ weakly PO. The converse can fail when some agent is satiated. You will see in Lesson 4 that every CE is weakly PO with no assumptions at all; LNS is what upgrades this to full PO.

2. See it: the lens and the contract curve

Use the Edgeworth box from Lesson 2: total endowment \((1,1)\), \(u^A=a\log x_1+(1-a)\log x_2\), \(u^B=b\log x_1+(1-b)\log x_2\). Pick any allocation, A's bundle \(\bar x=(\bar x_1,\bar x_2)\) and B's \(\mathbf 1-\bar x\), and draw both indifference curves through it.

  • The region between them, where both agents are strictly better off, is the lens. A non-empty lens means \(\bar x\) is not PO.
  • At an interior allocation, the lens is empty exactly when the indifference curves are tangent, i.e. the marginal rates of substitution are equal: $$\underbrace{\frac{a}{1-a}\frac{x_2}{x_1}}_{MRS^A} = \underbrace{\frac{b}{1-b}\frac{1-x_2}{1-x_1}}_{MRS^B} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x_2=\frac{k_b x_1}{k_a(1-x_1)+k_b x_1},\quad k_a=\tfrac{a}{1-a},\ k_b=\tfrac{b}{1-b}.$$ This locus is the contract curve: the set of interior PO allocations.
Interactive. Drag the allocation \(\bar x\). The blue curve is A's indifference curve through \(\bar x\) and the orange curve is B's. The shaded lens contains the allocations that make both strictly better off. The dashed violet curve is the contract curve, and its thick aqua stretch is the part inside the lens: the PO allocations that Pareto dominate \(\bar x\). Try: drag \(\bar x\) onto the contract curve and watch the lens collapse.
Tangency is not the definition

"MRSs equal" characterizes PO only for interior allocations with smooth, convex preferences. The definition is "no feasible Pareto improvement". At corners, with kinks (Leontief), or with non-convexities you must go back to the definition. Exercise 3.3 is a typical exam case.

3. Local non-satiation

Definition: Local non-satiation (LNS) S1 p.15

\((u^i,X^i)\) satisfies local non-satiation if for every \(x\in X^i\) and every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is an \(\hat x\in B_\varepsilon(x)\cap X^i\) with \(u^i(\hat x)>u^i(x)\), where $$B_\varepsilon(x)=\{x'\in X^i:\ \|x-x'\|<\varepsilon\},\qquad \|x\|^2=\sum_{k=1}^m x_k^2 .$$

In words: however close you insist on staying, there is always something slightly better nearby. Note that LNS is a joint property of \(u^i\) and \(X^i\): the better bundle must be consumable. Two pictures show how it fails:

Left: a bliss point. \(u=-(x_1-0.5)^2-(x_2-0.55)^2\) has circular indifference curves. At the bliss point \(x^b\) nothing in any ball is better, so LNS fails at that one point, and that is enough to violate the definition. Right: a thick indifference curve. \(u\) equals \(x_1+x_2\) below the band, stays constant at \(0.8\) inside the shaded band \(0.8\le x_1+x_2\le 1.2\), and equals \(x_1+x_2-0.4\) above it. This \(u\) is continuous and (weakly) increasing, yet in the interior of the band a small enough ball contains nothing strictly better. This is the counterexample behind slide question 2.

Intuition: why "local", and why not monotonicity?

The proof only ever perturbs a bundle by a tiny amount to make a budget argument. So we need "better is available nearby", not "more is better". LNS allows goods that are bads (\(u=x_2-x_1^2\) satisfies it: just raise \(x_2\)), and it says nothing about convexity or continuity. It is about the weakest assumption that makes "money left on the table is wasted" true.

4. The slide questions on LNS S1 p.19

Fix the vocabulary first; the answers depend on it. For bundles in \(\mathbb{R}^m\), write \(x'\ge x\) for "every coordinate weakly larger" and \(x'\gg x\) for "every coordinate strictly larger".

NameCondition
increasing (weakly)\(x'\ge x\ \Rightarrow\ u(x')\ge u(x)\)
monotone (MWG)\(x'\gg x\ \Rightarrow\ u(x')>u(x)\)
strictly increasing\(x'\ge x,\ x'\neq x\ \Rightarrow\ u(x')>u(x)\)

Throughout, take \(X^i=\mathbb{R}^m_+\) (or any set such that \(x\in X^i\) implies \(x+t\,\mathbf e_k\in X^i\) for small \(t>0\)). Try the three questions as Exercise 3.1 before opening the solutions.

5. Check your understanding

1. Allocation \(\bar x\) is PO. What can you conclude about any other feasible allocation \(x\)?
  • Everybody weakly prefers \(\bar x\) to \(x\)
  • Somebody strictly prefers \(x\) to \(\bar x\)
  • If anybody gains, somebody else loses
  • Total utility is higher under \(\bar x\)
No feasible \(x\) has everyone weakly better and someone strictly better, so if someone strictly gains, someone strictly loses. PO does not rank \(\bar x\) above every alternative, and there is no "total utility" in the definition.
2. On \(X=\mathbb{R}^2_+\), which utility function violates LNS?
  • \(u=\min\{x_1+x_2,\,1\}\)
  • \(u=\min\{x_1,\,x_2\}\)
  • \(u=x_2-x_1^2\)
  • \(u=x_1^2+x_2^2\)
\(\min\{x_1+x_2,1\}\) is satiated once \(x_1+x_2\ge 1\): at \((1,1)\) nothing nearby is better. Leontief satisfies LNS (raise both goods a bit), \(x_2-x_1^2\) does (raise \(x_2\)), and \(x_1^2+x_2^2\) is strictly increasing on \(\mathbb{R}^2_+\).
3. In the proof that LNS makes budgets bind, which property of \(u\) is used?
  • Continuity of \(u\) at the optimal bundle
  • Quasi-concavity of \(u\) on the whole set
  • Strict monotonicity in at least one good
  • Only a strictly better bundle close by
The proof perturbs \(\bar x\) to a nearby better bundle and uses Cauchy–Schwarz to keep it affordable. That is LNS and nothing more. No continuity, convexity or monotonicity is needed.
4. Goods are indivisible, \(X^i=\mathbb{Z}^m_+\), and \(u\) is strictly increasing. Does LNS hold?
  • Yes, since adding one unit always helps
  • No, since tiny balls hold only the point
  • Yes, since strict monotonicity implies it
  • No, since utility cannot be continuous
For \(\varepsilon<1\), \(B_\varepsilon(x)\cap\mathbb{Z}^m_+=\{x\}\). The better bundle must be in \(X^i\) and within ε, so LNS fails even though more is better. LNS is a property of \((u,X)\) jointly.

6. Exercises

Exercise 3.1 Slides p.19 (the three LNS questions)
  1. If \(u\) is continuous and strictly increasing, does it satisfy local non-satiation?
  2. If \(u\) is continuous and increasing, does it satisfy local non-satiation?
  3. If \(u\) is strictly increasing, but not necessarily continuous, does it satisfy local non-satiation?
Hint

For (a), construct \(\hat x\) explicitly and check its distance from \(x\). Then ask which hypotheses your construction actually used. For (b), think about the most boring increasing function there is.

Show solution

(a) Yes. Fix \(x\in X\) and \(\varepsilon>0\). Let \(\hat x = x+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\mathbf e_1\). Then \(\hat x\in X\) (we may increase a coordinate), \(\|\hat x-x\|=\varepsilon/2<\varepsilon\), and \(\hat x\ge x\) with \(\hat x\ne x\), so strict monotonicity gives \(u(\hat x)>u(x)\). ∎

(b) Not in general. A constant function \(u\equiv 0\) is continuous and (weakly) increasing but nothing is ever strictly better. A less trivial example is the thick-band utility in the figure: continuous, increasing, and violating LNS inside the band. Caveat: if "increasing" is read as MWG's "monotone" (\(x'\gg x\Rightarrow u(x')>u(x)\)), then the answer is yes: take \(\hat x=x+\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sqrt m}\mathbf 1\), which has \(\|\hat x-x\|=\varepsilon/2\) and \(\hat x\gg x\). On an exam, state which definition you use.

(c) Yes. The proof of (a) never used continuity. Continuity is irrelevant for LNS; it matters for existence of optima and for the Second Welfare Theorem (Arrow's remark, Lesson 7). The slide pairs (a) with (c) to make exactly this point.

Exercise 3.2 Exam-style

In the Edgeworth box with \(a=1/3\), \(b=1/2\) (Exercise 2.2), find the contract curve \(x_2=\phi(x_1)\). Then verify that the CE allocation \(x^A=(1/3,1/2)\) lies on it.

  1. Compute \(\phi(1/3)\).
  2. Explain, without the welfare theorem, why landing on the contract curve was guaranteed.
Show solution

\(k_a=\frac{1/3}{2/3}=\tfrac12\), \(k_b=1\), so \(\phi(x_1)=\frac{x_1}{\frac12(1-x_1)+x_1}=\frac{2x_1}{1+x_1}\). Then \(\phi(1/3)=\frac{2/3}{4/3}=\tfrac12\) ✓.

(b) In a CE, each consumer's interior optimum sets \(MRS^i=p_1/p_2\). Both face the same price ratio, so \(MRS^A=MRS^B\): tangency, hence the contract curve. This is the calculus version of the First Welfare Theorem.

Exercise 3.3 Exam-style (PO without calculus)

Total endowment \((1,1)\). \(u^A=x_1+x_2\) (perfect substitutes) and \(u^B=\min\{x_1,x_2\}\) (Leontief). Characterize the set of all PO allocations.

Hint

If B's bundle is off its diagonal, B has some "useless" units. What happens if you give those to A? Then write A's utility in terms of B's bundle: \(u^A=2-(x_1^B+x_2^B)\).

Show solution

Claim: PO ⇔ B's bundle is on the diagonal, \(x^B=(t,t)\) for some \(t\in[0,1]\), and A gets \((1-t,1-t)\).

(⇒) If \(x^B_1\ne x^B_2\), say \(x^B_1>x^B_2\), move \(x^B_1-x^B_2\) units of good 1 from B to A. B's min is unchanged; A gains strictly. So the original allocation was not PO.

(⇐) At \(x^B=(t,t)\): \(u^B=t\), \(u^A=2-2t\). Any feasible allocation has \(u^B=\min\{x^B_1,x^B_2\}\le\frac{x^B_1+x^B_2}{2}=\frac{2-u^A}{2}\), i.e. \(u^A+2u^B\le 2\), with equality on the diagonal. Raising one agent's utility therefore strictly lowers the other's bound, so no Pareto improvement exists. Note that MRS^B is undefined at the kink, so tangency would not have helped.

Exercise 3.5 Proof (satiation breaks the link)

Two goods, two agents, each endowed with \((1,1)\). \(u^A=\min\{x_1+x_2,\,1\}\) and \(u^B=x_1+x_2\). Show that \(p=(1,1)\) with no trade is a CE, and that this CE is not PO. Which part of the Lemma fails for A?

Show solution

At \(p=(1,1)\), A's budget is \(x_1+x_2\le 2\). Every bundle with \(x_1+x_2\ge1\) gives the maximum utility 1, so \(x^A=(1,1)\) is optimal. B maximizes \(x_1+x_2\) subject to \(x_1+x_2\le2\), so \(x^B=(1,1)\) is optimal. Markets clear: this is a CE.
Not PO: give A \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\) and B \((\tfrac32,\tfrac32)\). A still gets 1; B gets 3 instead of 2.
For A, \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\) is weakly preferred to \((1,1)\) yet strictly cheaper. That is a failure of Part 2 of the Lemma, which is equation (1) in the 1WT proof. Notice that A's chosen bundle does exhaust its budget; what fails is Part 2, not Part 1. A could have spent less and been just as happy, and that wasted value is what B could have used.

Key takeaways

  • PO means no feasible allocation makes everyone weakly better and someone strictly better. It is price-free and says nothing about equity.
  • In the smooth interior case, PO ⇔ equal MRSs (the contract curve). Otherwise, use the definition.
  • LNS: in every ε-ball around every \(x\in X^i\) there is a strictly better consumable bundle. Strictly increasing ⇒ LNS (continuity irrelevant); weakly increasing does not imply LNS.
  • Lemma: LNS ⇒ budgets bind, and weakly-better bundles cost at least as much. This is the whole of what the 1WT needs from preferences.
