Local Stability and Almost-Reciprocal Pairs

By the end you can:

  2. derive the quadratic \(Q(\lambda)=0\) by differentiating the Euler equation at the steady state;

Primary source Alvarez, Local Stability of Optimal Trajectories, Linearization, slides 1–15. The rigorous treatment — using the implicit function theorem to pass from the linearization to an exact local solution — is Stokey, Lucas & Prescott, Recursive Methods in Economic Dynamics (1989), Ch. 6, especially the analysis around steady states for interior solutions. 1. What local stability means

Lesson 28 treated the Euler equation as a second-order difference equation in \(x\) and used a shooting algorithm to find the unique solution that hits the steady state. That approach requires solving a fixed-point problem numerically. Here we ask a more targeted question: given that we are near the steady state \(x^*\), how fast does the optimal path converge? Stab p.2

The optimal decision rule \(g:\mathbb R^m\to\mathbb R^m\) maps today's state to tomorrow's optimal state: \(x_{t+1}=g(x_t)\). The steady state satisfies \(x^*=g(x^*)\). For \(x_t\) near \(x^*\), write \(\xi_t\equiv x_t-x^*\) for the deviation. First-order Taylor expansion around \(x^*\):

$$x_{t+1}-x^*=g(x_t)-g(x^*)\approx g'(x^*)(x_t-x^*)$$

so \(\xi_{t+1}\approx g'(x^*)\xi_t\). In the scalar case \(m=1\), iterating gives \(\xi_t\approx [g'(x^*)]^t\xi_0\). This converges to zero if and only if \(|g'(x^*)|\lt1\).

Local stability

The steady state \(x^*\) is locally stable (locally asymptotically stable) if there is a neighbourhood \(U\) of \(x^*\) such that every trajectory starting in \(U\) converges to \(x^*\). For the linearized system \(\xi_{t+1}=g'(x^*)\xi_t\), this holds if and only if \(|g'(x^*)|\lt1\). When \(|g'(x^*)|>1\), paths starting near \(x^*\) diverge away from it; the steady state is locally unstable.

Why \(g'(x^*)\) governs speed

The derivative \(g'(x^*)\) is a local slope: it says "starting one unit away from steady state, next period you are \(g'(x^*)\) units away." If \(|g'|\lt1\), the gap shrinks each period by a factor \(|g'|\), giving geometric convergence. If \(g'\lt0\), convergence is oscillatory: overshooting then undershooting. If \(g'=0\) (a fixed point with flat policy), you jump straight to steady state in one period. If \(|g'|>1\), the gap grows — which only matters if you happen to start exactly on the optimal path, since the EE+TC system picks the right one.

2. Differentiating the Euler equation

The Euler equation holds for every \(x_t\) near \(x^*\) along the optimal path Stab p.4:

$$F_y(x_t,g(x_t))+\beta F_x(g(x_t),g(g(x_t)))=0\qquad\text{for all }x_t.$$

Differentiate both sides with respect to \(x_t\) and evaluate at \(x^*\) (where \(g(x^*)=x^*\)):

$$F_{yx}+F_{yy}\,g'(x^*)+\beta\big[F_{xx}+F_{xy}\,g'(x^*)\big]g'(x^*)+\beta F_{xx}=0.$$

Wait — let's be careful with the chain rule on the second term. Let \(h=g(x_t)\) and \(k=g(h)\). The second term is \(\beta F_x(h,k)\), which depends on \(x_t\) through both \(h=g(x_t)\) and \(k=g(g(x_t))\):

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x_t}\beta F_x(g(x_t),g(g(x_t)))=\beta\big[F_{xx}\cdot g'(x_t)+F_{xy}\cdot g'(g(x_t))\cdot g'(x_t)\big].$$

At \(x_t=x^*\), where \(g(x^*)=x^*\) and \(g'(x^*)=\lambda\) (what we're solving for):

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x_t}\big[\beta F_x\big]\bigg|_{x^*}=\beta(F_{xx}+F_{xy}\lambda)\lambda=\beta F_{xx}\lambda+\beta F_{xy}\lambda^2.$$

Combining with the first term \(F_{yx}+F_{yy}\lambda\), the full derivative at \(x^*\) is zero:

$$0=F_{yx}+F_{yy}\lambda+\beta F_{xx}\lambda+\beta F_{xy}\lambda^2=F_{yx}+[F_{yy}+\beta F_{xx}]\lambda+\beta F_{xy}\lambda^2.$$
The quadratic \(Q(\lambda)\) Stab p.4

All second derivatives are evaluated at the steady state \((x^*,x^*)\). Define

$$Q(\lambda)\equiv\beta F_{xy}\,\lambda^2+[F_{yy}+\beta F_{xx}]\,\lambda+F_{yx}.$$

\(g'(x^*)\) is a root of \(Q(\lambda)=0\). There are (generically) two roots; the optimal policy picks one.

Interpreting \(Q\)

Think of \(Q(\lambda)\) as the "linearized Euler equation" written as a polynomial in the slope. The steady-state Euler equation is \(F_y+\beta F_x=0\) (which is what \(Q(1)=0\) would say if the cross-partials were zero and curvatures matched). Instead, \(Q(\lambda)\) captures the full curvature of the return: \(F_{xy}\lambda^2\) is the feedback of next period's choice on current marginal costs, \(F_{yy}\lambda\) is own curvature of return in next-period state, and \(F_{xx}\lambda\) is own curvature in current state.

3. Almost-reciprocal pairs

Theorem: almost-reciprocal pairs Stab p.5

Suppose \(F_{xy}(x^*,x^*)\ne0\). If \(\lambda_1\ne0\) is a root of \(Q(\lambda)=0\), then \(\lambda_2\equiv1/(\beta\lambda_1)\) is also a root.

Proof
  1. Write Vieta's formulas. Since \(Q\) is a monic quadratic (after dividing by \(\beta F_{xy}\)), its two roots \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\) satisfy: $$\lambda_1+\lambda_2=-\frac{F_{yy}+\beta F_{xx}}{\beta F_{xy}},\qquad \lambda_1\lambda_2=\frac{F_{yx}}{\beta F_{xy}}.$$
  2. Use symmetry of mixed partials. For \(F:X\times X\to\mathbb R\) smooth, \(F_{xy}(x^*,x^*)=F_{yx}(x^*,x^*)\) (Clairaut's theorem, since the arguments are from the same space \(X\)). Therefore $$\lambda_1\lambda_2=\frac{F_{xy}}{\beta F_{xy}}=\frac{1}{\beta}.$$
  3. Conclude. \(\lambda_1\lambda_2=1/\beta\) says exactly that \(\lambda_2=1/(\beta\lambda_1)\). The roots come in "almost reciprocal" pairs: each is the reciprocal of \(\beta\) times the other. \(\square\)
Economic interpretation of the pairing

The Euler equation is second-order because it links three periods: \(x_t\), \(x_{t+1}\), \(x_{t+2}\). Its linearization is a quadratic in the slope. The pairing \(\lambda_1\lambda_2=1/\beta\) reflects time-reversal symmetry: running the problem backwards (reversing time) replaces \(\beta\) with \(1/\beta\), swapping \(\lambda_1\) for \(\lambda_2\). This is the discrete-time analogue of the Hamiltonian structure that appears in Lesson 38.

Case I vs. Case II Stab p.6
Case I (locally stable)
One root satisfies \(|\lambda_1|\lt1\). Then automatically \(|\lambda_2|=1/(\beta|\lambda_1|)>1/\beta>1\): the other root is outside the unit circle. The optimal policy has \(g'(x^*)=\lambda_1\), giving geometric convergence at rate \(\lambda_1\).
Case II (locally unstable)
Both roots satisfy \(|\lambda_i|>1\). The steady state is unstable: any path starting nearby diverges unless it starts exactly on the unstable manifold (a set of measure zero). The EE plus the TC does pick the right path, but it is not one that converges to \(x^*\).
What Case II means economically

An unstable steady state is not a problem for the existence of an optimum — the path still exists and the EE+TC still pins it down (Lessons 27–28). But it means the long-run behaviour is not convergence to \(x^*\). The path may converge to a cycle, or escape to a corner, or grow without bound. For models like the AK model (Lesson 31), "steady state" is a constant growth rate, not a fixed point, and stability analysis works differently.

4. The neoclassical growth model

Apply these results to \(F(x,y)=U(f(x)-y)\), the return to consuming \(f(x)-y\) when today's capital is \(x\) and tomorrow's is \(y\). Stab p.12

Computing the second derivatives at the steady state

At the interior steady state \((k^*,k^*)\) with \(c^*=f(k^*)-k^*\), the Euler equation \(1=\beta f'(k^*)\) gives \(f'=1/\beta\). Write \(U'=U'(c^*)\), \(U''=U''(c^*)\), \(f'=1/\beta\), \(f''=f''(k^*)\). The first-order derivatives are:

$$F_y=-U',\qquad F_x=U'f'.$$

The second-order derivatives (using \(\partial^2/\partial y^2\), etc.):

$$F_{yy}=U'',\quad F_{xx}=U''[f']^2+U'f''=\frac{U''}{\beta^2}+U'f'',\quad F_{xy}=F_{yx}=-U''f'=-\frac{U''}{\beta}.$$

The quadratic \(\tilde Q(\lambda)\)

Plug into \(Q(\lambda)=\beta F_{xy}\lambda^2+[F_{yy}+\beta F_{xx}]\lambda+F_{yx}\) and divide by \(-U''>0\) (note \(U''\lt0\)):

$$\tilde Q(\lambda)\equiv\lambda^2-\left[1+\frac{1}{\beta}+\frac{f''/f'}{U''/U'}\right]\lambda+\frac{1}{\beta}=0.$$

Since both \(f''/f'\lt0\) and \(U''/U'\lt0\) for concave \(f\) and \(U\), the ratio \((f''/f')/(U''/U')\gt0\). Write \(\rho_f\equiv-f''/f'>0\) for the elasticity of marginal product and \(\rho_U\equiv-U''/U'>0\) for the coefficient of relative risk aversion (divided by \(c\)). Then: Stab p.13

$$\tilde Q(\lambda)=\lambda^2-\left[1+\frac{1}{\beta}+\frac{\rho_f}{\rho_U}\right]\lambda+\frac{1}{\beta}.$$

Signs confirming Case I

  • \(\tilde Q(0)=1/\beta>0\) — positive at zero.
  • \(\tilde Q(1)=1-[1+1/\beta+\rho_f/\rho_U]+1/\beta=-\rho_f/\rho_U\lt0\) — negative at one.
  • \(\tilde Q\) is an upward-opening parabola (leading coefficient \(+1>0\)).

Therefore \(\tilde Q\) has exactly one root in \((0,1)\) and one root above \(1/\beta>1\). The optimal policy slope is the root \(\lambda_1\in(0,1)\), confirming that the neoclassical growth model's steady state is always locally stable under these standard assumptions.

Interactive. The quadratic \(\tilde Q(\lambda)=\lambda^2-[1+1/\beta+\rho_f/\rho_U]\lambda+1/\beta\). The blue curve is \(\tilde Q\); the two roots are marked. Try: (1) Raise \(\rho_f/\rho_U\): the middle dip falls further below zero, the stable root \(\lambda_1\) rises toward 1 (slower convergence). (2) Raise \(\beta\) toward 1: \(1/\beta\to1\), the roots pinch together and the stable root approaches 1 from below.

Comparative statics of the speed of convergence

How does \(\lambda_1=g'(k^*)\in(0,1)\) change with the curvatures of \(U\) and \(f\)? Stab p.14

  • Higher \(\rho_U\) (more concave utility, higher risk aversion). The dip of \(\tilde Q\) at \(\lambda=1\) is \(-\rho_f/\rho_U\), which shrinks in absolute value. The root \(\lambda_1\) rises toward 1: convergence is slower. Intuition: more concave \(U\) means households want smoother consumption and return to steady state more gradually.
  • Higher \(\rho_f\) (more concave technology, faster MPK decline). The dip at 1 deepens in absolute value, so \(\lambda_1\) falls toward 0: convergence is faster. Intuition: steeper MPK decline means the return to steady-state capital is stronger, pulling the economy back more sharply.

3. In the neoclassical growth model, \(\tilde Q(1)=-\rho_f/\rho_U\lt0\) because:
  • \(\rho_f\) and \(\rho_U\) are both positive and divide to a positive ratio

  • \(\rho_U\) is zero because utility is linear in this model
  • \(\tilde Q(1)\) cannot be negative unless \(\beta\gt1\)
\(\rho_f=-f''/f'\gt0\) because \(f''\lt0\) and \(f'\gt0\) (concave technology). \(\rho_U=-U''/U'\gt0\) because \(U''\lt0\) and \(U'\gt0\) (concave utility). Their ratio is positive, so \(-\rho_f/\rho_U\lt0\). This sign is what guarantees one root lies in \((0,1)\).
4. If Case II holds (both roots \(|\lambda_i|\gt1\)), the steady state is:
  • globally stable, with oscillatory convergence
  • globally stable, but only from one direction

Both roots outside the unit circle means the linearized system \(\xi_{t+1}=g'(x^*)\xi_t\) has \(|g'|>1\): deviations grow. The steady state is locally unstable. The optimum still exists (EE+TC still pin it down), but convergence to \(x^*\) fails for all starting points \(x_0\ne x^*\).

6. True or false?

  1. If \(|g'(x^*)|\lt1\), the steady state is globally stable.
    Answer
    False. Local stability means convergence from a neighbourhood of \(x^*\), not from every starting point. For global stability you need a global Lyapunov function argument or monotone dynamics. For example, if \(F\) is quadratic the linear decision rule is globally optimal (Exercise 33.2), but for general \(F\) the Taylor approximation is only valid near \(x^*\).
  2. In the neoclassical growth model, \(g'(k^*)\) always lies in the interval \((0,1)\).
    Answer
    True under the standard assumptions (strictly concave \(U\) and \(f\), interior steady state). The quadratic argument above shows \(\tilde Q(0)=1/\beta>0\) and \(\tilde Q(1)=-\rho_f/\rho_U\lt0\), so the smaller root is in \((0,1)\). Note: if \(U\) were linear (\(\rho_U=0\)), the analysis breaks down (see the note on linear utility in Lesson 29).
  3. If \(\lambda\) is a root of \(Q(\lambda)=0\), then \(\beta\lambda\) is also a root.
    Answer
    False. The partner of \(\lambda_1\) is \(\lambda_2=1/(\beta\lambda_1)\), not \(\beta\lambda_1\). The product of roots is \(1/\beta\), so \(\lambda_2=1/(\beta\lambda_1)\). The two are "almost reciprocal" in the sense that \(\lambda_1\lambda_2\beta=1\). Confusing \(\beta\lambda\) with \(1/(\beta\lambda)\) is a common exam mistake.
  4. A higher discount factor \(\beta\) (more patient households) always leads to faster convergence in the neoclassical growth model.
    Answer
    False. From \(\tilde Q(\lambda)=\lambda^2-[1+1/\beta+\rho_f/\rho_U]\lambda+1/\beta=0\), increasing \(\beta\) reduces \(1/\beta\), which lowers both the constant term and the coefficient in brackets. The net effect on \(\lambda_1\) is ambiguous in general. Moreover, a higher \(\beta\) shifts the steady state \(k^*\) (since \(\beta f'(k^*)=1\)), so comparing speeds at different \(\beta\) values also involves different steady states.

7. Exercises

Exercise 33.1 Stab p.9 (zero root)

Suppose \(F_{xy}(x^*,x^*)=0\).

  1. Show that \(\lambda=0\) is a root of \(Q(\lambda)=0\). What does this imply about \(g'(x^*)\)? (5 pts)
  2. What is the economic interpretation of \(g'(x^*)=0\)? Under what structural assumption does \(F_{xy}=0\) hold? (5 pts)
  3. What happens to the almost-reciprocal-pairs result when \(F_{xy}=0\)? Is the partner of \(\lambda=0\) well-defined? (5 pts)
Hint

For (a), plug \(\lambda=0\) into \(Q\) and use \(F_{xy}=0\). For (b), think about what \(g'(x^*)=0\) means for the Taylor expansion \(x_{t+1}-x^*\approx g'(x^*)(x_t-x^*)\). For (c), \(1/(\beta\cdot0)\) is undefined, but look at the quadratic \(Q\) when \(F_{xy}=0\): what kind of polynomial is it?

Show solution

(a) \(Q(0)=\beta F_{xy}\cdot0+[F_{yy}+\beta F_{xx}]\cdot0+F_{yx}=F_{yx}=F_{xy}=0\). So yes, \(\lambda=0\) is a root, meaning \(g'(x^*)=0\). The Taylor approximation gives \(x_{t+1}-x^*\approx0\cdot(x_t-x^*)=0\): starting from any \(x_0\) near \(x^*\), the economy reaches the steady state in one period.

(b) \(g'(x^*)=0\) means the optimal policy is flat at the steady state: today's capital has no first-order effect on next period's target. This holds when \(F_{xy}=0\), which occurs when the return \(F(x,y)\) is additively separable in \(x\) and \(y\) — the marginal cost of increasing \(y\) does not depend on the level of \(x\). The investment problem without adjustment costs (\(F(x,y)=f(x)+(1-\delta)x-y\), so \(F_{xy}=0\)) is the leading example from Lesson 32.

(c) When \(F_{xy}=0\), \(Q(\lambda)=[F_{yy}+\beta F_{xx}]\lambda\) is linear, with only one finite root \(\lambda=0\). The partner \(1/(\beta\cdot0)\) is \(+\infty\), which is consistent: the "unstable root" has gone to infinity, reflecting that there is no second-period feedback. The almost-reciprocal-pairs theorem assumes \(F_{xy}\ne0\) precisely to avoid this degenerate case.

Exercise 33.2 Stab p.11 (quadratic \(F\) gives an exact solution)

Suppose \(F(x,y)=ax^2+bxy+cy^2+dx+ey+f\) is quadratic. Show that the linear approximation \(x_{t+1}-x^*=g'(x^*)(x_t-x^*)\) is exact (holds for all \(x_t\), not just those near \(x^*\)), and that \(g'(x^*)=\lambda_1\) is the smaller root of \(Q(\lambda)=0\).

Hint

With quadratic \(F\), the second derivatives \(F_{xx}=2a\), \(F_{xy}=b\), \(F_{yy}=2c\) are constants, so \(Q(\lambda)\) is exactly the equation for the slope of the optimal linear policy \(g(x)=\lambda x+\mu\). Substitute \(g(x)=\lambda_1 x+\mu\) into the Euler equation and check it holds for all \(x\).

Show solution

Guess a linear policy \(g(x)=\lambda x+\mu\). Substituting into the EE \(F_y(x,g(x))+\beta F_x(g(x),g(g(x)))=0\):

\(F_y(x,\lambda x+\mu)=e+b x+2c(\lambda x+\mu)\) and \(F_x(\lambda x+\mu,\lambda^2x+\lambda\mu+\mu)=d+2a(\lambda x+\mu)+b(\lambda^2x+\lambda\mu+\mu)\).

For the EE to hold for all \(x\), the coefficient of \(x\) must vanish: \(b+2c\lambda+\beta[2a\lambda+b\lambda^2]=0\), i.e. \(\beta b\lambda^2+2(c+\beta a)\lambda+b=0\). This is exactly \(Q(\lambda)=0\) (with \(F_{xy}=b\), \(F_{yy}=2c\), \(F_{xx}=2a\), \(F_{yx}=b\)). The smaller root \(\lambda_1\) gives the saddle-path slope, and since all derivatives of \(F\) are constants, the Taylor expansion is exact. The intercept \(\mu\) is then determined by the constant terms (the level of the steady state).

Exercise 33.3 Exam-style (a separable example)

Consider the problem \(\max\sum_{t=0}^\infty\beta^t\ln(x_t-y_t)\) subject to \(y_t=x_{t+1}\in[0,x_t]\), so \(F(x,y)=\ln(x-y)\).

  1. Find the steady state \(x^*\) (where \(x^*=\beta x^*\) holds from the EE). Show there is no interior steady state. What does this tell you about the applicability of the local stability analysis? (5 pts)
  2. Now add a production function: \(F(x,y)=\ln(f(x)-y)\) with \(f(x)=Ax^\alpha\). Find \(x^*\) and compute \(Q(\lambda)\). What is \(g'(x^*)\)? (10 pts)
  3. As \(\alpha\to1\) (linear technology), what happens to the stable root? Relate to the AK model of Lesson 31. (5 pts)
Hint

For (a), the EE is \(-1/(x-y)+\beta(-1/(y-y'))(-1)=0\). At steady state \(y=y'=x\), so \(x-y=0\), which is not in the domain. For (b), use \(F(x,y)=\ln(f(x)-y)\) and compute \(F_{xy},F_{yy},F_{xx}\) as in §4. The steady state solves \(\beta f'(x^*)=1\).

Show solution

(a) At steady state \(x=y=x^*\), so \(x-y=0\) and \(\ln\) is undefined. There is no interior steady state. The model's solution is to consume all current resources (\(c_t=x_t-y_t>0\) with no accumulation); local stability analysis around a steady state does not apply directly.

(b) With \(F(x,y)=\ln(Ax^\alpha-y)\): let \(c=Ax^\alpha-y\). Then \(F_y=-1/c\), \(F_x=A\alpha x^{\alpha-1}/c\). The steady state: \(\beta f'(x^*)=1\Rightarrow\beta A\alpha (x^*)^{\alpha-1}=1\Rightarrow x^*=(\beta A\alpha)^{1/(1-\alpha)}\), and \(c^*=Ax^{*\alpha}-x^*=(1/\beta-1)x^*/(1-\text{something})\). Using the formulas from §4 with \(U(c)=\ln c\), so \(U'=1/c\), \(U''=-1/c^2\), \(\rho_U=-U''c/U'=1\) (log utility has unit EIS), and \(\rho_f=-f''x^*/f'=1-\alpha\): \(\tilde Q(\lambda)=\lambda^2-[1+1/\beta+(1-\alpha)/1]\lambda+1/\beta=0\). Smaller root: \(\lambda_1=g'(k^*)=\beta(1-\alpha)\) (can be verified by substitution; it is the well-known result for the Cobb-Douglas-log neoclassical model).

(c) As \(\alpha\to1\): \(\lambda_1=\beta(1-\alpha)\to0\). The stable root goes to zero, meaning any starting point converges to steady state in one period — consistent with the AK model (Lesson 31) where \(k'=\bar g k\) and the "steady state" is a balanced growth path, not a fixed point.

Exercise 33.4 Exam-style (comparative statics of stability)

In the neoclassical growth model \(F(x,y)=U(f(x)-y)\), consider two economies identical except that economy A has \(\rho_U^A=0.5\) and economy B has \(\rho_U^B=2.0\), while both have \(\rho_f=0.3\) and \(\beta=0.95\).

  1. Compute \(\tilde Q(\lambda)\) for each economy and find the stable root \(\lambda_1\) numerically. Which economy converges faster to steady state? (10 pts)
  2. Give a 2-sentence economic intuition for why the economy with higher \(\rho_U\) converges more slowly. (5 pts)
  3. Now hold \(\rho_U=1\) fixed and vary \(\rho_f\). At what value of \(\rho_f\) does \(\lambda_1=1/2\)? (5 pts)
Hint

For (a), \(\tilde Q(\lambda)=\lambda^2-[1+1/\beta+\rho_f/\rho_U]\lambda+1/\beta\). Use the quadratic formula. For (c), set \(\lambda_1\lambda_2=1/\beta\) and \(\lambda_1=1/2\) to get \(\lambda_2=2/\beta\). Then \(\lambda_1+\lambda_2=1+1/\beta+\rho_f/\rho_U\) gives \(\rho_f\).

Show solution

(a) Economy A: \(\tilde Q_A(\lambda)=\lambda^2-[1+1/0.95+0.3/0.5]\lambda+1/0.95=\lambda^2-2.653\lambda+1.053\). Roots: \(\lambda_1\approx0.586\). Economy B: \(\tilde Q_B(\lambda)=\lambda^2-[1+1/0.95+0.3/2]\lambda+1/0.95=\lambda^2-2.203\lambda+1.053\). Roots: \(\lambda_1\approx0.730\). Economy A converges faster (lower \(\lambda_1\)).

(b) With higher \(\rho_U\), households have a stronger desire to smooth consumption over time. When the economy is away from steady state, they spread the adjustment over many periods rather than making large jumps, so convergence is slower.

(c) If \(\lambda_1=1/2\), then \(\lambda_2=2/\beta=2/0.95\approx2.105\). Sum: \(1/2+2/0.95=1+1/\beta+\rho_f/1=1+1/0.95+\rho_f\). So \(\rho_f=1/2+2/0.95-1-1/0.95=1/2+1/0.95-1=1/2+(1/0.95-1)=0.5+0.0526\approx0.553\).

Key takeaways

  • The optimal decision rule \(g(x)\) with \(g(x^*)=x^*\) has slope \(g'(x^*)\) that governs local stability: stable iff \(|g'(x^*)|\lt1\).
  • Differentiating the Euler equation at the steady state produces the quadratic \(Q(\lambda)=\beta F_{xy}\lambda^2+[F_{yy}+\beta F_{xx}]\lambda+F_{yx}=0\), which \(g'(x^*)\) must satisfy.
  • The two roots always satisfy \(\lambda_1\lambda_2=1/\beta\) (product of roots = \(F_{yx}/(\beta F_{xy})=1/\beta\) by symmetry of mixed partials). This "almost-reciprocal" pairing means one root is inside the unit circle iff the other is outside.
  • Case I (one root \(|\lambda|\lt1\)): locally stable, convergence at rate \(\lambda_1\). Case II (both roots \(|\lambda|>1\)): unstable steady state.
  • Neoclassical growth model: \(\tilde Q(0)=1/\beta>0\) and \(\tilde Q(1)=-\rho_f/\rho_U\lt0\), so always Case I. Speed of convergence: lower with high \(\rho_U\) (smooth consumption), higher with high \(\rho_f\) (steep MPK decline).
  • When \(F_{xy}=0\) (additively separable \(F\)), the quadratic degenerates: \(\lambda=0\) is the only finite root, meaning convergence in one period (the frictionless investment benchmark of Lesson 32).
