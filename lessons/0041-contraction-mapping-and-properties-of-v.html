Contraction Mapping, Blackwell's Conditions, and Properties of V*

Covers Det DP slides 11–20 · About 35 min · Prerequisites: Lesson 40 (Bellman equation, Principle of Optimality)
By the end you can:
  1. define a contraction mapping on a metric space and state the Contraction Mapping Theorem (CMT);
  2. state Blackwell's sufficient conditions (monotonicity + discounting) and prove they imply \(T\) is a contraction;
  3. use the Corollary of the CMT to show \(V^*\) inherits properties of \(T\)-invariant subspaces;
  4. prove that \(V^*\) is monotone in \(x\) when \(F(\cdot,y)\) is increasing and \(\Gamma\) is increasing;
  5. prove that \(V^*\) is concave in \(x\) when \(X\) and \(\Gamma\) are convex and \(F\) is concave.
Primary source Alvarez, Deterministic DP slides 11–20. Standard reference: Stokey, Lucas & Prescott (SLP), Chapter 3 (Metric Spaces, the Contraction Mapping Theorem) and Chapter 4 (Bounded Dynamic Programming).

1. Bounded Dynamic Programming: the function space

To apply the Principle of Optimality rigorously, we need a space of candidate value functions and a notion of distance on that space. The natural choice is the space of continuous bounded functions Det DP p.11:

Definition: the space \(S\) of bounded continuous functions

Let \(X\) be the state space. Define:

$$S = \bigl\{f : X \to \mathbb{R} \;\big|\; f \text{ is continuous and } \|f\| \equiv \sup_{x \in X} |f(x)| \lt \infty \bigr\}.$$

Equip \(S\) with the sup norm \(\|f\| = \sup_{x \in X}|f(x)|\) and define the metric \(\rho(f, g) = \|f - g\|\).

Theorem: \((S, \rho)\) is a complete metric space

The set of bounded and continuous functions is complete: every Cauchy sequence in \(S\) converges to a limit in \(S\). See SLP or RMED for a proof.

Why these two properties?

Bounded so that the sup norm is finite and iterating the Bellman operator stays in the space. Continuous so that the Theorem of the Maximum guarantees the maximizer \(g(x)\) exists and is well-behaved. Completeness is what lets us invoke the Contraction Mapping Theorem — it ensures the limit of an iterative procedure actually lives in the space we started with.

2. Contractions and the Contraction Mapping Theorem

Definition: contraction mapping

Let \((S, \rho)\) be a metric space. An operator \(T : S \to S\) is a contraction with modulus \(\beta \in (0,1)\) if:

$$\rho(Tf, Tg) \leq \beta\, \rho(f, g) \quad \text{for all } f, g \in S.$$

Exercise. Let \(S = [0,1]\), \(\rho(x,y)=|x-y|\), and \(T\) be a function from \([0,1]\) to itself. What does it mean for \(T\) to be a contraction?

In our application, \(S\) will be the space of continuous bounded functions from \(X\) to \(\mathbb{R}\), and \(T\) will be the Bellman operator. The key result is Det DP p.13:

Contraction Mapping Theorem (CMT)

If \((S, \rho)\) is a complete metric space and \(T : S \to S\) is a contraction with modulus \(\beta\), then:

  1. There exists a unique fixed point \(s^* \in S\) such that \(s^* = Ts^*\).
  2. Iterations of \(T\) converge to the fixed point from any starting point: for any \(s_0 \in S\), $$\rho(T^n s_0,\, s^*) \leq \beta^n\, \rho(s_0, s^*).$$

Here \(T^{n+1}(s) = T(T^n(s))\).

Value function iteration

The CMT says: pick any starting guess \(V_0 \in S\) (even \(V_0 \equiv 0\)), apply the Bellman operator repeatedly, and the sequence \(V_0, TV_0, T^2 V_0, \ldots\) converges to the true value function \(V^*\) at the geometric rate \(\beta\). This is value function iteration, the workhorse algorithm for solving DP problems numerically. The error after \(n\) iterations is at most \(\beta^n \|V_0 - V^*\|\). With \(\beta = 0.95\), 100 iterations cut the error by a factor of \(0.95^{100} \approx 0.006\).

3. The Corollary: inheriting properties

One of the most elegant tools in the DP toolkit is the following corollary, which lets us read off properties of \(V^*\) Det DP p.14:

Corollary of the CMT (inheritance of properties)

Let \((S, \rho)\) be a complete metric space, let \(T : S \to S\) be a contraction, and let \(s^* = Ts^*\) be the unique fixed point. Suppose \(S' \subset S\) is a closed subset such that

$$T S' \subset S', \quad \text{i.e., } s \in S' \implies Ts \in S'.$$

Then \(s^* \in S'\). Moreover, if \(S'' \subset S'\) and \(T S'' \subset S''\), then \(s^* \in S''\).

How to use the Corollary

Strategy: to show \(V^*\) has some property (monotonicity, concavity, differentiability), define \(S' = \{f \in S : f \text{ has the property}\}\) and show (a) \(S'\) is closed in \(S\) and (b) \(Tf\) has the property whenever \(f\) does. The Corollary then gives \(V^* \in S'\) for free — no direct proof about \(V^*\) needed.

Common mistake: invariance is essential

It is not enough to know \(V^* \in S\) and that \(S'\) contains "nice" functions. You must verify that \(T\) maps \(S'\) into itself — i.e., the property is preserved by one application of \(T\). Without \(TS' \subset S'\), the Corollary gives nothing.

4. Blackwell's Sufficient Conditions

Checking the contraction inequality \(\|Tf - Tg\| \leq \beta\|f-g\|\) directly can be tedious. Blackwell's conditions offer a convenient shortcut Det DP p.15:

Blackwell's Sufficient Conditions

Let \(S\) be the space of bounded functions from \(X\) to \(\mathbb{R}\) with the sup norm. Let \(T : S \to S\). Assume:

  1. Monotonicity. For any \(f, g \in S\) with \(f(x) \leq g(x)\) for all \(x \in X\): $$Tf(x) \leq Tg(x) \quad \text{for all } x \in X.$$
  2. Discounting. There exists \(\beta \in (0,1)\) such that for any \(f \in S\) and \(a \in \mathbb{R}_+\): $$T(f + a)(x) \leq Tf(x) + \beta a \quad \text{for all } x \in X.$$

Then \(T\) is a contraction with modulus \(\beta\).

Proof of Blackwell's Theorem
  1. For any \(f, g \in S\), write \(f = g + (f - g)\). Since \(\|f - g\|\) is the sup norm, we have \(f(x) \leq g(x) + \|f - g\|\) for all \(x\).
  2. Applying monotonicity: \(Tf(x) \leq T(g + \|f-g\|)(x)\).
  3. Applying discounting with \(a = \|f-g\|\): \(T(g + \|f-g\|)(x) \leq Tg(x) + \beta\|f-g\|\).
  4. Combining: \(Tf(x) \leq Tg(x) + \beta\|f-g\|\).
  5. Reversing the roles of \(f\) and \(g\): \(Tg(x) \leq Tf(x) + \beta\|f-g\|\).
  6. Taking sup over \(x\): \(\|Tf - Tg\| = \sup_x |Tf(x) - Tg(x)| \leq \beta\|f - g\|\). \(\square\)

5. The Bellman operator maps \(S\) into itself

Now we apply this to the Bellman operator \(Tv\)(x) = \(\max_{y \in \Gamma(x)}\{F(x,y) + \beta v(y)\}\). Assume \(F\) is bounded and continuous and \(\Gamma\) is continuous and has compact range Det DP p.17:

Theorem: \(T\) maps \(S\) into itself and is a contraction

Assume \(F : X \times X \to \mathbb{R}\) is bounded and continuous, and \(\Gamma : X \rightrightarrows X\) is continuous with compact range. Define the Bellman operator:

$$(Tv)(x) = \max_{y \in \Gamma(x)} \bigl\{F(x,y) + \beta v(y)\bigr\}.$$

Then: (i) \(T\) maps \(S\) into itself (the maximum exists and \(Tv\) is continuous and bounded); and (ii) \(T\) is a contraction with modulus \(\beta\).

Proof sketch
  1. \(T\) maps into \(S\): \(F\) bounded and continuous and \(\Gamma\) continuous with compact range imply \(F(x,y)+\beta v(y)\) is continuous in \((x,y)\). By the Theorem of the Maximum, \(Tv(x)\) is continuous and the maximizer \(g(x)\) exists. Boundedness of \(F\) and \(v\) gives \(\|Tv\| \leq \|F\|/(1-\beta) \lt \infty\).
  2. Monotonicity: If \(f \geq v\) pointwise, then \(F(x,y)+\beta f(y) \geq F(x,y)+\beta v(y)\) for each \((x,y)\). Taking the max over \(y\in\Gamma(x)\) preserves the inequality: \(Tf(x) \geq Tv(x)\).
  3. Discounting: \(T(v+a)(x) = \max_{y\in\Gamma(x)}\{F(x,y)+\beta(v(y)+a)\} = \max_{y\in\Gamma(x)}\{F(x,y)+\beta v(y)\} + \beta a = Tv(x) + \beta a\).
  4. By Blackwell's Theorem, \(T\) is a contraction with modulus \(\beta\). \(\square\)

6. Monotonicity of \(V^*\)

We now use the Corollary to establish that \(V^*\) is increasing in \(x\) Det DP p.19:

Theorem: \(V^*\) is increasing

Assume \(F(\cdot, y)\) is increasing in \(x\) for each fixed \(y\), and \(\Gamma\) is increasing in \(x\) (i.e., \(x \leq x' \implies \Gamma(x) \subset \Gamma(x')\)). Then the fixed point \(V^*\) satisfying \(V^* = TV^*\) is increasing in \(x\).

Proof (using the Corollary)
  1. Define \(S' = \{f \in S : f \text{ is increasing}\} \subset S\). \(S'\) is closed in \(S\) (the pointwise limit of increasing functions is increasing, and uniform limits of increasing functions converge to an increasing function).
  2. We show \(TS' \subset S'\): take \(f \in S'\) and \(x \leq x'\). Let \(g(x)\) achieve the maximum \(Tf(x) = F(x, g(x)) + \beta f(g(x))\).
  3. Since \(\Gamma(x) \subset \Gamma(x')\) (Γ increasing), we have \(g(x) \in \Gamma(x')\), so \(g(x)\) is feasible at \(x'\).
  4. Since \(F(\cdot,y)\) is increasing: \(F(x', g(x)) \geq F(x, g(x))\).
  5. Therefore: \(Tf(x') = \max_{y \in \Gamma(x')}\{F(x',y)+\beta f(y)\} \geq F(x', g(x)) + \beta f(g(x)) \geq F(x, g(x)) + \beta f(g(x)) = Tf(x)\).
  6. So \(Tf\) is increasing, i.e., \(Tf \in S'\). By the Corollary of the CMT, \(V^* \in S'\). \(\square\)

7. Concavity of \(V^*\)

By a parallel argument, convexity of the problem implies concavity of the value function Det DP p.20:

Theorem: \(V^*\) is concave

Assume \(X\) is convex, \(\Gamma\) is convex (i.e., \(y \in \Gamma(x)\) and \(y' \in \Gamma(x')\) imply \(\theta y + (1-\theta)y' \in \Gamma(\theta x + (1-\theta)x')\) for all \(\theta \in (0,1)\)), and \(F\) is concave in \((x,y)\). Then \(V^*\) is concave in \(x\). Moreover, if \(F\) is strictly concave in \((x,y)\), then \(V^*\) is strictly concave.

Proof (using the Corollary)
  1. Define \(S'' = \{f \in S : f \text{ is concave}\}\). \(S''\) is closed in \(S\) (uniform limits of concave functions are concave).
  2. We show \(TS'' \subset S''\): take \(f \in S''\), any \(x, x' \in X\), and \(\theta \in (0,1)\). Let \(x^\theta = \theta x + (1-\theta)x'\).
  3. Let \(y\) achieve \(Tf(x) = F(x,y)+\beta f(y)\) and \(y'\) achieve \(Tf(x') = F(x',y')+\beta f(y')\).
  4. Define \(y^\theta = \theta y + (1-\theta)y'\). By convexity of \(\Gamma\): \(y^\theta \in \Gamma(x^\theta)\), so it is feasible.
  5. By concavity of \(F\): \(F(x^\theta, y^\theta) \geq \theta F(x,y) + (1-\theta)F(x',y')\).
  6. By concavity of \(f\): \(f(y^\theta) \geq \theta f(y) + (1-\theta)f(y')\).
  7. Therefore: \(Tf(x^\theta) \geq F(x^\theta, y^\theta) + \beta f(y^\theta) \geq \theta[F(x,y)+\beta f(y)] + (1-\theta)[F(x',y')+\beta f(y')] = \theta Tf(x) + (1-\theta)Tf(x')\).
  8. So \(Tf\) is concave, i.e., \(Tf \in S''\). By the Corollary, \(V^* \in S''\). \(\square\)
Economic meaning of concavity

Concavity of \(V^*\) in \(x\) means diminishing marginal value of wealth: an extra unit of capital is worth more when you have little than when you have a lot. This is the DP analogue of diminishing marginal utility. It also implies (by the Benveniste-Scheinkman theorem in Lesson 42) that \(V^*\) is differentiable almost everywhere, and the derivative has a clean expression in terms of the primitives.

8. Quiz

What does the Contraction Mapping Theorem guarantee?

  • That Cauchy sequences converge in every metric space
  • A unique fixed point and geometric convergence of iterations
  • That the maximum of a continuous function is always attained
  • That bounded monotone sequences converge to their supremum
The CMT says: on a complete metric space, a contraction \(T\) has a unique fixed point \(s^* = Ts^*\), and for any starting point \(s_0\), the iterates satisfy \(\rho(T^n s_0, s^*) \leq \beta^n \rho(s_0, s^*)\) — geometric convergence at rate \(\beta\). The other statements are the completeness axiom, the Extreme Value Theorem, and the Monotone Convergence Theorem — all different results.

Blackwell's discounting condition says \(T(f+a)(x) \leq Tf(x) + \beta a\). Why does \(\beta\lt 1\) matter?

  • It ensures the max in the Bellman operator is always finite
  • It guarantees the feasibility correspondence Γ is non-empty
  • It makes \(T\) shrink differences so iterates converge together
  • It prevents the fixed point from lying at the boundary of \(S\)
The factor \(\beta \lt 1\) is what makes \(T\) a contraction: when you shift a function up by \(a\), \(T\) only shifts the image by \(\beta a \lt a\). Combined with monotonicity, this yields \(\|Tf - Tg\| \leq \beta\|f-g\|\). Without \(\beta \lt 1\), the gap between iterates would not shrink and they might not converge.

To show \(V^*\) is increasing via the Corollary, what must you verify?

  • That \(T\) maps increasing functions to increasing functions
  • That \(V^*\) is a fixed point of a monotone Bellman operator
  • That the feasibility set Γ contains only increasing sequences
  • That the starting guess \(V_0\) is an increasing function
The Corollary requires showing \(TS' \subset S'\) where \(S' = \{\text{increasing functions}\}\): if \(f\) is increasing, then \(Tf\) is also increasing. Once this invariance is established, the Corollary gives \(V^* \in S'\). The starting guess \(V_0\) is irrelevant to the conclusion — the Corollary works from any starting point.

Why does the proof of concavity of \(V^*\) need \(\Gamma\) to be convex?

  • To ensure the Bellman operator maps continuous functions to continuous ones
  • To verify that \(F\) is bounded on the feasible set
  • To apply the Blackwell discounting condition pointwise
  • To construct a feasible mix \(y^\theta = \theta y + (1{-}\theta)y'\) at the mix state \(x^\theta\)
The key step is: given optimal choices \(y\) at \(x\) and \(y'\) at \(x'\), define \(y^\theta = \theta y + (1-\theta)y'\) and use it to lower-bound \(Tf(x^\theta)\). This requires \(y^\theta\in\Gamma(x^\theta)\), which is exactly what convexity of \(\Gamma\) provides. Without it, \(y^\theta\) might not be feasible and the lower bound breaks.

9. True or False

True or False — justify in one or two lines
  1. If \(T\) is a contraction on \((S,\rho)\) and \(s_0 \in S\), then \(\rho(T^{100}s_0, s^*) \leq \beta^{100}\rho(s_0, s^*)\). Det DP p.13
    Answer

    True. This is exactly the second conclusion of the CMT: \(\rho(T^n s_0, s^*) \leq \beta^n \rho(s_0, s^*)\) for all \(n\geq 0\). Since \(\beta \in (0,1)\), after 100 iterations the distance to the fixed point is reduced by the factor \(\beta^{100}\). If \(\beta=0.95\), that is \(0.95^{100} \approx 0.006\), less than 1% of the original error.

  2. Blackwell's discounting condition alone (without monotonicity) is sufficient to conclude \(T\) is a contraction. Det DP p.15
    Answer

    False. Both conditions are needed. Monotonicity is used to go from the pointwise inequality \(f \leq g + \|f-g\|\) to \(Tf \leq T(g+\|f-g\|)\). Without monotonicity, that step fails. The discounting condition alone only tells you how \(T\) responds to adding a constant, not how it compares two arbitrary functions.

  3. If \(V^*\) is the fixed point of the Bellman operator and \(S' \subset S\) is closed with \(TS' \subset S'\), then \(V^* \in S'\). Det DP p.14
    Answer

    True. This is precisely the Corollary of the CMT. Starting from any \(s_0 \in S'\): \(Ts_0 \in S'\) (by invariance), \(T^2 s_0 \in S'\), and so on. The iterates stay in \(S'\) and converge (in \(S\)) to \(V^*\). Since \(S'\) is closed and contains the entire sequence, the limit \(V^*\) is in \(S'\).

  4. Strict concavity of \(F\) in \((x,y)\) implies that the optimal policy \(g^*\) is a function (not a correspondence). Det DP p.20
    Answer

    True. The Bellman equation maximizes \(F(x,y)+\beta V^*(y)\) over \(y \in \Gamma(x)\). Under the theorem's assumptions, \(V^*\) is concave. If \(F\) is strictly concave in \(y\) (or \(F+\beta V^*\) is strictly concave in \(y\)), the maximum is attained at a unique point \(g^*(x)\) for each \(x\). With only weak concavity, there might be a whole interval of optimizers, so strict concavity is needed to pin down a unique policy function.

10. Exercises

Exercise 41.1 Det DP p.20 (neoclassical growth: checking monotonicity assumptions)

Consider the neoclassical growth model with \(F(k, k') = U(f(k) - k')\), where \(U\) is strictly increasing, and \(\Gamma(k) = [0, f(k)]\).

  1. Show \(F(k, k')\) is increasing in \(k\) for each fixed \(k' \geq 0\). What property of \(U\) and \(f\) do you need? (8 pts)
  2. Show \(\Gamma(k) = [0, f(k)]\) is increasing in \(k\): i.e., \(k \leq k'\) implies \(\Gamma(k) \subset \Gamma(k')\). What property of \(f\) do you need? (5 pts)
  3. Conclude that \(V^*\) is increasing in \(k\). (3 pts)
Hint

For (a): increasing \(k\) with fixed \(k'\) increases consumption \(f(k)-k'\), which raises \(U\) since \(U'>0\). For (b): \(f\) increasing means \(f(k) \leq f(k')\), so any \(y \leq f(k) \leq f(k')\) is feasible at \(k'\) as well.

Show solution

(a) Fix \(k'\geq 0\). Let \(k \leq \tilde k\). Since \(f\) is increasing (Inada conditions imply \(f'>0\)), we have \(f(k) \leq f(\tilde k)\), so \(f(k)-k' \leq f(\tilde k)-k'\). Since \(U\) is strictly increasing (\(U'>0\)), \(U(f(k)-k') \leq U(f(\tilde k)-k')\), i.e., \(F(k,k') \leq F(\tilde k, k')\). Assumptions needed: \(f\) increasing and \(U\) increasing.

(b) Let \(k \leq k'\). Then \(f(k) \leq f(k')\) (since \(f\) is increasing). So \(\Gamma(k) = [0, f(k)] \subset [0, f(k')] = \Gamma(k')\). Assumption: \(f\) increasing.

(c) By parts (a) and (b), the assumptions of the monotonicity theorem are satisfied. By the theorem, \(V^*(k)\) is increasing in \(k\). \(\square\)

Exercise 41.2 Proof (Blackwell's conditions for the Bellman operator)

Let \((Tv)(x) = \max_{y \in \Gamma(x)}\{F(x,y) + \beta v(y)\}\) with \(\beta \in (0,1)\).

  1. Verify that \(T\) is monotone: if \(v(x) \leq w(x)\) for all \(x\), then \(Tv(x) \leq Tw(x)\) for all \(x\). (8 pts)
  2. Verify the discounting condition: for any constant \(a \geq 0\), show \(T(v+a)(x) = Tv(x) + \beta a\). (8 pts)
  3. Conclude from Blackwell's theorem that \(T\) is a contraction with modulus \(\beta\). (4 pts)
Show solution

(a) Since \(v(y) \leq w(y)\) for all \(y\), we have \(F(x,y)+\beta v(y) \leq F(x,y)+\beta w(y)\) for each \((x,y)\). Taking the max over \(y \in \Gamma(x)\) preserves the inequality: \(\max_{y \in \Gamma(x)}\{F(x,y)+\beta v(y)\} \leq \max_{y\in\Gamma(x)}\{F(x,y)+\beta w(y)\}\), i.e., \(Tv(x) \leq Tw(x)\).

(b) \(T(v+a)(x) = \max_{y\in\Gamma(x)}\{F(x,y)+\beta(v(y)+a)\} = \max_{y\in\Gamma(x)}\{F(x,y)+\beta v(y)\}+\beta a = Tv(x)+\beta a\). (The constant \(\beta a\) pulls out of the max.) Note this holds with equality, which is stronger than the \(\leq\) required by Blackwell.

(c) Both conditions of Blackwell's theorem are satisfied (with the constant \(\beta \in (0,1)\) appearing in the discounting condition). By Blackwell's theorem, \(T\) is a contraction with modulus \(\beta\). Combined with the fact that \(S\) is complete, the CMT gives a unique fixed point \(V^*\) with \(TV^* = V^*\). \(\square\)

Exercise 41.3 Exam-style (value function iteration convergence)

True or false: "If we apply the Bellman operator \(T\) starting from \(V_0 \equiv 0\), the sequence \(T^n V_0\) converges to \(V^*\) at the geometric rate \(\beta\)." Justify carefully. (15 pts)

Hint

The CMT bounds the distance after \(n\) iterations by \(\beta^n \rho(V_0, V^*)\). Is "geometric rate \(\beta\)" an exact or asymptotic statement? Also think about whether the choice \(V_0 \equiv 0\) is necessary.

Show solution

True (with clarification of "rate").

Under the standard boundedness assumptions, \(T\) is a contraction with modulus \(\beta \in (0,1)\) on the complete metric space \((S, \|\cdot\|)\). By the CMT, for any starting point \(V_0 \in S\):

$$\|T^n V_0 - V^*\| \leq \beta^n \|V_0 - V^*\|.$$

Starting from \(V_0 \equiv 0\): \(\|V_0 - V^*\| = \|V^*\| \leq \|F\|/(1-\beta) \lt \infty\) (since \(F\) is bounded). So:

$$\|T^n V_0 - V^*\| \leq \beta^n \cdot \|V^*\|.$$

This is a geometric bound at rate \(\beta\). The choice \(V_0 \equiv 0\) is not necessary — the CMT holds from any \(V_0 \in S\). The convergence is at least geometric (the bound may be tighter in practice). \(\square\)

Key takeaways

  • The Bellman operator \(T\) acts on \(S\), the space of continuous bounded functions with the sup norm. \(S\) is complete, which is the foundation for the CMT.
  • CMT: a contraction \(T\) on a complete metric space has a unique fixed point \(V^*\), and iterates \(T^n V_0 \to V^*\) at geometric rate \(\beta\). This is the basis for value function iteration.
  • Blackwell's conditions: (i) monotonicity (\(f \leq g \Rightarrow Tf \leq Tg\)) and (ii) discounting (\(T(f+a) \leq Tf + \beta a\)) together imply \(T\) is a contraction. The Bellman operator satisfies both.
  • Corollary: if \(S' \subset S\) is closed and \(T\)-invariant (\(TS' \subset S'\)), then \(V^* \in S'\). This lets you read off properties of \(V^*\) by checking them for one application of \(T\).
  • Monotonicity of \(V^*\): follows when \(F(\cdot,y)\) is increasing and \(\Gamma\) is increasing. Concavity of \(V^*\): follows when \(X\) and \(\Gamma\) are convex and \(F\) is concave in \((x,y)\). Both are proved via the Corollary.
