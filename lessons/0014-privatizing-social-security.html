Privatizing Social Security: Implicit Liabilities Become Explicit Debt

Covers Class Note III, pp. 1–14 · About 35 min · Prerequisites: Lesson 11, Lesson 13
By the end you can:
  1. write the household, government and market-clearing conditions of the OLG economy with taxes and government debt, and derive asset-market clearing from them (Walras' law);
  2. construct the privatized tax system that replaces a PAYG scheme and verify it is an equilibrium with the same consumption and interest rate;
  3. compute the resulting debt \(\tilde B_t=-\theta/(1+\bar r)\) and saving \(\tilde s_t=\theta/(1+\bar r)\), and state precisely what kind of Ricardian equivalence this is.
Primary source Alvarez, Class Note III, pp. 2–14. For government debt in OLG economies, the classic is Diamond, "National Debt in a Neoclassical Growth Model", AER 55(5), 1965. For Ricardian equivalence, see Barro (1974), "Are Government Bonds Net Wealth?", JPE 82(6).
Notation check: Class Note III flips Note II
  • Endowments flip. Here \(e^t_t=\alpha\) (young) and \(e^t_{t+1}=1-\alpha\) (old) N3 p.2. In Note II and Lessons 8–13 it was the reverse.
  • The PAYG tax is called \(\theta\), not \(\tau\). Here \(\tau^t_s\) denotes the tax paid by generation \(t\) at date \(s\), so a negative \(\tau\) is a transfer received.
  • \(B_t\) is government assets: \(B_t<0\) means the government is in debt.

1. The economy with taxes and government debt

There is one good per period and two-period lives, and agents can now trade one-period bonds at rate \(r_t\) N3 p.3.

Budgets and market clearing

Generation \(t\ge1\): $$s_t+c^t_t=e^t_t-\tau^t_t,\qquad c^t_{t+1}=(1+r_t)s_t+e^t_{t+1}-\tau^t_{t+1}.$$ Initial old: \(c^0_1=(1+r_0)s_0+e^0_1-\tau^0_1\).
Government (assets \(B_t\), purchases \(g_t\)): $$B_t+g_t=(1+r_{t-1})B_{t-1}+\tau^t_t+\tau^{t-1}_t .$$ Goods market: \(g_t+c^t_t+c^{t-1}_t=e^t_t+e^{t-1}_t\). Asset market: \(B_t+s_t=0\), with \(B_0+s_0=0\).

Eliminating \(s_t\) from the household's two budgets gives the lifetime budget constraint $$c^t_t+\frac{c^t_{t+1}}{1+r_t}=e^t_t-\tau^t_t+\frac{e^t_{t+1}-\tau^t_{t+1}}{1+r_t}.$$ The household cares only about the present value of its taxes, not their timing. That is the whole engine of this lesson.

Walras' law: asset-market clearing is automatic N3 p.4

If household budgets, the government budget and the goods market hold at every date, and \(B_0+s_0=0\), then \(B_t+s_t=0\) for all \(t\ge1\).

Proof
  1. Add the budgets of the two generations alive at \(t\). Young: \(s_t+c^t_t=e^t_t-\tau^t_t\). Old: \(c^{t-1}_t=(1+r_{t-1})s_{t-1}+e^{t-1}_t-\tau^{t-1}_t\). Summing, $$s_t+c^t_t+c^{t-1}_t=e^t_t+e^{t-1}_t+(1+r_{t-1})s_{t-1}-\tau^t_t-\tau^{t-1}_t .$$
  2. Use goods-market clearing, \(c^t_t+c^{t-1}_t=e^t_t+e^{t-1}_t-g_t\): $$s_t+\tau^t_t+\tau^{t-1}_t=(1+r_{t-1})s_{t-1}+g_t .$$
  3. Combine with the government budget \(B_t+g_t=(1+r_{t-1})B_{t-1}+\tau^t_t+\tau^{t-1}_t\). Adding the two equations, \(g_t\) and the taxes cancel: $$B_t+s_t=(1+r_{t-1})(B_{t-1}+s_{t-1}).$$ (The slide says "subtracting". It means moving the shared terms across; the arithmetic is the same.)
  4. Induction. \(B_0+s_0=0\) gives \(B_1+s_1=0\), and so on for every \(t\).
∎

So when we verify an equilibrium below, we only need household optimality, the government budget and goods-market clearing. Asset-market clearing then follows. (Compare Walras' law in Lesson 2.)

2. The status quo: pay-as-you-go

The PAYG system is \(\tau^t_t=\theta\) and \(\tau^t_{t+1}=-\theta\): each young person pays \(\theta\), and each old person receives \(\theta\). Assume \(g_t=0\), \(r_t=\bar r\) and \(s_0=B_0=0\) N3 p.5.

  • Government: taxes equal transfers each period, so \(B_t=(1+\bar r)B_{t-1}+\theta-\theta\). With \(B_0=0\), \(B_t=0\) for all \(t\).
  • Households: as in Note II, the equilibrium is no trade: \(s_t=0\), with \(c^t_t=\alpha-\theta\) and \(c^t_{t+1}=1-\alpha+\theta\). The interest rate \(\bar r\) is whatever makes autarky optimal at these after-tax endowments. With log preferences \((1-\beta)\log c_y+\beta\log c_o\), that is \(1+\bar r=\frac{(1-\beta)(1-\alpha+\theta)}{\beta(\alpha-\theta)}\).
  • Present value of generation \(t\)'s taxes: $$\tau^t_t+\frac{\tau^t_{t+1}}{1+\bar r}=\theta-\frac{\theta}{1+\bar r}=\frac{\theta\bar r}{1+\bar r}.$$ This is positive iff \(\bar r>0\). PAYG is a net tax on each generation exactly when the market return beats PAYG's implicit return of 0. The initial old receive \(\theta\) for free.

3. The reform: eliminate the old-age transfer, keep the present value

The privatized tax system N3 p.6
$$\tilde\tau^0_1=\tau^0_1=-\theta,\qquad \tilde\tau^t_t=\tau^t_t+\frac{\tau^t_{t+1}}{1+\tilde r_t},\qquad \tilde\tau^t_{t+1}=0 .$$

The initial old still get their promised \(\theta\). Every later generation gets no old-age transfer, and its tax when young is cut to the present value of its old lifetime taxes. Guessing \(\tilde r_t=\bar r\): $$\tilde\tau^t_t=\frac{\theta\bar r}{1+\bar r},\qquad \theta-\tilde\tau^t_t=\frac{\theta}{1+\bar r}\ \text{(the tax cut = PV of the lost transfer)}.$$

Now verify that this guess is an equilibrium with the same consumption. The slides do this in five pieces, numbered here.

Verification that \(\tilde r_t=\bar r\) and \(\tilde c=c\) N3 pp.7–11
  1. Budget sets are unchanged. At \(\tilde r=\bar r\), the right side of the lifetime budget is $$e^t_t-\tilde\tau^t_t+\frac{e^t_{t+1}-0}{1+\bar r}=e^t_t-\Big(\tau^t_t+\frac{\tau^t_{t+1}}{1+\bar r}\Big)+\frac{e^t_{t+1}}{1+\bar r}=e^t_t-\tau^t_t+\frac{e^t_{t+1}-\tau^t_{t+1}}{1+\bar r}.$$ This is identical to the PAYG budget. The initial old face the same tax \(-\theta\) and hold \(s_0=0\).
  2. Consumption is unchanged. Same preferences on the same budget set give the same choice: \(\tilde c^t_t=\alpha-\theta\), \(\tilde c^t_{t+1}=1-\alpha+\theta\), \(\tilde c^0_1=1-\alpha+\theta\). Every generation is indifferent between the systems, so goods markets still clear.
  3. Government debt. At \(t=1\), \(B_0=0\) and the government collects \(\tilde\tau^1_1=\theta\bar r/(1+\bar r)\) while paying \(\theta\) to the old: $$\tilde B_1=\frac{\theta\bar r}{1+\bar r}-\theta=-\frac{\theta}{1+\bar r}.$$ For \(t\ge2\) the old get nothing: \(\tilde B_t=(1+\bar r)\tilde B_{t-1}+\theta\bar r/(1+\bar r)\). If \(\tilde B_{t-1}=-\theta/(1+\bar r)\), then \(\tilde B_t=-\theta+\theta\bar r/(1+\bar r)=-\theta/(1+\bar r)\). By induction, \(\tilde B_t=-\theta/(1+\bar r)\) for all \(t\ge1\).
  4. Private saving. Young consumption is still \(\alpha-\theta\), but the young now pay only \(\tilde\tau^t_t\): $$\tilde s_t=\alpha-\frac{\theta\bar r}{1+\bar r}-(\alpha-\theta)=\frac{\theta}{1+\bar r}.$$ When old, this yields \((1+\bar r)\tilde s_t=\theta\), exactly replacing the eliminated transfer.
  5. Asset market. \(\tilde B_t+\tilde s_t=0\). (By Walras' law this had to happen.) All equilibrium conditions hold at \(\tilde r=\bar r\), so the guess is verified.
∎

4. Reading the reform

Interactive: what changes and what doesn't. Here \(\bar r\) comes from log preferences at the after-tax endowments \((\alpha-\theta,1-\alpha+\theta)\). Left: the tax paid by the young (solid) and the transfer received by the old (dashed), under PAYG (blue) and the privatized system (orange). The privatized old-age transfer is \(\theta\) at \(t=1\) only (the initial old) and zero afterwards. Right: government assets \(B_t\) and private saving \(s_t\). Under PAYG both are zero. Under privatization the government is in debt \(\tilde B_t=-\theta/(1+\bar r)\), held by the young as saving \(\tilde s_t\) (aqua). Try: raise \(\alpha\) until \(\bar r<0\). The privatized young tax turns negative (a subsidy), but the debt is still \(-\theta/(1+\bar r)\).

Date \(t=1\) N3 p.12

  • The initial old still receive the promised \(\theta\).
  • The young pay a lower tax; the cut is \(\theta/(1+\bar r)\), and they save exactly that amount, \(\tilde s_1=\theta/(1+\bar r)\).
  • The government finances the transfer to the old by issuing debt \(-\tilde B_1=\theta/(1+\bar r)\) to the young.

Dates \(t\ge2\) N3 p.13

  • Each young generation pays \(\theta\bar r/(1+\bar r)\) and buys \(\theta/(1+\bar r)\) of government bonds.
  • When old it gets no transfer, but its bonds pay \((1+\bar r)\cdot\theta/(1+\bar r)=\theta\).
  • The government taxes each new young generation to cover the interest, \(\bar r\cdot\theta/(1+\bar r)\), and rolls over the principal forever.
Intuition: the implicit debt was always there

A PAYG system is a promise: each young generation is told "pay \(\theta\) now, and the next generation will pay you \(\theta\)". That promise is a liability of the government even though it never appears on its balance sheet. Privatization writes it down. The government owes \(\theta\) to whoever is old next period, worth \(\theta/(1+\bar r)\) today, and it issues exactly that much explicit debt. Nothing real changes: consumption, welfare and the interest rate are the same. What changes is how claims are labelled: private saving rises, and so does government debt, one for one.

The government never repays, and that's fine here

The debt \(\tilde B_t\) is constant forever and is never retired. That is sustainable because the OLG economy always has a new generation to buy it (compare the transfer chain of Lesson 12). When \(\bar r<0\), the debt even shrinks on its own, \((1+\bar r)\tilde B\lt \tilde B\) in absolute value. The government then has income to pay out, so the privatized "tax" on the young becomes a subsidy, \(\theta\bar r/(1+\bar r)<0\) (Exercise 14.4).

5. Ricardian equivalence, OLG style

What the example shows N3 p.14

The reform changes the timing of each generation's taxes but not their present value: $$\tilde\tau^t_t+\frac{\tilde\tau^t_{t+1}}{1+\bar r}=\tau^t_t+\frac{\tau^t_{t+1}}{1+\bar r}\quad\text{for every }t.$$ Consequently \(\tilde c=c\) and \(\tilde r=r\), while \(\tilde s_t=\theta/(1+\bar r)\) and \(\tilde B_t=-\theta/(1+\bar r)\). Government debt can replace an implicit social-security liability without changing allocations, provided each generation's present-value budget is preserved.

Note the qualifier. Classical Ricardian equivalence (Barro 1974) says the timing of taxes doesn't matter at all, because infinitely-lived (or altruistic) households internalize all future taxes. In the OLG model, households don't care about taxes levied on future generations. So a debt policy that shifts present value across generations does change allocations (Exercise 14.6). In this lesson the reform was carefully designed to shift nothing across generations, and that is why it is neutral. The Blanchard–Yaari model makes the same point in continuous time (Lesson 20).

6. Check your understanding

1. After privatization, how do consumption and the interest rate compare with PAYG?
  • Consumption is unchanged but the interest rate rises
  • Young consumption rises because their taxes are lower
  • Both are unchanged since lifetime budgets are the same
  • Old consumption falls since the transfer is eliminated
Each generation's tax is cut by exactly the PV of the transfer it loses, so its lifetime budget set at \(\bar r\) is unchanged. Same budget, same choice, so goods markets clear at the same \(\bar r\).
2. What finances the initial old's transfer \(\theta\) at date 1 under the privatized system?
  • Government bonds sold to the young plus their reduced tax
  • A one-off tax on the initial old's own date-one endowment
  • The full PAYG tax θ, which the young still pay at date one
  • Nothing: the initial old lose their transfer at the reform
The young pay \(\theta\bar r/(1+\bar r)\) in taxes and buy \(\theta/(1+\bar r)\) of bonds, which sums to \(\theta\). Hence \(\tilde B_1=-\theta/(1+\bar r)\).
3. Why didn't we need to check asset-market clearing \(\tilde B_t+\tilde s_t=0\) separately?
  • Because government debt is always zero in equilibrium
  • Because households never save in an endowment economy
  • Because the interest rate adjusts to clear the bond market
  • Because budgets plus goods clearing imply it (Walras' law)
Summing household budgets, using goods clearing and adding the government budget gives \(B_t+s_t=(1+r_{t-1})(B_{t-1}+s_{t-1})\). With \(B_0+s_0=0\), it holds at all dates. (We checked it anyway, as a sanity check.)
4. Which debt policy would not be neutral in this OLG economy?
  • Cut a generation's young tax and raise its old tax in PV
  • Cut one generation's tax and raise the next generation's
  • Replace PAYG with debt preserving each generation's PV
  • Issue bonds to the young that their own old taxes repay
Shifting PV from one generation to another changes both generations' budget sets, so allocations change. Options a, c and d all preserve each generation's PV and are neutral.

Exercise 14.1 True or false? Justify. Problem set 2025, P1 Exam-inspired

Answer each in one to four lines.

  1. "In the OLG economy with taxes and government debt, the household budget constraints, the government budget constraint and goods-market clearing together imply asset-market clearing." Class Note III p.4 / Problem set 2025, P1 Q1
    Show answer

    True, given the initial condition \(B_0+s_0=0\). Combining the budgets with goods-market clearing gives \(B_t+s_t=(1+r_{t-1})(B_{t-1}+s_{t-1})\), and induction does the rest. This is Walras' law. (The slide says "subtracting", but you actually add the household and government constraints.)

  2. "Privatizing PAYG so that every generation's present value of taxes is unchanged leaves consumption and the interest rate unchanged." Problem set 2025, P1 Q3–Q6
    Show answer

    True. At the old interest rate, every lifetime budget set is the same, so every choice is the same. Saving and government debt change one-for-one, so the asset market still clears at \(\bar r\). This confirms the guess.

  3. "Under the privatized system, the government must eventually repay the debt it issued to pay the initial old." Exam-inspired
    Show answer

    False. \(\tilde B_t=-\theta/(1+\bar r)\) is constant forever. Each young generation's tax \(\theta\bar r/(1+\bar r)=-\bar r\tilde B\) pays exactly the interest, and the principal is rolled over to the next generation of buyers. That works because OLG never runs out of new generations.

  4. "After privatization, private saving rises by exactly the amount of government debt issued." Problem set 2025, P1 Q5–Q6
    Show answer

    True. \(\tilde s_t=\theta/(1+\bar r)=-\tilde B_t\). The young save their tax cut in government bonds, and at old age those bonds pay \(\theta\), exactly replacing the abolished transfer.

  5. "If \(\bar r<0\), the privatized system must tax the young more heavily than PAYG did." Exam-inspired
    Show answer

    False. The privatized tax is \(\tilde\tau^t_t=\theta\bar r/(1+\bar r)<0\) when \(\bar r<0\): the young receive a subsidy. The debt shrinks in real terms on its own, and the government passes that to the young (Exercise 14.4).

  6. "Ricardian equivalence fails in OLG models because households ignore their own future taxes." Exam-inspired
    Show answer

    False. Households fully account for their own future taxes. That is why re-timing taxes within one generation's lifetime is neutral (privatization; Exercise 14.6a). Equivalence fails when debt shifts present value across generations, because households don't care about taxes on generations not yet born (Exercise 14.6b).

  7. "In the endowment OLG economy, government debt that is rolled over forever, with no taxes, can make every generation better off." 2025 final-review notes: a welfare role for government debt
    Show answer

    True, when the no-debt equilibrium has \(\bar r<0\). Debt then plays the role of PAYG (or of a valued bubble). It supplies the intergenerational asset the young want, and the equilibrium moves to \(r=0\) and the best symmetric allocation (Exercise 14.7). With \(\bar r>0\) no such debt can be sustained.

  8. "Abolishing PAYG in an economy with a positive interest rate is a Pareto improvement, because PAYG's implicit return (zero) is below the market rate." Exam-inspired
    Show answer

    False. Every generation \(t\ge1\) gains, but the initial old lose their transfer \(\theta\). The PAYG equilibrium has \(\bar r>0\) and finite value, so by the 1WT it is Pareto optimal, and any change hurts someone (Exercise 14.5).

Exercise 14.2 Problem set 2025, P1 (Q1–Q7) Slides N3 pp.3–14

Generation \(t\ge1\) faces \(s_t+c^t_t=e^t_t-\tau^t_t\) and \(c^t_{t+1}=s_t(1+r_t)+e^t_{t+1}-\tau^t_{t+1}\). The initial old have \(c^0_1=s_0(1+r_0)+e^0_1-\tau^0_1\). The government budget is \(B_t+g_t=B_{t-1}(1+r_{t-1})+\tau^t_t+\tau^{t-1}_t\), goods-market clearing is \(g_t+c^t_t+c^{t-1}_t=e^t_t+e^{t-1}_t\), and \(B_0+s_0=0\). PAYG: \(\tau^t_t=\theta\), \(\tau^t_{t+1}=-\theta\), with \(g_t=0\), \(e^t_t=\alpha\), \(e^{t-1}_t=1-\alpha\), \(s_0=B_0=0\) and a constant rate \(\bar r\). The privatized system is \(\tilde\tau^0_1=\tau^0_1\), \(\tilde\tau^t_t=\tau^t_t+\tau^t_{t+1}/(1+\tilde r_t)\), \(\tilde\tau^t_{t+1}=0\). Guess \(\tilde r_t=\bar r\).

  1. (15 pts) Use the budgets and goods-market clearing to show that the asset market clears: \(B_t+s_t=0\) for all \(t\). Which equations did you not use?
  2. (5 pts) Interpret \((\tilde\tau^t_t,\tilde\tau^t_{t+1})\). Two lines.
  3. (10 pts) Show that \(\tilde c^t_t=c^t_t\), \(\tilde c^t_{t+1}=c^t_{t+1}\) and \(\tilde c^0_1=c^0_1\).
  4. (10 pts) Show that \(\tilde B_t=-\theta/(1+\bar r)\) for all \(t\ge1\). Hint: solve the government budget at \(t=1\), then use induction.
  5. (5 pts) Show that \(\tilde s_t=\theta/(1+\bar r)\).
  6. (5 pts) Verify that the asset market clears, and say what this confirms.
  7. (10 pts) Interpret the asset market at \(t=1\) and at \(t\ge2\): who issues, who buys, and who pays the interest?
Show solution
  1. (a) From the young's budget, \(\tau^t_t=e^t_t-s_t-c^t_t\). From the old's, \(\tau^{t-1}_t=e^{t-1}_t+s_{t-1}(1+r_{t-1})-c^{t-1}_t\). From goods-market clearing, \(g_t=e^t_t+e^{t-1}_t-c^t_t-c^{t-1}_t\). Substituting all three into the government budget, consumption and endowments cancel, leaving \(B_t+s_t=(1+r_{t-1})(B_{t-1}+s_{t-1})\). Induction from \(B_0+s_0=0\) gives \(B_t+s_t=0\). Not used: asset-market clearing itself (it is implied), and household optimality (the identity holds for any plans satisfying the budgets).
  2. (b) Each generation's present value of taxes is unchanged, \(\tilde\tau^t_t+\tilde\tau^t_{t+1}/(1+\bar r)=\theta-\theta/(1+\bar r)\), but nothing is paid or received when old. The young's tax falls from \(\theta\) to \(\theta\bar r/(1+\bar r)\), a cut equal to the PV of the abolished benefit. The initial old still receive \(\theta\).
  3. (c) The lifetime budget is \(c^t_t+\frac{c^t_{t+1}}{1+\bar r}=e^t_t+\frac{e^t_{t+1}}{1+\bar r}-\big(\tau^t_t+\frac{\tau^t_{t+1}}{1+\bar r}\big)\). The tax PV is the same under both systems, so the budget set is the same and so is the optimal choice. The initial old face the same tax and consume all their wealth. Hence \(\tilde c^t_t=\alpha-\theta\) and \(\tilde c^t_{t+1}=1-\alpha+\theta\).
  4. (d) At \(t=1\): \(\tilde B_1=\tilde\tau^0_1+\tilde\tau^1_1=-\theta+\theta\bar r/(1+\bar r)=-\theta/(1+\bar r)\). For \(t\ge2\): \(\tilde B_t=(1+\bar r)\tilde B_{t-1}+\theta\bar r/(1+\bar r)\). If \(\tilde B_{t-1}=-\theta/(1+\bar r)\), then \(\tilde B_t=-\theta+\theta\bar r/(1+\bar r)=-\theta/(1+\bar r)\). Errors in a circulating solution: it writes the government budget with \(\tilde\tau^{t-1}_t/(1+\bar r)\) and gets the sign of \(\tilde\tau^0_1\) wrong mid-derivation. The final answer is right.
  5. (e) Subtract the two young-age budgets, using \(\tilde c=c\): \(\tilde s_t=s_t+(\tau^t_t-\tilde\tau^t_t)=0+\theta-\theta\bar r/(1+\bar r)=\theta/(1+\bar r)\). When old, this pays \((1+\bar r)\tilde s=\theta\), exactly replacing the benefit.
  6. (f) \(\tilde B_t+\tilde s_t=0\) for every \(t\). Together with (a) and (c), this confirms that \(\tilde r=\bar r\) is an equilibrium of the privatized economy.
  7. (g) At \(t=1\), the government pays \(\theta\) to the initial old but collects only \(\theta\bar r/(1+\bar r)\) from the young. It covers the gap by issuing debt \(\theta/(1+\bar r)\), which the young buy with their tax cut. At \(t\ge2\), each young generation pays \(\theta\bar r/(1+\bar r)=-\bar r\tilde B\), exactly the interest on the debt, and buys the outstanding bonds from the old. The principal is rolled over forever. The implicit PAYG liability has become explicit debt, while consumption, welfare and \(r\) are unchanged.
Exercise 14.3 Slides N3 p.6

The definition of \(\tilde\tau^t_t\) uses \(\tilde r_t\), the interest rate after the reform, which we don't know in advance. Explain the logic of "guess \(\tilde r=\bar r\), then verify". What would go wrong if the government used the wrong interest rate in the formula?

Show solution

The tax system depends on the equilibrium price, so we look for a fixed point. Conjecture a price, compute the policy and the households' responses, and check that markets clear at the conjectured price. If they do, we have found an equilibrium. Suppose the government computed \(\tilde\tau\) with some \(r'\ne\bar r\). Then each generation's lifetime budget at \(\bar r\) would change by \(\theta\big(\frac{1}{1+r'}-\frac{1}{1+\bar r}\big)\). Consumption would change, \(\bar r\) would no longer clear the market, and the reform would redistribute across generations instead of being neutral.

Exercise 14.4 Exam-inspired (privatizing in a dynamically inefficient economy)

Same economy as Exercise 14.2.

  1. (10 pts) Show that the PAYG equilibrium has \(\bar r<0\) iff \(1-\alpha+\theta<\beta\). Hint: no private trade, so \(1+\bar r\) is the MRS at the after-tax endowment.
  2. (5 pts) When \(\bar r<0\), what is the sign of the privatized young tax \(\tilde\tau^t_t\)? Interpret.
  3. (10 pts) Verify that the government budget holds at \(t\ge2\) with \(\tilde B=-\theta/(1+\bar r)\). Explain where the government's "income" comes from when \(\bar r<0\).
  4. (5 pts) Under PAYG, is each generation \(t\ge1\) a net taxpayer or a net recipient in present value? Connect your answer to Lesson 11.
Show solution

(a) The after-tax endowments are \((\alpha-\theta,1-\alpha+\theta)\), so \(1+\bar r=\frac{(1-\beta)(1-\alpha+\theta)}{\beta(\alpha-\theta)}\). This is \(<1\) iff \((1-\beta)(1-\alpha+\theta)<\beta(\alpha-\theta)\). Expanding, that is \(1-\alpha+\theta<\beta[(1-\alpha+\theta)+(\alpha-\theta)]=\beta\). So the after-tax old endowment must be below the share \(\beta\) the old would choose at \(r=0\).
(b) \(\tilde\tau^t_t=\theta\bar r/(1+\bar r)<0\): the young receive a subsidy. The reform replaces a system whose PV of taxes is negative.
(c) \((1+\bar r)\tilde B+\tilde\tau^t_t=-\theta+\frac{\theta\bar r}{1+\bar r}=-\frac{\theta}{1+\bar r}=\tilde B\) ✓. With \(\bar r<0\), the debt's real value shrinks by \(-\bar r\,|\tilde B|>0\) each period without any action. That "negative interest" is the resource the government hands to the young.
(d) The PV of PAYG taxes is \(\theta\bar r/(1+\bar r)<0\), so each generation is a net recipient. PAYG's implicit return (0) beats the market return (\(\bar r<0\)), which is exactly when PAYG can be Pareto-improving.

Exercise 14.5 Exam-inspired (abolishing PAYG without compensation)

Suppose instead the government simply abolishes PAYG at date 1: no transfer to the initial old, and no tax on anyone. Assume the economy without PAYG has a positive interest rate.

  1. Who loses and who gains?
  2. Can the abolition be a Pareto improvement? Answer using the First Welfare Theorem.
  3. Contrast with privatization. Why is privatization "free" while abolition is not?
Show solution

(a) The initial old lose \(\theta\). Generations \(t\ge1\) now consume their endowment \((\alpha,1-\alpha)\) instead of \((\alpha-\theta,1-\alpha+\theta)\). With a positive interest rate in the no-PAYG economy, each young person's MRS at the endowment exceeds 1. Moving toward more young consumption along \(c_y+c_o=1\) raises utility, so they gain.
(b) No. The PAYG equilibrium is a CE of an exchange economy with after-tax endowments. Its \(\bar r\) is positive, because PAYG raises the old's endowment and hence \(\bar r\). So the value of the aggregate endowment is finite, and the 1WT says the PAYG equilibrium is Pareto optimal. Any change must hurt someone, here the initial old.
(c) Privatization keeps every generation's PV of taxes, including the initial old's, so it reallocates nothing. The cost of honouring the initial old's promise doesn't disappear; it becomes explicit debt that is rolled over forever. Abolition defaults on that implicit debt, which is why the initial old pay.

Exercise 14.6 Exam-inspired (when debt is not neutral)

Start from the economy with no taxes and no debt (\(\theta=0\)), endowments \((\alpha,1-\alpha)\), and interest rate \(r^*\). At date 1 the government gives each young person \(\Delta\) and issues bonds worth \(\Delta\).

  1. Policy A: the debt is repaid at date 2 by taxing the same generation (now old) \((1+r^*)\Delta\). Show that nothing real changes.
  2. Policy B: the debt is repaid at date 2 by taxing generation 2 (young) \((1+r^*)\Delta\). Who gains and who loses? Is the interest rate at date 1 unchanged?
Show solution

(a) Generation 1's lifetime budget changes by \(+\Delta-(1+r^*)\Delta/(1+r^*)=0\). It consumes as before and saves the extra \(\Delta\) in government bonds, so \(s_1=\Delta=-B_1\) and the asset market clears. This is the Ricardian experiment within a generation, and it is neutral.
(b) Asset clearing at date 1 forces \(s_1=\Delta\). So generation 1 consumes \(c^1_1=\alpha+\Delta-\Delta=\alpha\) when young and \(c^1_2=1-\alpha+(1+r_1)\Delta\) when old: it gains. Generation 2 pays \((1+r_1)\Delta\) when young and holds no bonds, so it consumes \((\alpha-(1+r_1)\Delta,\ 1-\alpha)\): it loses. The interest rate is not unchanged. Generation 1 must be willing to hold \(\Delta\) of bonds even though it is richer when old. So \(1+r_1=v_1/v_2\) evaluated at \((\alpha,1-\alpha+(1+r_1)\Delta)\), which exceeds \(1+r^*\) because old-age consumption is higher. The allocation changes: debt is not neutral when it moves PV across generations. This is the failure of Ricardian equivalence in OLG models, developed further in Lesson 20.

Exercise 14.7 Exam-inspired (rolled-over debt as a substitute for PAYG)

Same endowments \((\alpha,1-\alpha)\) and log preferences, no PAYG. At date 1 the government issues debt \(b\) per young person (so \(B_t=-b\)), hands the proceeds to the initial old, and rolls the debt over forever with no taxes: \(B_t=(1+r_{t-1})B_{t-1}\).

  1. (5 pts) Write the no-policy equilibrium rate \(\bar r\).
  2. (5 pts) Show that a stationary equilibrium with \(b>0\) requires \(r=0\). One line.
  3. (10 pts) Derive the young's saving \(s(r)\) and use asset-market clearing to find \(b\). Show that \(b>0\) iff \(\bar r<0\).
  4. (10 pts) Find the allocation. Does it Pareto-dominate the no-policy equilibrium? Include the initial old.
  5. (5 pts) Which PAYG tax \(\theta\) gives the same allocation? What other "asset" in this course does the same job?
Show solution
  1. (a) No trade, so \(1+\bar r=\frac{(1-\beta)(1-\alpha)}{\beta\alpha}\). This is \(<1\) iff \(1-\alpha<\beta\), i.e. \(\alpha+\beta>1\).
  2. (b) Constant \(B_t=-b\ne0\) with \(B_t=(1+r)B_{t-1}\) requires \(1+r=1\).
  3. (c) Wealth is \(\alpha+(1-\alpha)/(1+r)\), and \(c_y=(1-\beta)\times\)wealth, so \(s(r)=\alpha-c_y=\beta\alpha-\frac{(1-\beta)(1-\alpha)}{1+r}\). Asset-market clearing \(s_t+B_t=0\) at \(r=0\) gives \(b=s(0)=\beta\alpha-(1-\beta)(1-\alpha)=\alpha+\beta-1\). This is positive iff \(\alpha+\beta>1\iff\bar r<0\).
  4. (d) The young consume \(\alpha-b=1-\beta\), and the old consume \(1-\alpha+b=\beta\): the best symmetric allocation. Every generation \(t\ge1\) strictly prefers it to the endowment (unless \(b=0\)), and the initial old gain \(b>0\). So it Pareto-dominates the no-policy CE exactly when that CE has \(\bar r<0\).
  5. (e) PAYG with \(\theta=b=\alpha+\beta-1\) gives \((\alpha-\theta,1-\alpha+\theta)=(1-\beta,\beta)\). The same job is done by a valued bubble (the zero-dividend tree of Exercise 12.6(f)) and by fiat money. In Blanchard–Yaari, the positive debt steady state \(\bar B\) exists iff the autarky rate \(\theta-\alpha\) (BY notation) is negative (Lesson 20). It is the same logic.

Key takeaways

  • Household budgets plus the government budget plus goods clearing imply \(B_t+s_t=0\) (Walras' law), given \(B_0+s_0=0\).
  • PAYG costs each generation \(\theta\bar r/(1+\bar r)\) in PV, which is negative iff \(\bar r<0\).
  • Privatization keeps every generation's PV of taxes: \(\tilde\tau^t_t=\theta\bar r/(1+\bar r)\), \(\tilde\tau^t_{t+1}=0\), and the initial old still get \(\theta\).
  • Result: consumption and \(\bar r\) unchanged; \(\tilde s_t=\theta/(1+\bar r)\) and \(\tilde B_t=-\theta/(1+\bar r)\). The implicit PAYG liability becomes explicit, rolled-over debt.
  • This is Ricardian equivalence generation by generation. Debt that shifts PV across generations is not neutral.
