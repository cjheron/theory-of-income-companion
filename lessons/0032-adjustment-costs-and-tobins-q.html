Adjustment Costs, Investment and Tobin's q

Covers Euler Equations & Transversality, slides 45–52 · About 40 min · Prerequisites: Lesson 31 (homogeneous problems, linear policies), Lesson 27 (the Euler equation and sufficiency)
By the end you can:
  1. write a firm's investment problem as a dynamic problem \([X,\Gamma,F,\beta]\) and explain why it is the same as an economy with linear utility;
  2. show that without adjustment costs capital jumps to its steady state in one period, and that a linear technology has no interior solution at all;
  3. derive the Euler equation for the linear technology with adjustment costs, prove that the growth rate \(\bar g\) is unique and lies in \((1,1/\beta)\), and sign its comparative statics;

1. Where we are

Lesson 31 showed that when \(F\) is homogeneous of degree one and \(\Gamma\) is a cone, the optimal policy is linear, \(x_{t+1}=\bar g\,x_t\). The Euler equation then collapses from a second-order difference equation to a single equation in one unknown, \(F_y(1,\bar g)+\beta F_x(1,\bar g)=0\). The last application in the deck uses this result for a firm that invests subject to adjustment costs. It delivers three things at once:

  • a theory of the growth rate of a firm, pinned down by technology, adjustment costs and the interest rate;
  • a closed form for the firm's market value that turns out to be linear in capital;
  • a clean statement of Tobin's q. The market-to-book ratio, which you can observe, equals the marginal cost of investing, so it is a sufficient statistic for investment.

The Euler-equation tools are those of Lesson 27: write \(F\), take \(F_x\) and \(F_y\), impose the Euler equation, and check transversality. Here, transversality ends up being the condition \(\beta\bar g\lt1\), which keeps the firm's value finite.

2. The investment problem

A firm (or an economy with linear utility \(u(c)=c\)) owns capital \(k_t\), produces \(f(k_t)\), and invests \(i_t\). Capital follows \(k_{t+1}=i_t+(1-\delta)k_t\). Installing capital may cost extra goods, \(\phi(i_t/k_t)\,k_t\). The firm maximizes the present value of its cash flows EE p.45:

$$\max_{\{i_t\}_{t=0}^\infty}\ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\beta^t\Big[f(k_t)-i_t-\phi\Big(\frac{i_t}{k_t}\Big)k_t\Big]\quad\text{s.t. } k_{t+1}=i_t+(1-\delta)k_t,\ k_0\text{ given.}$$
Why "a firm" and "an economy with \(u(c)=c\)" are the same problem

The bracket is the firm's dividend: output minus investment spending minus installation costs. A price-taking firm owned by agents who discount at \(r\) maximizes the present value of dividends at \(\beta=1/(1+r)\). In the planner's version, the bracket is consumption, and with \(u(c)=c\) the planner maximizes \(\sum\beta^tc_t\), the same objective. Linear utility means there is no motive to smooth consumption. Everything that slows investment down must come from the technology, here the adjustment cost \(\phi\). Two differences between the versions are worth noting. The planner needs \(c_t\ge0\), while a firm can have negative dividends (it raises equity). And the firm's \(\beta\) is a market price, while the planner's is a preference parameter.

To put this in the notation of Lesson 26, use the law of motion to substitute out the control, \(i_t=k_{t+1}-(1-\delta)k_t\). The state is \(x=k\), next period's state is \(y=k'\), and

$$F(x,y)=f(x)-\big[y-(1-\delta)x\big]-\phi\Big(\frac{y-(1-\delta)x}{x}\Big)x .$$

When investment is reversible, as assumed throughout, \(\Gamma(x)=\mathbb R_+\) for the firm. The planner would add \(c\ge0\).

3. Concave technology, no adjustment costs ("old news")

Start with \(\phi\equiv0\) and \(f\) strictly concave, satisfying the Inada conditions. Investment may be positive or negative EE p.46. Then

$$F(x,y)=f(x)+(1-\delta)x-y,\qquad F_x(x,y)=f'(x)+1-\delta,\qquad F_y(x,y)=-1 .$$
Proposition (slide 46): the steady state is reached immediately

Let \(k^*\) solve \(f'(k^*)=r+\delta\). For every \(k_0>0\), the optimal plan is \(k_t=k^*\) for all \(t\ge1\). Investment is \(i_0=k^*-(1-\delta)k_0\), which may be negative, and \(i_t=\delta k^*\) for \(t\ge1\).

Proof
  1. Euler equation. \(F_y(k_t,k_{t+1})+\beta F_x(k_{t+1},k_{t+2})=0\) reads \(-1+\beta\big[f'(k_{t+1})+1-\delta\big]=0\). Only \(k_{t+1}\) appears: \(f'(k_{t+1})=1/\beta-1+\delta=r+\delta\).
  2. Unique solution. \(f'\) is strictly decreasing, and by the Inada conditions it runs from \(+\infty\) to \(0\). So \(f'(k)=r+\delta\) has exactly one root \(k^*>0\), and the Euler equation forces \(k_{t+1}=k^*\) for every \(t\ge0\), starting from any \(k_0\).
  3. Transversality. \(\beta^tF_x(k_t,k_{t+1})k_t=\beta^t(1+r)k^*\to0\), since \(f'(k^*)+1-\delta=1+r\) and \(\beta\lt1\).
  4. Sufficiency. \(F\) is concave, being a concave function of \(x\) plus a linear function of \(y\). \(F_x=f'+1-\delta>0\), and \(X=\mathbb R_+\). By the theorem of Lesson 27, a path satisfying the Euler equation and transversality is optimal. ∎
Why there is no transition

The marginal cost of a unit of next period's capital is one unit of goods today, whatever the quantity. The marginal benefit is \(\beta(f'(k')+1-\delta)\). Neither side depends on \(k_t\), so today's capital is irrelevant for tomorrow's choice. There is nothing to smooth, because utility is linear, and no penalty for large changes, because there are no adjustment costs. So the firm closes any gap in one period. This is the "old news" of the slide and the benchmark adjustment costs are meant to break. Without them, investment is infinitely elastic to any change in \(r\), which is counterfactual. Compare the neoclassical growth model with concave \(u\) (Lesson 27, and the linear-utility exercise in Lesson 29). With concave \(u\), the transition is slow because households want smooth consumption.

Can \(f\) be linear here? No.

Let \(f(k)=Ak\), with no adjustment costs. Then \(F_x=A+1-\delta\) is a constant, and the Euler equation becomes \(\beta(A+1-\delta)=1\), that is, \(A-\delta=r\). This involves no endogenous variable at all:

  • \(A-\delta>r\). No path satisfies the Euler equation. Each unit of capital returns more than \(r\), so the firm wants to invest without bound. With reversible investment and unlimited equity, the value is \(+\infty\). In the planner's version with \(c\ge0\), consuming nothing and reinvesting everything always beats any plan, but "consume at infinity" pays nothing. The supremum is not attained, and no optimum exists.
  • \(A-\delta\lt r\). Capital is a bad investment. The firm sells it all at once: \(k_1=0\), a corner where the Euler equation fails.
  • \(A-\delta=r\). The knife-edge case. Every path satisfies the Euler equation, and the firm is indifferent among all of them.

Either way, there is no interior, determinate growth rate. A linear technology needs a convex adjustment cost to produce one, which is the next model.

4. Linear technology with adjustment costs

Now let \(f(k)=Ak\) and keep the installation cost. Write everything in terms of the growth factor \(g=k_{t+1}/k_t\). Investment per unit of capital is \(i/k=g-(1-\delta)\). Define the adjustment-cost function EE p.47

$$a\Big(\frac{k_{t+1}}{k_t}\Big)\equiv\frac{k_{t+1}}{k_t}-(1-\delta)+\phi\Big(\frac{k_{t+1}-k_t(1-\delta)}{k_t}\Big),$$

the total cost, in goods per unit of current capital, of moving capital from \(k_t\) to \(k_{t+1}\). It is investment spending plus installation, both per unit of \(k_t\). The period return is then

$$F(x,y)=Ax-a\Big(\frac yx\Big)x .$$

\(F\) is homogeneous of degree one: \(F(\lambda x,\lambda y)=\lambda Ax-a(y/x)\lambda x=\lambda F(x,y)\). With \(\Gamma(x)=\mathbb R_+\), a cone, the result of Lesson 31 applies. The policy is \(k_{t+1}=\bar g\,k_t\) for a constant \(\bar g\), and the firm grows at a constant rate forever.

The four assumptions on \(a(\cdot)\) (slide 47), and what each one buys
  1. \(a\) is positive and strictly convex. Convexity makes the marginal cost \(a'(g)\) increase with the speed of adjustment, so spreading growth out is cheaper than doing it in one go. This is what produces an interior growth rate (§3 showed what happens without it). On "positive": \(a(1)=\delta+\phi(\delta)>0\) even when capital is held constant, because \(a\) includes replacement investment. The slide's gloss "any change implies cost" is loose. What matters is \(a>0\) and \(a''>0\).
  2. \(a'(1)=1\). Around constant capital, one more unit of \(k_{t+1}\) costs exactly one unit of goods: \(a'(g)=1+\phi'(g-1+\delta)\), so this says \(\phi'(\delta)=0\). Replacement investment has no marginal installation cost. It is a normalization that makes "q = 1/β" the no-growth benchmark (§7).
  3. \(a(1/\beta)>A\). Growing at the gross rate \(1/\beta=1+r\) would cost more than the firm's entire output. This bounds growth below the interest rate, so the present value of dividends stays finite (\(\beta\bar g\lt1\)).
  4. \(A-a(1)>r\). Keeping capital constant gives a net cash flow \(A-a(1)\) per unit of capital, which beats the interest rate. So capital is worth accumulating, and the firm grows: \(\bar g>1\).

5. The Euler equation and the growth rate

The derivatives depend only on \(y/x\)

Differentiate \(F(x,y)=Ax-a(y/x)x\) EE p.49:

$$F_x(x,y)=A-a\Big(\frac yx\Big)+a'\Big(\frac yx\Big)\frac yx,\qquad F_y(x,y)=-a'\Big(\frac yx\Big).$$

(For \(F_x\), use the product rule. \(\partial/\partial x\) of \(-a(y/x)x\) is \(-a(y/x)-x\,a'(y/x)\cdot(-y/x^2)\).) Both are homogeneous of degree zero, as they must be for an HD1 function. Interpretations:

  • \(-F_y=a'(g)\) is the marginal cost, in today's goods, of one more unit of capital tomorrow.
  • \(F_x\) is the marginal value of one more unit of capital today. It yields output \(A\), and it makes any given \(k'\) cheaper to reach: \(-a(g)+a'(g)g\) is the reduction in total adjustment cost from having a larger base.

The Euler equation is one equation in \(\bar g\)

Substitute \(x_{t+1}/x_t=x_{t+2}/x_{t+1}=\bar g\) into \(F_y(x_t,x_{t+1})+\beta F_x(x_{t+1},x_{t+2})=0\):

$$\boxed{\;-a'(\bar g)+\beta\big[A-a(\bar g)+a'(\bar g)\,\bar g\big]=0\;}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \beta A=\underbrace{\beta a(\bar g)+a'(\bar g)(1-\beta\bar g)}_{\equiv\,R(\bar g)} .$$

Guessing a constant growth rate is legitimate because Lesson 31's homogeneity result says the optimal policy is linear. If the optimum is unique, it must be one of these constant-growth paths.

Proposition (slides 48–49): a unique growth rate, between 1 and \(1/\beta\) EE p.48 EE p.49

Under the four assumptions, the Euler equation has exactly one solution \(\bar g\) in \((-\infty,1/\beta)\), and it satisfies \(1\lt\bar g\lt1/\beta\). Equivalently, the net growth rate satisfies \(0\lt\gamma\lt r\). The path \(k_t=\bar g^tk_0\) satisfies transversality.

Proof
  1. The left side of the Euler equation is decreasing. Let \(E(g)\equiv-a'(g)+\beta[A-a(g)+a'(g)g]\). Then \(E'(g)=-a''(g)+\beta[-a'(g)+a'(g)+a''(g)g]=-a''(g)(1-\beta g)\), which is strictly negative for \(g\lt1/\beta\), since \(a''>0\). Equivalently, \(R'(g)=\beta a'(g)+a''(g)(1-\beta g)-\beta a'(g)=a''(g)(1-\beta g)>0\) on \(g\lt1/\beta\). So there is at most one root below \(1/\beta\).
  2. At \(g=1\), \(R\) is below \(\beta A\). \(R(1)=\beta a(1)+a'(1)(1-\beta)=\beta a(1)+1-\beta\), using assumption 2. Assumption 4 says \(A-a(1)>r=(1-\beta)/\beta\). Multiplying by \(\beta\) gives \(\beta A>\beta a(1)+1-\beta=R(1)\).
  3. At \(g=1/\beta\), \(R\) is above \(\beta A\). \(R(1/\beta)=\beta a(1/\beta)+a'(1/\beta)\cdot0=\beta a(1/\beta)>\beta A\), by assumption 3.
  4. Existence and location. \(R\) is continuous, so by the intermediate value theorem there is \(\bar g\in(1,1/\beta)\) with \(R(\bar g)=\beta A\). By step 1, it is the only root below \(1/\beta\).
  5. Transversality. \(\beta^tF_x(k_t,k_{t+1})k_t=\beta^t\big[A-a(\bar g)+a'(\bar g)\bar g\big]\bar g^tk_0=(\beta\bar g)^t\cdot\text{const}\to0\), because \(\beta\bar g\lt1\). This is the condition \(\beta|\bar g|\lt1\) of Lesson 31. \(F\) is concave, because \(a\) convex makes \(a(y/x)x\) jointly convex (it is the perspective of a convex function). \(F_x=A-a(\bar g)+a'(\bar g)\bar g>0\) on the path, because the Euler equation gives \(\beta[A-a(\bar g)]=a'(\bar g)(1-\beta\bar g)>0\). And \(X=\mathbb R_+\). So the sufficiency theorem of Lesson 27 says this path is optimal. ∎

A root above \(1/\beta\) would mean growing faster than the interest rate. It violates transversality and gives an infinite value, so it is never the answer. That is why the slide restricts attention to \(g\lt1/\beta\).

Comparative statics in \(A\)

Higher productivity raises growth EE p.49. Differentiate \(R(\bar g)=\beta A\) to get \(R'(\bar g)\,d\bar g=\beta\,dA\), so

$$\frac{d\bar g}{dA}=\frac{\beta}{a''(\bar g)(1-\beta\bar g)}>0 .$$

In the figure, a higher \(A\) shifts the flat line up along an increasing curve.

When do "higher adjustment costs" lower growth?

The tempting answer is "whenever \(\tilde a(g)>a(g)\) for all \(g\neq1\)". That is not enough, because the Euler equation depends on the level of costs and on the marginal cost \(a'\) EE p.49. (The 2025 version of the slides claimed the pointwise ordering suffices. The 2026 deck corrected it.) What moves \(\bar g\) is the curve \(R\):

The right ordering

If \(\tilde R(g)\equiv\beta\tilde a(g)+\tilde a'(g)(1-\beta g)>R(g)\) on the relevant interval \([1,1/\beta)\), then \(\tilde{\bar g}\lt\bar g\). A sufficient condition on primitives is that the extra cost \(d\equiv\tilde a-a\) is non-negative and non-decreasing on \([1,1/\beta)\), since \(\tilde R-R=\beta d+d'(1-\beta g)\).

Proof. \(\tilde R(\bar g)>R(\bar g)=\beta A\), and \(\tilde R\) is increasing, so its crossing with \(\beta A\) lies to the left of \(\bar g\). For instance, raising \(\varphi\) in the figure adds \(d=\tfrac{\Delta\varphi}2(g-1)^2\), which is non-negative and increasing for \(g\ge1\), so growth falls.

Worked counterexample: pointwise higher costs, faster growth

Take any \(a\) satisfying the assumptions, with growth \(\bar g\in(1,1/\beta)\). Pick \(g_*>\bar g\) and small \(\varepsilon,\eta>0\), and set

$$\tilde a(g)=a(g)+\varepsilon\,(g_*-g)^2+\eta .$$

Then \(\tilde a>a\) everywhere, and \(\tilde a\) is strictly convex. At the old growth rate,

$$\tilde R(\bar g)-R(\bar g)=\beta\big[\varepsilon(g_*-\bar g)^2+\eta\big]-2\varepsilon(g_*-\bar g)(1-\beta\bar g)=\varepsilon(g_*-\bar g)\big[\beta(g_*-\bar g)-2(1-\beta\bar g)\big]+\beta\eta .$$

Choose \(g_*\) with \(0\lt g_*-\bar g\lt2(1-\beta\bar g)/\beta\), so the bracket is negative, then take \(\eta\) small enough. Then \(\tilde R(\bar g)\lt\beta A\), and the new crossing is to the right: \(\tilde{\bar g}>\bar g\). The extra cost is higher everywhere, but it falls as \(g\) rises towards \(g_*\). Growing a little faster now saves cost at the margin, and the margin is what the Euler equation sees.

This \(\tilde a\) has \(\tilde a'(1)=1-2\varepsilon(g_*-1)\ne1\). If you insist on keeping assumption 2, use a bump that vanishes to second order at \(g=1\), such as \(d(g)=\kappa(g-1)^2\big[(g_*-g)^2+\eta\big]\) with \(g_*\) slightly above \(\bar g\). A numerical check: with \(r=0.05\), \(\delta=0.1\), \(A=0.2\), \(\varphi=100\), we have \(\bar g=1.01127\). Adding this bump with \(g_*=1.0125\), \(\eta=10^{-6}\), \(\kappa=2\cdot10^4\) keeps \(\tilde a\) strictly convex with \(\tilde a'(1)=1\), and raises growth to \(1.01132\).

6. Reading the Euler equation

Rearrange the Euler equation EE p.50:

$$a'(\bar g)\,[1-\beta\bar g]=\beta\,[A-a(\bar g)]\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad a'(\bar g)=\beta\,\frac{A-a(\bar g)}{1-\beta\bar g}.$$
  • \(A-a(\bar g)\) is the dividend per unit of capital: output \(A\) minus everything spent on investment and installation, per unit of \(k_t\).
  • \(\dfrac{1}{1-\beta\bar g}=\sum_{t\ge0}(\beta\bar g)^t\) is the growing-annuity (Gordon growth) factor. It is the present value of a stream that starts at 1 and grows at gross rate \(\bar g\), discounted at \(\beta\). It is finite exactly when \(\beta\bar g\lt1\), which is transversality again.
  • So \(\beta\frac{A-a(\bar g)}{1-\beta\bar g}\) is the present value, as of today, of all future dividends generated by one extra unit of capital installed for tomorrow. Because everything is linear, a marginal unit of capital is worth the same as an average one. The Euler equation says: marginal cost of installing capital = present value of the dividends it generates.

In net rates, \(\gamma=\bar g-1\) and \(1/\beta=1+r\), so \(1/\beta-\bar g=r-\gamma\). Multiplying the Euler equation by \(1/\beta\) gives

$$a'(1+\gamma)=\frac{A-a(1+\gamma)}{r-\gamma}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad A=a(1+\gamma)+a'(1+\gamma)\,[r-\gamma].$$

This is a Gordon formula. The price of a unit of capital, \(a'\), equals its dividend divided by the interest rate minus the growth rate.

A higher interest rate lowers growth

\(\dfrac{d\gamma}{dr}=-\dfrac{a'(1+\gamma)}{a''(1+\gamma)\,(r-\gamma)}\lt0.\)

Proof
  1. Differentiate \(A=a(1+\gamma)+a'(1+\gamma)[r-\gamma]\) totally, with \(A\) fixed: \(0=a'\,d\gamma+a''\,[r-\gamma]\,d\gamma+a'\,(dr-d\gamma)\).
  2. The \(a'\,d\gamma\) terms cancel, leaving \(a''(r-\gamma)\,d\gamma=-a'\,dr\).
  3. Signs: \(a''>0\). \(r-\gamma>0\) because \(\bar g\lt1/\beta\). \(a'(\bar g)>a'(1)=1>0\) because \(\bar g>1\) and \(a\) is strictly convex. So \(d\gamma/dr\lt0\). ∎

Intuition. A higher \(r\) shrinks the Gordon factor \(1/(r-\gamma)\), so the dividends from a new unit of capital are worth less, and the firm accepts less marginal installation cost: it grows more slowly. This is the q-theory version of "investment falls when interest rates rise". Unlike §3, the response is finite, because convex costs make investment respond smoothly.

7. Tobin's q

The value of the firm is linear in capital

Let \(V(k)\) be the maximized present value of dividends starting from capital \(k\) EE p.51. Along the optimal path, capital grows at \(\bar g\), so

$$V(k)=k\,[A-a(\bar g)]+\beta\,V(k\bar g).$$
\(V\) is homogeneous of degree one (the slide's "Exercise: why?")

For every \(\lambda>0\), \(V(\lambda k)=\lambda V(k)\).

Proof
  1. Take any feasible plan \(\{k_t\}\) from \(k_0=k\). Because \(\Gamma\) is a cone, \(\{\lambda k_t\}\) is feasible from \(\lambda k\).
  2. Because \(F\) is HD1, its payoff is \(\sum\beta^tF(\lambda k_t,\lambda k_{t+1})=\lambda\sum\beta^tF(k_t,k_{t+1})\). So \(V(\lambda k)\ge\lambda V(k)\).
  3. Apply the same argument from \(\lambda k\) with the factor \(1/\lambda\): \(V(k)\ge\frac1\lambda V(\lambda k)\). Combining the two, \(V(\lambda k)=\lambda V(k)\). ∎

So \(V(k)=V(1)\,k\), and \(V(k\bar g)=\bar g\,V(k)\). Substituting into the recursion:

$$V(k)=[A-a(\bar g)]\,k+\beta\bar g\,V(k)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad V(k)=\frac{A-a(\bar g)}{1-\beta\bar g}\,k ,$$

which is finite because \(\beta\bar g\lt1\). The same answer comes from summing the dividends directly: \(\sum_t\beta^t[A-a(\bar g)]\bar g^tk\).

Average q, marginal q, and the marginal cost of investment

Define Tobin's q as the market value of the firm divided by the replacement (book) value of its capital, with capital goods priced at 1 EE p.52:

$$q\equiv\frac{V(k)}{k}=V(1)=\frac{A-a(\bar g)}{1-\beta\bar g}.$$

Now use the Euler equation \(\beta[A-a(\bar g)]=a'(\bar g)(1-\beta\bar g)\):

$$\boxed{\;q=\frac{1}{\beta}\,a'(\bar g)=(1+r)\,a'(\bar g)\;}$$

The 2025 slides wrote "Tobin's q = derivative of adjustment cost \(a'(\cdot)\)", which contradicted the displayed formula. The 2026 deck states the \(1/\beta\) factor explicitly. Four consequences follow.

  1. Marginal q equals average q. \(V\) is linear, so \(V'(k)=V(1)=V(k)/k\). The shadow value of one more unit of installed capital, which governs investment, equals the observable market-to-book ratio. This is Hayashi's (1982) result: it needs a price-taking firm with constant returns in production and in installation. Here these are \(f=Ak\) and an adjustment cost of the form \(a(k'/k)k\).
  2. q is a sufficient statistic for investment. Since \(a'\) is strictly increasing, \(\bar g=a'^{-1}(\beta q)\). Productivity \(A\) and the interest rate \(r\) affect growth only through q. That is why "q should forecast the growth rate of capital": observe the stock market, invert the marginal cost, and read off investment.
  3. The benchmark is \(q=1+r\), not \(q=1\). Because \(a'(1)=1\), \(\bar g>1\iff a'(\bar g)>1\iff q>1/\beta=1+r\). A firm with \(1\lt q\lt1+r\) is shrinking.
  4. Timing explains the \(1/\beta\). \(V(k)\) is measured at the start of the period and includes this period's dividend. The ex-dividend value of next period's capital, per unit, is \(\beta V(k')/k'=\beta V(1)=a'(\bar g)\). With that convention, \(q^{\text{ex}}=a'(\bar g)\) and the benchmark is \(q^{\text{ex}}=1\), as in most textbooks and in continuous time, where the two conventions coincide. The investment first-order condition reads directly \(a'(k'/k)=\beta V'(k')\): marginal installation cost = discounted marginal q. (This is the Bellman-equation view of the problem, which arrives in lecture folder 8.)
The firm picks its growth rate to maximize a Gordon valuation

Because the optimal policy is a constant growth rate, the problem reduces to a one-dimensional maximization:

$$V(1)=\max_{g\lt1/\beta}\ \frac{A-a(g)}{1-\beta g}.$$

Its first-order condition, \(-a'(g)(1-\beta g)+\beta[A-a(g)]=0\), is the Euler equation. Faster growth costs dividends today, through the numerator, and buys a longer-lived growing stream, through the denominator. The optimum balances the two. The envelope theorem then gives comparative statics for q almost for free:

  • \(\partial q/\partial A=1/(1-\beta\bar g)>0\);
  • a higher \(\varphi\) lowers q, since \(\partial q/\partial\varphi=-\tfrac12(\bar g-1)^2/(1-\beta\bar g)\lt0\) with the figure's cost function;
  • a higher \(r\) (lower \(\beta\)) lowers q, since \(\partial q/\partial\beta=\bar g[A-a(\bar g)]/(1-\beta\bar g)^2>0\).

Discrete-time Tobin's q has not appeared on a past Alvarez exam. The continuous-time Hayashi model has (a 2010/11 final), and Exercise 32.4 shows that the discrete model reproduces its key formula exactly. The rest follows the exam format, with general functions and no numbers.

True or false? Justify. Past exam Exam-inspired

Answer True or False and justify in at most four lines.

  1. With \(f\) strictly concave and Inada, no adjustment costs, and reversible investment, the optimal capital stock is constant from period 1 on, whatever \(k_0\) is. slide 46
    Answer

    True. The Euler equation \(-1+\beta[f'(k_{t+1})+1-\delta]=0\) pins down \(k_{t+1}=k^*\), with \(f'(k^*)=r+\delta\), independently of \(k_t\). Transversality holds since \(k_t=k^*\) is bounded, and \(F\) is concave with \(F_x>0\), so the path is optimal (§3). With irreversible investment (\(i\ge0\)) the statement fails when \(k_0>k^*/(1-\delta)\), since capital can then only depreciate towards \(k^*\).

  2. With \(f(k)=Ak\) and no adjustment costs, the firm's problem has an interior solution in which capital grows at a constant rate determined by \(A\), \(\delta\) and \(r\). exam-inspired (slide 46)
    Answer

    False. The Euler equation becomes \(\beta(A+1-\delta)=1\), which contains no endogenous variable. If \(A-\delta>r\), the value is unbounded (no optimum). If \(A-\delta\lt r\), the firm sells all its capital at once (corner). If \(A-\delta=r\), every path is optimal. No determinate growth rate exists without convex adjustment costs.

  3. Under the assumptions of slide 47, the firm's capital grows faster than the interest rate when productivity \(A\) is high enough. exam-inspired
    Answer

    False. Assumption 3, \(a(1/\beta)>A\), gives \(R(1/\beta)>\beta A\), so \(\bar g\lt1/\beta\): \(\gamma\lt r\). A root with \(\beta\bar g\ge1\) would violate transversality and make \(V\) infinite. If \(A\) is raised enough to break assumption 3, there is no optimum at all, rather than an optimum with \(\gamma\ge r\).

  4. If \(\tilde a(g)>a(g)\) for all \(g\ne1\), the firm with adjustment-cost function \(\tilde a\) grows more slowly. exam-inspired (slide 48 Q6; the 2025 slides answered "true")
    Answer

    False in general. The Euler equation \(\beta A=\beta a(g)+a'(g)(1-\beta g)\) depends on \(a'\), which a pointwise ordering does not control. Counterexample (§5): \(\tilde a=a+\varepsilon(g_*-g)^2+\eta\) with \(g_*\) slightly above \(\bar g\) raises growth. Sufficient for lower growth: \(\tilde a-a\ge0\) and non-decreasing on \([1,1/\beta)\).

  5. In the linear model with adjustment costs, the marginal value of an extra unit of installed capital equals the firm's market-to-book ratio. exam-inspired (circulating practice question on marginal and average q)
    Answer

    True. \(F\) is HD1 and \(\Gamma\) is a cone, so \(V\) is HD1: \(V(k)=V(1)k\), and \(V'(k)=V(1)=V(k)/k\). Marginal q equals average q (Hayashi 1982). This needs constant returns in production and in installation.

  6. In the model of slides 47–52, Tobin's q, defined as \(V(k)/k\) with \(V\) including the current dividend, equals the marginal adjustment cost \(a'(\bar g)\). exam-inspired (the 2025 wording of slide 52)
    Answer

    False. \(V(1)=\frac{A-a(\bar g)}{1-\beta\bar g}\), and the Euler equation \(\beta[A-a(\bar g)]=a'(\bar g)(1-\beta\bar g)\) gives \(q=a'(\bar g)/\beta=(1+r)a'(\bar g)\). The statement is true for the ex-dividend ratio \(\beta V(k')/k'\).

  7. In this model, a firm with \(q>1\) is growing. exam-inspired
    Answer

    False with the cum-dividend definition. Since \(a'(1)=1\) and \(a'\) is increasing, \(\bar g>1\iff q=a'(\bar g)/\beta>1/\beta=1+r\). A firm with \(1\lt q\le1+r\) is not growing. The threshold "q = 1" is right for the ex-dividend q, and in continuous time.

  8. A permanent rise in the interest rate lowers both the growth rate of capital and Tobin's q. exam-inspired (slide 50)
    Answer

    True. From \(A=a(1+\gamma)+a'(1+\gamma)(r-\gamma)\): \(d\gamma/dr=-a'/[a''(r-\gamma)]\lt0\), using \(r>\gamma\) and \(a'>0\). For q: \(q=\max_g\frac{A-a(g)}{1-\beta g}\), and by the envelope theorem \(\partial q/\partial\beta=\bar g[A-a(\bar g)]/(1-\beta\bar g)^2>0\), so a lower \(\beta\) (higher \(r\)) lowers q. Equivalently, the ex-dividend \(q^{\text{ex}}=a'(\bar g)\) falls with \(\bar g\).

  9. In the Hayashi model, the co-state on capital (marginal q) equals the marginal product of capital. Past exam: 2010/11 Final Q1, part 7 (a commonly circulated answer)
    Answer

    False. Marginal q is the marginal value of installed capital, \(V'(k)\): the present value of everything an extra unit contributes. Here that is \(A\) per period plus the adjustment cost it saves, discounted with growth. The marginal product is \(A\). A commonly circulated answer key makes this mistake. The same key calls \(V(k)/k\) "average productivity", when it is average q, the market-to-book ratio.

  10. If \(f\) has decreasing returns, \(f(k)=Ak^\alpha\) with \(\alpha\lt1\), and the adjustment cost is \(a(k'/k)k\), then average q is at least as large as marginal q. exam-inspired (Hayashi 1982)
    Answer

    True. \(F\) is jointly concave and \(\Gamma\) is convex, so \(V\) is concave. With \(V(0)\ge0\), concavity gives \(V(0)\le V(k)-V'(k)k\), i.e. \(V(k)/k\ge V'(k)+V(0)/k\ge V'(k)\). Decreasing returns create rents on inframarginal capital, so the market-to-book ratio overstates the value of the marginal unit, and regressions of investment on average q are misspecified.

Hint

For (a), substitute \(i=y-(1-\delta)x\). For (d), \(k^*\) jumps when \(r\) changes, and only \(k_1\) is free. For (e), the Euler equation no longer contains any endogenous variable.

Show solution

(e) The EE becomes \(\beta(A+1-\delta)=1\). If \(A-\delta>r\), no path satisfies it. If \(A-\delta=r\), every feasible path with \(\beta^tk_t\to0\) gives the same value \((1+r)k_0\) (the sum telescopes, because \(\beta^t(1+r)=\beta^{t-1}\)), so the solution is indeterminate.

Average q exceeds marginal q by the rents on inframarginal units: early units of capital have a high marginal product, and the firm's value includes those rents, but the marginal unit earns less.
(d) Investment responds to marginal q, while the stock market measures average q. With decreasing returns (or market power) the two differ, so average q is a noisy, biased proxy. This is one explanation for the weak empirical fit of q-regressions. Hayashi's conditions (constant returns in production and installation, price taking) are what make the observable ratio the right regressor.

Key takeaways

  • Without adjustment costs and with concave \(f\), capital jumps to \(f'(k^*)=r+\delta\) in one period. With linear \(f\), there is no interior solution at all. Convex adjustment costs are what make investment smooth and determinate.
  • With \(f=Ak\) and cost \(a(k'/k)k\), \(F\) is HD1, the policy is \(k'=\bar gk\), and the Euler equation is the single equation \(\beta A=\beta a(\bar g)+a'(\bar g)(1-\beta\bar g)\). The four assumptions put the unique root in \((1,1/\beta)\), so \(0\lt\gamma\lt r\), and transversality is \(\beta\bar g\lt1\).
  • Read the Euler equation as "marginal installation cost = PV of dividends": \(a'(\bar g)=\beta\frac{A-a(\bar g)}{1-\beta\bar g}\). Growth rises with \(A\) and falls with \(r\): \(d\gamma/dr=-a'/[a''(r-\gamma)]\).
  • Pointwise higher costs need not lower growth. The ordering that matters is on \(\beta a+a'(1-\beta g)\), for example \(\tilde a-a\ge0\) and non-decreasing.
  • \(V(k)=\frac{A-a(\bar g)}{1-\beta\bar g}k\) is linear, so marginal q = average q (Hayashi), and \(q=V(k)/k=a'(\bar g)/\beta=(1+r)a'(\bar g)\). The no-growth benchmark is \(q=1+r\) cum-dividend, or \(q=1\) ex-dividend.
  • q is a sufficient statistic for investment: \(\bar g=a'^{-1}(\beta q)\).
