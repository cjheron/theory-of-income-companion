Stress-Testing the First Welfare Theorem

Covers Slides 1, pp. 19–23 (all the "Question." slides) · About 40 min · Prerequisite: Lesson 4
By the end you can:
  1. for any proposed modification of the economy, say whether the 1WT survives, and point to the exact line of the proof that does or doesn't break;
  2. tell apart assumptions the 1WT uses (LNS, price-taking, complete markets, no externalities, finitely-valued aggregate) from ones it doesn't use (convexity, continuity, common beliefs);
  3. solve a textbook externality example: compute the CE, the efficient allocation, the deadweight loss and the corrective tax.
Primary source Alvarez, Slides 1, pp. 19–23. For externalities and market power: Mas-Colell, Whinston & Green, ch. 11 (externalities) and §16.C (the 1WT and its assumptions). The OLG failure of the 1WT, which comes next in the course, is in Shell, "Notes on the Economics of Infinity", JPE 1971.

1. The method: audit the proof line by line

The 1WT proof from Lesson 4 has four moves. Each one uses a specific feature of the environment. To decide whether some modification breaks the theorem, ask which move it attacks.

MoveWhat it saysWhat it uses
(1)\(u^i(x^i)\ge u^i(\bar x^i)\Rightarrow p x^i\ge p\bar x^i\)LNS; \(\bar x^i\) maximizes \(u^i\) on the budget set; \(u^i\) depends only on \(x^i\)
(2)\(u^{i'}(x^{i'}) > u^{i'}(\bar x^{i'})\Rightarrow p x^{i'} > p\bar x^{i'}\)Utility maximization; \(u^{i'}\) depends only on \(x^{i'}\)
(3)Sum (1)–(2): \(p\sum_i x^i > p\sum_i\bar x^i\)Linearity of \(p\cdot\); finite sums (Lesson 12!)
(4)\(p\sum_j\bar y^j\ge p\sum_j y^j\)Each firm maximizes profit taking \(p\) as given; \(Y^j\) doesn't depend on other firms
(5)Feasibility of both allocations, valued at \(p\), contradicts (3)–(4)Every commodity that matters has a price, i.e. complete markets
Intuition: what the 1WT really needs

The proof never uses convexity, continuity, differentiability, or the number of firms. It uses one fact about individuals: anything you'd like at least as much as what you have costs at least as much (moves 1–2). It also uses one fact about the system: every effect of anyone's action on anyone else goes through a price (moves 4–5). Externalities, market power and missing markets all break the second fact. Satiation breaks the first.

2. Local non-satiation, revisited S1 p.19

Recall LNS: for every \(x\in X^i\) and \(\varepsilon>0\), there is \(\hat x\in B_\varepsilon(x)\cap X^i\) with \(u^i(\hat x)>u^i(x)\) (Lesson 3). Throughout, take \(X^i=\mathbb{R}^m_+\). Slide 19 asks four questions. The first three test whether you know what LNS is implied by; the fourth tests whether you know what the 1WT doesn't need.

If \(u\) is…LNS?Why
continuous, strictly increasingYes\(\hat x = x + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2\sqrt m}\mathbf 1\) is within \(\varepsilon\) of \(x\), is in \(\mathbb{R}^m_+\), and is strictly larger, so \(u(\hat x)>u(x)\).
continuous, (weakly) increasingNot necessarilyA constant \(u\) is continuous and weakly increasing but satiated everywhere. So is \(u(x)=\min\{x_1+x_2,\,1\}\) on the region \(x_1+x_2\ge 1\).
strictly increasing, not continuousYesThe same \(\hat x\) works. The argument never used continuity. Continuity is irrelevant to LNS.
convex (instead of concave)IrrelevantThe 1WT uses only LNS. A convex \(u\) such as \(x_1^2+x_2^2\) is strictly increasing on \(\mathbb{R}^2_+\), so LNS holds and any CE is PO. Convexity affects existence of a CE (demand can jump to corners) and the 2WT, not the 1WT.

What goes wrong without LNS? Move (1) fails: a household at a "thick" part of its indifference map can have a weakly preferred bundle that is strictly cheaper. The figure shows the simplest case.

Satiation breaks move (1). Edgeworth box with total endowment \((1,1)\). A has \(u^A=\min\{x_1+x_2,1\}\), so A is fully satisfied anywhere in the shaded region \(x_1+x_2\ge 1\). B has \(u^B=\log x_1^B+\log x_2^B\). With endowment \(e^A=(0.8,0.8)\) and \(p=(1,1)\), no-trade is a CE: B demands its own endowment, and A's endowment is one of A's optimal bundles. But moving to \(x^A=(0.5,0.5)\) leaves A indifferent and makes B strictly better off. The CE is not PO, and A's new bundle is weakly preferred yet strictly cheaper.

3. Technology: increasing returns and a single firm S1 p.20

Increasing returns to scale

The 1WT still holds: if a CE exists, it is PO. Move (4) uses only that \(\bar y^j\) maximizes \(p\cdot y\) over \(Y^j\). It never asks \(Y^j\) to be convex. The bite of increasing returns is elsewhere: a CE may not exist. With \(Y=\{(-n,c): c\le n^\gamma\}\), \(\gamma>1\), the profit \(c - wn = n^\gamma - wn\) is unbounded in \(n\) at any \(w>0\) (if \(n\) can grow without bound), so there is no profit-maximizing plan. The only candidate CE has no production at all, and that one usually fails market clearing. Use the slider below to see how the profit function changes shape.

Interactive. Profit \(\pi(n)=n^\gamma - wn\) as a function of labour input for a price-taking firm. With \(\gamma<1\) (decreasing returns), profit has an interior maximum: there is a well-defined supply. With \(\gamma=1\), profit is linear: it is zero for every \(n\) if \(w=1\), and otherwise the firm wants \(n=0\) or \(n=\infty\). With \(\gamma>1\), profit eventually rises without bound, so no profit-maximizing plan exists and no CE has positive output. The 1WT is not violated; it simply has nothing to talk about.

Literally one firm

If the single firm takes prices as given, the 1WT holds. Nothing in the proof counts firms: \(J=\{1\}\) is a perfectly good index set. The theorem fails when the single firm exercises market power, for instance a monopolist choosing output knowing that its price falls as it sells more. Then the firm sets marginal revenue = marginal cost, so price exceeds marginal cost. Consumers equate their MRS to the price, the technology's MRT equals marginal cost, and MRS ≠ MRT. A small increase in output is a Pareto improvement. But that outcome is not a competitive equilibrium in the sense of Lesson 2, because the firm is not solving \(\max_{y\in Y}p\cdot y\) at given \(p\). The real lesson: price-taking is a behavioural assumption, not a statement about the number of firms. Large numbers are one justification for it; contestability and Bertrand competition are others.

4. Externalities S1 p.21

Consumption externalities: moves (1)–(2) break

Suppose \(u^{i'}(x^{i'},x^i)\): agent \(i'\) cares about \(i\)'s consumption, but in a CE takes \(\bar x^i\) as given. Then utility maximization only tells us $$u^{i'}(x,\bar x^i) > u^{i'}(\bar x^{i'},\bar x^i)\ \Rightarrow\ p x > p\bar x^{i'} .$$ A Pareto-dominating allocation changes both \(x^{i'}\) and \(x^i\). Then \(u^{i'}(x^{i'},x^{i})\ge u^{i'}(\bar x^{i'},\bar x^i)\) says nothing about \(u^{i'}(x^{i'},\bar x^{i})\), which is what the budget argument needs. Agent \(i'\) may be happier only because \(i\) changed behaviour, even though \(x^{i'}\) itself is cheap. So move (1) fails and the 1WT generally fails.

Production externalities: move (4) breaks

Suppose firm \(j'\)'s technology is \(Y^{j'}(y^j)\): an upstream factory's output pollutes a downstream fishery. In a CE, firm \(j'\) maximizes over \(Y^{j'}(\bar y^j)\). In the dominating allocation, \(y^{j'}\in Y^{j'}(y^j)\), a different set, so \(p\bar y^{j'}\ge p y^{j'}\) need not hold. Move (4) fails, and the 1WT generally fails. In both cases the root problem is the same: there is no market, and hence no price, for the side effect. Read this way, an externality is a missing market (see §5).

A worked example you can play with

Agent 1 smokes: \(u^1 = \log s + m^1\), where \(s\) is cigarettes and \(m\) is money (the numeraire). Agent 2 doesn't smoke and dislikes smoke: \(u^2 = m^2 - \gamma s\). Cigarettes are produced at constant cost: one unit of \(m\) makes one unit of \(s\), so the CE price of \(s\) is \(1\).

  • CE. Agent 1 solves \(\max_s \log s - s\), so \(1/s = 1\) and \(s^{CE}=1\).
  • PO. With quasi-linear utility, PO allocations (with interior \(m\)) maximize total surplus \(\log s - s - \gamma s\), so \(1/s = 1+\gamma\) and \(s^{PO}=1/(1+\gamma)\).
  • The Pareto improvement. Cut \(s\) from \(1\) to \(1/(1+\gamma)\) and have agent 2 compensate agent 1 in money. Total surplus rises by the shaded triangle, \(\gamma - \log(1+\gamma) > 0\), so there is room to make both strictly better off.
  • The fix. A Pigouvian tax \(t=\gamma\) per cigarette, with revenue rebated lump-sum, makes agent 1 face the social cost \(1+\gamma\). That restores efficiency. Equivalently, it creates the missing "price of smoke".
Interactive. Marginal benefit to the smoker \(1/s\) (blue), private marginal cost \(1\) (black) and social marginal cost \(1+\gamma\) (orange). The CE is where blue meets black; the PO is where blue meets orange. The shaded area is the surplus lost in the CE. Raise \(\gamma\) and watch it grow; at \(\gamma=0\) the two allocations coincide.

5. Markets, beliefs, and what agents must agree on S1 p.22

Incomplete markets

Three goods but markets for only two. Each agent consumes its endowment of the untraded good 3 and trades goods 1–2. Relative to the unconstrained notion of PO, the 1WT fails. Move (5) values feasibility using a price for every good, but good 3 has none, so agents' marginal rates of substitution involving good 3 are never equalized. Relative to constrained efficiency, it holds. Constrained efficiency is the planner who must also leave good 3 where it is. Fix \(x_3^i = e_3^i\) and define \(\tilde u^i(x_1,x_2)=u^i(x_1,x_2,e^i_3)\). The CE is then a CE of a complete-markets two-good economy, and the 1WT applies to it. So the answer to "what is the notion of efficiency?" is: the benchmark must respect the same trading restrictions the agents face. (Warning for later: with several goods per date or state, even constrained efficiency typically fails, because prices on the open markets change how valuable the incomplete trades are. That is Geanakoplos–Polemarchakis 1986.)

Beliefs

The 1WT does not require agents to agree, nor to be "right". With commodities indexed by states of nature, \(u^i(x)=\sum_s\pi^i_s\,v^i(x_s)\) can embed any subjective probabilities \(\pi^i\). The proof never looks inside \(u^i\). So a CE is PO with respect to the agents' own beliefs, which is ex ante efficiency. If beliefs differ, the CE involves betting (Exercise 5.6), and at most one person's beliefs can be correct. A planner who uses "true" probabilities might call the outcome excessively risky, but that is a different welfare criterion.

What must agents agree on?

They must agree on the commodity space and the prices. Everyone has to recognise the same list of commodities: the same dates, the same set of possible states, and states that are publicly verifiable, so that a contract "deliver 1 unit if state \(s\)" can be enforced. And everyone faces the same price for each commodity. Agents need not agree on how likely the states are.

The intertemporal budget constraint

With dated commodities there is a single budget constraint. All trades for all dates (and states) are contracted at date 0 at prices \(p_t\), and then simply executed. That is the Arrow–Debreu interpretation. It is equivalent to a sequence of budget constraints with borrowing and lending at interest rates \(1+r_t = p_t/p_{t+1}\), plus a limit on borrowing (a no-Ponzi condition). Lesson 9 makes this concrete in the OLG model, and we'll see that what is "summable" turns out to matter.

6. Frictions, money and nominal contracts S1 p.23

  • Frictions (finding jobs, houses). Some can be folded into the technology: a "matching technology" that uses search effort as an input. If there is a competitive price for every relevant margin (e.g. competitive search, where firms post wages and workers direct their search), efficiency can survive. With random matching and bargaining, it typically fails because of congestion externalities: one more searcher makes it harder for others. Efficiency obtains only on a knife-edge (the Hosios condition). This is the subject of the DMP lectures later in the course.
  • How are transactions carried out? The unit of account. The model is silent about this. Trades happen at given prices in an abstract clearing house. The unit of account is just the numeraire. Rescaling all prices changes nothing (Lesson 2), so the unit of account has no real importance.
  • Money. Intrinsically useless fiat money cannot have positive value in a CE of a finite economy. At the last date nobody will accept it, so by backward induction nobody accepts it at any date. A role for money needs a deviation from the Arrow–Debreu structure: missing markets, a cash-in-advance constraint, or infinitely many generations. In the OLG model (Part II), valued money exists and can improve on an inefficient CE. Meanwhile, in cash-in-advance models inflation acts as a distorting tax (the Friedman rule).
  • Nominal contracts. If contracts are written in units of account and the price level then moves unexpectedly, allocations change for reasons unrelated to preferences or technology. Examples are nominal debt redistributing between borrowers and lenders, and sticky nominal wages. With complete, contingent, indexable contracts this cannot matter. The inefficiency comes from the contracting restriction, which is again a form of missing markets.

7. Check your understanding

1. A CE exists in an economy where some firm has increasing returns. Is the CE allocation PO (assuming LNS)?
  • No, because the proof requires a convex production set
  • Yes, because the proof only uses that firms maximize profits
  • No, because profits are infinite whenever returns increase
  • Yes, but only if there are many firms with that technology
Move (4) needs only \(p\bar y^j\ge p y\) for all \(y\in Y^j\). Increasing returns endanger existence of a CE, not its efficiency. Convexity of \(Y\) is a 2WT assumption (Assumption FF, Lesson 6).
2. Which step of the 1WT proof breaks with a consumption externality?
  • The profit-maximization inequality summed over all firms
  • The valuation of feasibility, since some good has no price
  • The final sum, because there are infinitely many households
  • The revealed-preference step: preferred implies more costly
Utility maximization at the CE compares bundles holding others' consumption at its CE level. A dominating allocation changes others' consumption too, so "weakly preferred" no longer implies "weakly more expensive".
3. Agents hold different subjective probabilities over states. The CE allocation is…
  • PO with respect to each agent's own subjective beliefs
  • PO only if the beliefs coincide with true probabilities
  • Never PO, because agents make bets against each other
  • PO only after the state of nature has been realised
Beliefs are part of \(u^i\), and the proof never looks inside \(u^i\). The CE is ex ante efficient by the agents' own lights. It need not be efficient ex post, and it need not be efficient under some "true" probability measure.
4. A single monopolist sets its price above marginal cost. Why does the 1WT not apply?
  • Because a single firm makes the production set nonconvex
  • Because consumer preferences then fail local non-satiation
  • Because the outcome is not a competitive equilibrium at all
  • Because Walras' law fails when firms earn positive profits
The 1WT is a statement about CE allocations. A price-setting monopolist doesn't solve \(\max_{y\in Y}p\cdot y\) at given \(p\), so its outcome isn't a CE. A single price-taking firm is fine.

8. Exercises

These follow the format of Alvarez's exams. True/false items like these were about half the points on recent first midterms, and the derivations are broken into short sub-steps. Answer each sub-part in a few lines before opening the solution.

Exercise 5.1 Past exams True or false? Justify.

For each statement, say true or false and justify it in one to four lines. The quickest justification names the step of the 1WT proof (§1) that does or does not go through.

  1. "If there are increasing returns to scale, a competitive equilibrium cannot be efficient." Fall 2025 Midterm 1, I.2
    Show answer

    False. Increasing returns make the production set non-convex. The 1WT never uses convexity: its moves need only LNS, profit maximization at \(p\), and feasibility. So if a CE exists, it is PO. Non-convexity breaks the 2WT (Lesson 7), and it can make a price-taking CE fail to exist, but neither of those is inefficiency of a CE. This is the classic Midterm 1 trap.

  2. "The First Welfare Theorem requires as an assumption that all utility functions be quasi-concave." Fall 2005 Midterm, Ex. 1 Q1
    Show answer

    False. The only assumption on preferences is local non-satiation (moves 1–2). Quasi-concavity (convex upper contour sets) is a hypothesis of the 2WT and of existence theorems.

  3. "To apply the FWT, check four things: complete markets, no externalities, LNS, and convex preferences and technologies." a checklist from a student review sheet; it contains an error
    Show answer

    False as a list of requirements: convexity does not belong on it (see item 2). The correct list is: a CE with complete markets (a price for every commodity), no externalities, LNS, and a finite value of aggregate consumption \(p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i\lt \infty\). The last condition is automatic with finitely many goods and agents. It is exactly what fails in OLG (Lesson 12).

  4. "In a security-market economy, if the number of securities is smaller than the number of states, the equilibrium allocation is necessarily inefficient." Fall 2005 Midterm, Ex. 1 Q3
    Show answer

    False. Incomplete markets make inefficiency possible, not necessary. Counterexample: endowments that are already PO (e.g. no aggregate risk, constant individual endowments, common beliefs). No trade is needed, so the no-trade equilibrium is PO even with zero securities. More generally, the outcome is efficient whenever the available securities happen to span the trades needed.

  5. "Consider one good indexed by the state of nature. If all agents have expected utility and are risk averse, but their subjective probabilities differ, then in a complete-markets CE each agent's consumption is a function only of the aggregate endowment." Fall 2005 Midterm, Ex. 1 Q2
    Show answer

    False. Efficiency requires \(\dfrac{u_i'(c_i(s))}{u_j'(c_j(s))}=\dfrac{\alpha_j\pi_j(s)}{\alpha_i\pi_i(s)}\). With common beliefs the right side is constant across states, so \(c_i\) depends on \(s\) only through \(Y(s)\). With different beliefs the likelihood ratio varies across states, so two states with the same \(Y\) give different \(c_i\): agents bet on their beliefs. Exercise 5.6 derives this.

  6. "If every household satisfies LNS, a CE allocation is PO even when one household's utility depends on another household's consumption." exam-inspired (S1 p.21)
    Show answer

    False. With a consumption externality, move 1 compares \(u^{i'}(x^{i'},x^i)\) with \(u^{i'}(\bar x^{i'},\bar x^i)\). The household optimized only over its own \(x^{i'}\) holding \(\bar x^i\) fixed. A dominating allocation can make \(i'\) happier by changing \(x^i\) while \(x^{i'}\) stays cheap, so "weakly better ⇒ weakly more expensive" fails.

  7. "If a CE allocation is Pareto optimal, preferences must satisfy LNS." exam-inspired
    Show answer

    False. LNS is sufficient, not necessary. For example, two agents who are both at their bliss points at the endowment form a no-trade CE that is trivially PO.

  8. "Lump-sum taxes in a CE break the 1WT, because they change agents' budget sets." exam-inspired
    Show answer

    False. A lump-sum transfer changes the right-hand side \(p\cdot e^i\) of the budget but not the prices anyone faces. Moves 1–2 use only that each agent optimizes on its own budget set at the common \(p\). Summing still gives the contradiction, because the transfers sum to zero. That is why the 2WT pairs prices with lump-sum transfers.

  9. "A proportional tax on labour income, with the revenue rebated lump-sum, breaks the 1WT." exam-inspired
    Show answer

    True (generically). Households face the wage \((1-t)w\) while firms face \(w\). So there is no single price vector at which everyone optimizes, and move 5 has no common valuation. Concretely, the MRS between leisure and consumption equals \((1-t)w\) while the MRT equals \(w\), and shifting a little time toward work is a Pareto improvement.

  10. "If good \(k\) is produced by only one firm, the 1WT does not apply." S1 p.20
    Show answer

    False as stated. The proof sums over \(j\in J\) and never uses \(|J|\). What it needs is that the firm takes \(p\) as given and maximizes \(p\cdot y\). A monopolist that internalizes its price impact is not behaving as in a CE, so the theorem says nothing about that outcome. But a single price-taking firm is fine.

Exercise 5.2 Slides p.19 Proof Which properties imply LNS?

Let \(X=\mathbb{R}^m_+\). Prove or give a counterexample.

  1. Continuous and strictly increasing \(\Rightarrow\) LNS. (10 points)
  2. Continuous and (weakly) increasing \(\Rightarrow\) LNS. (10 points)
  3. Strictly increasing, not necessarily continuous \(\Rightarrow\) LNS. (10 points)
  4. If every \(u^i\) is convex rather than concave, a CE (if one exists) is still PO. (10 points)
Hint

For (a) and (c), try \(\hat x = x+\delta\mathbf{1}\). What is \(\|\hat x - x\|\)? For (b), look for the laziest possible weakly increasing function. On an exam, state which definition of "increasing" you are using.

Show solution

(a) True. Fix \(x\) and \(\varepsilon>0\), and let \(\hat x = x + \delta\mathbf 1\) with \(\delta=\varepsilon/(2\sqrt m)\). Then \(\|\hat x - x\| = \delta\sqrt m = \varepsilon/2\lt \varepsilon\), \(\hat x\in\mathbb{R}^m_+\), and \(\hat x\gg x\), so strict monotonicity gives \(u(\hat x)>u(x)\).
(b) False. \(u\equiv 0\) is continuous and weakly increasing, and no bundle is ever strictly better. Less trivially, \(u=\min\{x_1+x_2,1\}\) is satiated on \(\{x_1+x_2\ge1\}\). (If "increasing" means \(x\gg x'\Rightarrow u(x)>u(x')\), the argument of (a) applies and the answer is true.)
(c) True. The proof of (a) never used continuity.
(d) True, provided the convex \(u^i\) satisfy LNS (e.g. \(u=x_1^2+x_2^2\), which is strictly increasing on \(\mathbb{R}^2_+\)), because the 1WT uses only LNS. Caveat: with convex \(u\), demands jump to corners, so a CE is less likely to exist, and the 2WT fails (Lesson 7).

Exercise 5.3 Exam-inspired Increasing returns: existence, not efficiency

This is the derivation behind statement 1 of Exercise 5.1. One consumption good \(c\) and labour \(n\) (price of \(c\) normalized to 1, wage \(w>0\)). A single price-taking firm has \(Y=\{(-n,c): 0\le c\le n^\gamma,\ n\ge0\}\) with \(\gamma>1\).

  1. Show that \(Y\) is not convex. (5 points)
  2. Show that if \(n\) is unbounded, the firm has no profit-maximizing plan at any \(w>0\). (5 points)
  3. Now let \(n\in[0,1]\) (one unit of time). Show that profit \(\pi(n)=n^\gamma-wn\) is maximized only at a corner, and find the firm's supply correspondence as a function of \(w\). (10 points)
  4. Explain in one line why market clearing for labour is then hard to achieve, so a CE may not exist. (5 points)
  5. Suppose nevertheless that a CE exists. Which lines of the 1WT proof use convexity of \(Y\)? Conclude. (5 points)
Show solution

(a) \((-1,1)\in Y\) and \((0,0)\in Y\), but their midpoint \((-\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\) requires \(\tfrac12\le(\tfrac12)^\gamma\), which fails for \(\gamma>1\).
(b) \(\pi(n)=n^\gamma-wn\to\infty\) as \(n\to\infty\) because \(\gamma>1\). There is no maximizer.
(c) \(\pi''(n)=\gamma(\gamma-1)n^{\gamma-2}>0\), so \(\pi\) is strictly convex on \([0,1]\). Its maximum is at an endpoint: \(\pi(0)=0\) or \(\pi(1)=1-w\). Supply is \(n=1\) if \(w<1\), \(n\in\{0,1\}\) if \(w=1\), and \(n=0\) if \(w>1\). The interior FOC point \(n=(w/\gamma)^{1/(\gamma-1)}\) is a profit minimum.
(d) Labour demand jumps between 0 and 1. If households want to supply some interior amount, no wage clears the market.
(e) None. Moves 4–5 use only \(p\cdot\bar y^j\ge p\cdot y^j\) for all \(y^j\in Y^j\) and feasibility. So any CE that exists is PO. IRS is a problem for existence and for the 2WT, not for the 1WT.

Exercise 5.4 Slides p.21 Exam-inspired A consumption externality

Use the smoking economy of §4 with a general \(\gamma>0\): \(u^1=\log s+m^1\), \(u^2=m^2-\gamma s\), and one unit of money makes one cigarette.

  1. Find \(s^{CE}\) and the Pareto-optimal \(s^{PO}\). Explain why PO allocations maximize total surplus here. (10 points)
  2. Show that moving from \(s^{CE}\) to \(s^{PO}\) raises total surplus by \(\Delta(\gamma)=\gamma-\log(1+\gamma)\), and prove \(\Delta(\gamma)>0\) for every \(\gamma>0\). (10 points)
  3. Find the interval of money transfers \(T\) from agent 2 to agent 1 under which both are strictly better off at \(s^{PO}\) than at the CE. (10 points)
  4. Show that a per-unit tax \(t\) on cigarettes, rebated lump-sum, implements \(s^{PO}\) iff \(t=\gamma\). Which "missing price" does the tax create? (5 points)
  5. Rewrite the economy with a production externality: a factory produces \(q\) at cost \(q^2/2\) and sells at price 1, and each unit lowers a fishery's catch by \(\gamma\in(0,1)\) units worth 1 each. Find \(q^{CE}\) and \(q^{PO}\), and name the line of the 1WT proof that fails. (10 points)
Show solution

(a) Agent 1 maximizes \(\log s-s\), so \(s^{CE}=1\). With quasi-linear utility, transferring money moves utility one-for-one between agents. So the utility possibility frontier is a line, and every PO allocation (with interior money) maximizes the sum \(\log s - s-\gamma s\). The FOC \(1/s=1+\gamma\) gives \(s^{PO}=1/(1+\gamma)\).
(b) Surplus \(W(s)=\log s-(1+\gamma)s\). Then \(W(s^{PO})-W(1)=-\log(1+\gamma)-1+(1+\gamma)=\gamma-\log(1+\gamma)\). Since \(\Delta(0)=0\) and \(\Delta'(\gamma)=1-\tfrac{1}{1+\gamma}>0\) for \(\gamma>0\), we get \(\Delta>0\).
(c) Agent 1's private surplus \(\log s-s\) falls by \(L=(0-1)-\big(-\log(1+\gamma)-\tfrac{1}{1+\gamma}\big)=\log(1+\gamma)+\tfrac{1}{1+\gamma}-1\ge0\). Agent 2 gains \(G=\gamma(1-\tfrac1{1+\gamma})=\tfrac{\gamma^2}{1+\gamma}\). Both strictly gain iff \(L\lt T\lt G\). The interval is non-empty because \(G-L=\Delta(\gamma)>0\).
(d) With tax \(t\), agent 1 solves \(\max\log s-(1+t)s\), so \(s=1/(1+t)\). This equals \(s^{PO}\) iff \(t=\gamma\). The tax is the price of smoke that the market was missing: agent 1 now pays the marginal damage \(\gamma\).
(e) The factory maximizes \(q-q^2/2\), so \(q^{CE}=1\). Efficiency maximizes \(q-q^2/2-\gamma q\), so \(q^{PO}=1-\gamma\). Move 4 fails: the fishery's production set depends on \(q\). At the CE it optimized over \(Y^{\text{fish}}(\bar q)\), but in the dominating allocation it operates on \(Y^{\text{fish}}(q)\), a different set, so \(p\cdot\bar y^{\text{fish}}\ge p\cdot y^{\text{fish}}\) need not hold.

Exercise 5.5 Slides p.22 Problem set 2022 (PS3 P1, Ex. 9 & 11) Incomplete markets

Two agents with \(u^i=\log x_1+\log x_2+\log x_3\). Endowments are \(e^A=(1,1,a)\) and \(e^B=(1,1,b)\) with \(a\ne b\), \(a,b>0\). Only goods 1 and 2 can be traded.

  1. Find the CE prices of goods 1–2 and the allocation. (10 points)
  2. Compute each agent's MRS between goods 3 and 1 at the CE. Use them to construct an explicit Pareto improvement. (10 points)
  3. Show that the CE is constrained PO: no reallocation that leaves good 3 where it is can be a Pareto improvement. (10 points)
  4. General principle (problem set): with the FOC \(q_k=\sum_s d_{ks}\eta^i_s\), where \(\eta^i_s=\frac1{\mu^i}\partial u^i/\partial x_s\), show that a PO interior allocation must have \(\eta^i\) equal across agents. Conclude that if the \(\eta^i\) differ, the allocation is not PO. (10 points)
Hint

For (d), use the weighted planner with weights \(\lambda^i=1/\mu^i\) and multipliers \(\gamma_s\) on each resource constraint.

Show solution

(a) Goods 1 and 2 enter symmetrically, so \(p_1=p_2\). Each agent spends half its tradeable wealth \(2p_1\) on each, giving \(x_1=x_2=1\). No trade: \(x^A=(1,1,a)\) and \(x^B=(1,1,b)\).
(b) \(MRS^i_{31}=x^i_1/x^i_3\), so \(MRS^A_{31}=1/a\) and \(MRS^B_{31}=1/b\). Say \(a>b\): then B values good 3 more. Let A give B \(\delta\) units of good 3 for \(\kappa\delta\) units of good 1, with \(1/a\lt \kappa<1/b\). To first order, A's utility changes by \(\delta(\kappa-1/a)>0\) and B's by \(\delta(1/b-\kappa)>0\). For small \(\delta\) both gain.
(c) Fix \(x^i_3=e^i_3\). Each agent's utility over goods 1–2 is \(\log x_1+\log x_2+\text{const}\), which satisfies LNS, and the CE is a CE of this complete two-good economy. By the 1WT it is PO in that economy, i.e. constrained PO.
(d) An interior PO allocation solves \(\max\sum_i\lambda^iu^i\) subject to resource constraints for some weights. FOC: \(\lambda^i\,\partial u^i/\partial x_s=\gamma_s\) for every \(i\). With \(\lambda^i=1/\mu^i\), this says \(\eta^i_s=\gamma_s\), the same for all \(i\). If the \(\eta^i\) differ, two agents value state-\(s\) goods differently at the margin. Moving goods from the low-\(\eta\) to the high-\(\eta\) agent and compensating with another good makes both better off. In (b), \(\eta\) for good 3 differs across A and B exactly because no market equalizes it.

Exercise 5.6 Past exam: Fall 2005 Midterm, Ex. 1 Q2 Beliefs and betting

One good, \(S\) states, aggregate endowment \(Y(s)>0\). Agent \(i\) has expected utility \(\sum_s\pi_i(s)\log c_i(s)\) with subjective probabilities \(\pi_i\). Markets are complete.

  1. Write the planner's problem with weights \(\alpha_i>0\) and its FOCs. (5 points)
  2. Solve for \(c_i(s)\) in closed form. (10 points)
  3. Show that if \(\pi_i=\pi\) for all \(i\), each \(c_i(s)\) depends on \(s\) only through \(Y(s)\). Show by example that this fails when beliefs differ. (10 points)
  4. Is the CE Pareto optimal? With respect to whose probabilities? (5 points)
  5. A planner evaluates welfare using the "true" probabilities \(\pi^*\). Can the CE be dominated under that criterion? What does this say about the slide's question "does the 1WT require that agents' view of the world be reasonable?" (10 points)
Show solution

(a) \(\max\sum_i\alpha_i\sum_s\pi_i(s)\log c_i(s)\) subject to \(\sum_ic_i(s)\le Y(s)\) for each \(s\). FOC: \(\alpha_i\pi_i(s)/c_i(s)=\mu(s)\).
(b) \(c_i(s)=\alpha_i\pi_i(s)/\mu(s)\). Summing over \(i\) gives \(\mu(s)=\sum_j\alpha_j\pi_j(s)/Y(s)\), so $$c_i(s)=\frac{\alpha_i\pi_i(s)}{\sum_j\alpha_j\pi_j(s)}\,Y(s).$$ (c) With common \(\pi\), the share \(\alpha_i/\sum_j\alpha_j\) is the same in every state, so \(c_i(s)=\text{share}_i\cdot Y(s)\). With two agents, \(\alpha_A=\alpha_B\), \(Y(1)=Y(2)=2\), \(\pi_A(1)=\pi_A\) and \(\pi_B(1)=1-\pi_A\ne\pi_A\), agent A gets \(c_A(1)=2\pi_A\ne c_A(2)=2(1-\pi_A)\) even though \(Y\) is the same in both states. The agents bet.
(d) Yes. Each \(u^i\) satisfies LNS and markets are complete, so the 1WT applies. Efficiency is with respect to each agent's own \(u^i\), which embeds \(\pi_i\).
(e) Yes, it can be. In the example of (c) with \(\pi^*(1)=\tfrac12\), the no-trade allocation \((1,1)\) gives both agents \(\tfrac12\log1+\tfrac12\log1=0\) under \(\pi^*\). The CE gives \(\tfrac12[\log2\pi_A+\log2(1-\pi_A)]<0\) whenever \(\pi_A\ne\tfrac12\), since \(4\pi_A(1-\pi_A)<1\). So under \(\pi^*\) the CE is dominated. The 1WT does not require beliefs to be correct; the efficiency it delivers is relative to the agents' own beliefs. Agents need only agree on the commodity space (which states exist and are verifiable) and face the same prices.

Exercise 5.7 Slides pp.22–23 Short answers

Answer each in at most three lines. (a) What must agents agree about in a classical CE? (b) Interpret the single intertemporal budget constraint. (c) Why can't intrinsically useless money have positive value in a finite Arrow–Debreu economy? (d) Give one friction that leads to inefficiency and one that need not.

Show solution

(a) The list of commodities (dates, locations, verifiable states) and the price of each. They need not agree on probabilities.
(b) All trades are contracted at date 0 at present-value prices \(p_t\). This is equivalent to sequential budget constraints with borrowing and lending at \(1+r_t=p_t/p_{t+1}\), plus a no-Ponzi limit.
(c) At the final date, money buys nothing in the future, so nobody gives up goods for it and its price is 0. At the date before, it can only buy something worth 0 later, so it is worth 0 then too. By induction it is worth 0 at every date.
(d) Random matching with bargaining generates congestion externalities and is inefficient except under the Hosios condition. Competitive (directed) search, with a price for every margin, can be efficient.

Key takeaways

  • The 1WT uses: LNS, price-taking optimization, utility and technology that depend only on one's own choices, a price for every relevant commodity, and finite sums.
  • It does not use convexity, continuity, the number of firms, or common or correct beliefs. Increasing returns threaten existence, not efficiency.
  • Externalities break moves (1)–(2) for consumption and move (4) for production. Both amount to a missing market, and a Pigouvian price restores efficiency.
  • With incomplete markets the CE is generally not PO, but in the simple fixed-endowment case it is constrained PO.
  • The one assumption we haven't stressed, that the value of the aggregate is finite, is what fails in the OLG model (Lesson 12).
