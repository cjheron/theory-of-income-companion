Euler Equations, Transversality, and Sufficiency

By the end you can:
  1. write the Euler equation (EE) and the transversality condition (TC) for any problem in \(F(x,y)\) form, and say in one sentence what each one means economically;
  2. derive the EE and TC of the neoclassical growth model, and rewrite its TC as "the discounted value of the capital carried forward goes to zero";

Notation for this lesson
  • \(F(x,y)\) is the period return (utility) from being at state \(x\) today and choosing state \(y\) for tomorrow. It is not a production function. \(F_x\) and \(F_y\) are its partial derivatives, which are vectors when \(m>1\). Products such as \(F_x\cdot x\) are inner products.
  • In the growth model, \(f(k)\equiv G(k,1)+(1-\delta)k\) is output plus undepreciated capital, so \(f'(k)=G_k(k,1)+1-\delta\) is the gross return on capital, \(1+r\). The golden rule is therefore \(f'(k)=1\), not \(f'(k)=0\).
  • \(\beta\in(0,1)\) is the discount factor, with \(\beta=1/(1+\rho)\). In Class Note II, \(\beta\) was the weight on old-age consumption (Lessons 8–13), and \(\alpha\) was the old-age endowment. Here \(\alpha\) will be the Cobb–Douglas capital share.
  • Starred variables \(\{x^*_t\}\) denote the candidate path that satisfies the EE and the TC; unstarred \(\{x_t\}\) is an arbitrary feasible alternative.

1. Where we are

Lesson 26 wrote every problem in this part of the course in one form EE p.3: $$V^*(x_0)=\max_{\{x_{t+1}\}_{t=0}^\infty}\ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\beta^tF(x_t,x_{t+1})\qquad\text{s.t. } x_{t+1}\in\Gamma(x_t)\ \text{ for all } t\ge0,\quad x_0\text{ given.}$$ This is an optimization over an infinite sequence. In a finite problem we would write first-order conditions and check second-order conditions. This lesson does the infinite-horizon version. There are first-order conditions for each date: the Euler equations. There is also a boundary condition at infinity, the transversality condition, that replaces the terminal condition of a finite problem. And there is a theorem saying that, under concavity, these conditions are enough. Lesson 28 does the converse (necessity).

2. The Euler equation and the transversality condition

Definition (EE and TC) EE p.11

Let \(X\subseteq\mathbb R^m\), \(F\in C^1\), \(\beta\in(0,1)\). A path \(\{x_{t+1}\}_{t=0}^\infty\) satisfies the Euler equations if $$F_y(x_t,x_{t+1})+\beta F_x(x_{t+1},x_{t+2})=0\qquad\text{for all }t\ge0, \tag{EE}$$ and the transversality condition if $$\lim_{t\to\infty}\beta^t\,F_x(x_t,x_{t+1})\cdot x_t=0. \tag{TC}$$

What the Euler equation says

The state \(x_{t+1}\) shows up in exactly two periods of the objective: as the choice in \(F(x_t,x_{t+1})\) and as the inherited state in \(F(x_{t+1},x_{t+2})\). Raise \(x_{t+1}\) by a small \(\varepsilon\) and leave every other date alone. Today's return changes by \(F_y(x_t,x_{t+1})\,\varepsilon\), which is typically negative: carrying more capital into tomorrow means eating less today. Tomorrow's return changes by \(F_x(x_{t+1},x_{t+2})\,\varepsilon\), worth \(\beta F_x\,\varepsilon\) in today's units. At an optimum, this one-period perturbation can't help in either direction, so $$\underbrace{-F_y(x_t,x_{t+1})}_{\text{marginal cost today}}=\underbrace{\beta F_x(x_{t+1},x_{t+2})}_{\text{discounted marginal benefit tomorrow}}.$$ This is exactly the "alternative (and simpler) proof" of Lesson 28 (slide 22). Note that it uses no value function and no envelope condition.

What the transversality condition says

\(F_x(x_t,x_{t+1})\) is the marginal return of one more unit of the state at date \(t\): its shadow price at date \(t\), in date-\(t\) utility. Multiply by \(\beta^t\) and it is in date-0 utility. So \(\beta^tF_x\cdot x_t\) is the present value of the whole stock held at \(t\), and the TC says it must vanish in the limit EE p.11. If it stayed positive, the plan would be piling up a stock that has positive value and is never used. Consuming a bit of it at some finite date would then be better: the plan leaves value on the table. Stokey–Lucas give the market reading: in a many-sector growth model, \(F_x\) is the vector of capital-goods prices, and the TC says the present value of the capital stock, at date-\(t\) prices, goes to zero. Lesson 29 builds a path that satisfies every Euler equation but violates the TC, and shows exactly how much value it wastes.

The EE is a system of \(m\) second-order difference equations in \(x_t\) (it involves \(x_t,x_{t+1},x_{t+2}\)). With \(x_0\) given, its solutions form an \(m\)-parameter family, one for each choice of \(x_1\). We need \(m\) more boundary conditions to pick one, and the TC supplies them, at \(t=\infty\). This is the logic of the shooting algorithm in Lesson 28.

General principle EE p.11

Under appropriate concavity, interiority, and regularity conditions, EE and TC are necessary and sufficient for \(\{x_{t+1}\}\) to be optimal.

The qualifier matters, and it is new in the 2026 deck: last year's slide stated the principle with no conditions. The two directions need different assumptions. Sufficiency (this lesson) needs concavity plus two sign conditions, and no interiority. Necessity (Lesson 28) needs interiority and differentiability but no concavity, and for the TC the slides' argument is only heuristic. Exams test exactly this distinction. For example, Fall 2005 asked whether concavity is needed for the necessity of the TC (it is not).

3. The neoclassical growth model (slide 11 exercise)

From Lesson 26: \(F(x,y)=U(f(x)-y)\) and \(\Gamma(x)=[0,f(x)]\), with \(c_t=f(k_t)-k_{t+1}\) EE p.4. The derivatives are $$F_x(k_t,k_{t+1})=U'(c_t)\,f'(k_t),\qquad F_y(k_t,k_{t+1})=-U'(c_t).$$

  1. Euler equation. Substitute into (EE): \(-U'(c_t)+\beta U'(c_{t+1})f'(k_{t+1})=0\), i.e. $$U'(c_t)=\beta\,U'(c_{t+1})\,f'(k_{t+1}),\qquad c_t=f(k_t)-k_{t+1}.$$ The utility cost of saving one more unit today equals the discounted utility value of the \(f'(k_{t+1})=1+r_{t+1}\) units it returns tomorrow. Written in terms of \(k\) alone it is a second-order difference equation: \(U'(f(k_t)-k_{t+1})=\beta U'(f(k_{t+1})-k_{t+2})f'(k_{t+1})\).
  2. Transversality. Substitute into (TC): $$\lim_{t\to\infty}\beta^tU'(c_t)\,f'(k_t)\,k_t=0.$$
  3. An equivalent form. Along a path that satisfies the EE, the EE at date \(t-1\) gives \(\beta^tF_x(k_t,k_{t+1})=-\beta^{t-1}F_y(k_{t-1},k_t)=\beta^{t-1}U'(c_{t-1})\). So the TC is the same as $$\lim_{t\to\infty}\beta^{t}\,U'(c_{t})\,k_{t+1}=0.$$ Read it this way: \(\beta^tU'(c_t)\) is the date-0 utility price of a unit of goods at \(t\), and \(k_{t+1}\) is what the plan sets aside at \(t\) instead of eating. The present value of the capital carried forward must vanish. This is the form you will meet in continuous time (\(\lim e^{-\rho t}\lambda(t)k(t)=0\)).
Two routes to the same Euler equation

You can also get the EE without substituting \(c\) out: write the Lagrangian of the sequence problem with a multiplier \(\beta^t\lambda_t\) on each period's law of motion, take FOCs in \(c_t\) (or \(i_t\)) and \(k_{t+1}\), and eliminate \(\lambda_t\). Fall 2005 asked for exactly this (Exercise 27.1), then asked for the \(F\)-route, then checked that the two agree. They must: the \(F\)-route just solves the law of motion for the control before differentiating. In the Lagrangian, \(\lambda_t\) is the shadow price of the state. The FOC for \(k_{t+1}\) says that price equals the discounted marginal product plus the discounted resale value of the undepreciated part. This is again the "\(F_x\) is a price" reading of the TC.

4. A tangent line lies above a concave function (slide 13)

The sufficiency proof rests on one inequality EE p.13: for a differentiable \(f:\mathbb R^K\to\mathbb R\), $$f\ \text{concave}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(x)\le f(x^0)+\nabla f(x^0)\cdot(x-x^0)\qquad\text{for all }x,x^0. \tag{T}$$ In words: the linear approximation at any point overestimates a concave function everywhere. The figure lets you check it, and shows what goes wrong without concavity.

Blue shading is where the tangent lies above \(f\) (the gap \(f(x^0)+f'(x^0)(x-x^0)-f(x)\ge0\)); orange shading is where it lies below. (2) Switch to "concave, with a flat part" and put \(x^0\) on the flat piece: the tangent coincides with \(f\) on a whole interval, so the inequality holds with equality there. (T) still holds, but strict concavity, and with it uniqueness (Lesson 28), is lost. (3) Switch to "not concave": for most \(x^0\), orange regions appear. That is exactly where Step 1 of the sufficiency proof would fail.

Three ways to see (T), in increasing generality:

  1. One dimension, by picture. A concave function's slope is non-increasing. To the right of \(x^0\) the function rises more slowly than the tangent does, and to the left it falls faster. Either way it ends up below the tangent.
  2. One dimension, by Taylor. If \(f\in C^2\), then \(f(x)=f(x^0)+f'(x^0)(x-x^0)+\tfrac12f''(\xi)(x-x^0)^2\) for some \(\xi\) between \(x\) and \(x^0\). Concavity means \(f''\le0\), so the remainder is \(\le0\).
  3. \(K\) dimensions, by Taylor. If \(f\in C^2\), then $$f(x)=f(x^0)+\nabla f(x^0)\cdot(x-x^0)+\tfrac12(x-x^0)'\,D^2f(\xi)\,(x-x^0)$$ for some \(\xi\) on the segment from \(x^0\) to \(x\). A \(C^2\) function is concave if and only if its Hessian \(D^2f\) is negative semidefinite everywhere, so the quadratic form is \(\le0\).
The slide says "negative definite". That is too strong.

Concavity is equivalent to a negative semidefinite Hessian. A negative definite Hessian is sufficient for strict concavity but not necessary: \(f(x)=-x^4\) is strictly concave yet \(f''(0)=0\). A linear function is concave with a zero Hessian. None of this matters for the inequality (semidefinite is all the Taylor argument needs), but it matters on exams that ask you to verify concavity of \(F(x,y)\). On the diagonal of an adjustment-cost problem, for instance, the Hessian is often only semidefinite (catalogued example: \(F=-ay^2-b(y-x)^4\) has \(F_{xx}=0\) and a zero determinant at \(x=y\), yet \(F\) is strictly concave).

You don't even need two derivatives. If \(f\) is concave and differentiable, then for \(\lambda\in(0,1)\), \(f(x^0+\lambda(x-x^0))\ge(1-\lambda)f(x^0)+\lambda f(x)\). Rearranging, \(\frac{f(x^0+\lambda(x-x^0))-f(x^0)}{\lambda}\ge f(x)-f(x^0)\), and letting \(\lambda\downarrow0\) the left side tends to \(\nabla f(x^0)\cdot(x-x^0)\). The converse also holds: a differentiable function satisfying (T) at every point is concave (Exercise 27.6).

5. Sufficiency: the theorem and its proof

Theorem (sufficiency of EE and TC) EE p.12

Assume (1) \(F\) is concave in \((x,y)\) (and \(C^1\)); (2) \(F_x(x^*_t,x^*_{t+1})\ge0\): the state is a "good"; (3) \(X=\mathbb R^m_+\): states are bounded below. If a feasible path \(\{x^*_{t+1}\}_{t=0}^\infty\) satisfies the EE and the TC, it is optimal.

Proof EE pp.14–17

Fix any feasible alternative \(\{x_{t+1}\}\) with the same initial condition \(x_0=x_0^*\), so \(x_t\ge0\) for all \(t\). Abbreviate \(F^*_x(t)\equiv F_x(x^*_t,x^*_{t+1})\) and \(F^*_y(t)\equiv F_y(x^*_t,x^*_{t+1})\), and let $$D_T\equiv\sum_{t=0}^T\beta^t\big[F(x_t,x_{t+1})-F(x^*_t,x^*_{t+1})\big].$$ We show that \(\lim_{T\to\infty}D_T\le0\): the alternative is no better.

  1. Linearize, using concavity [assumption 1]. Apply (T) to \(F\) at the point \((x^*_t,x^*_{t+1})\), evaluated at \((x_t,x_{t+1})\): $$F(x_t,x_{t+1})-F(x^*_t,x^*_{t+1})\le F^*_x(t)\cdot(x_t-x^*_t)+F^*_y(t)\cdot(x_{t+1}-x^*_{t+1}).$$ Multiply by \(\beta^t\ge0\) and sum: $$D_T\le\sum_{t=0}^T\beta^t\Big[F^*_x(t)\cdot(x_t-x^*_t)+F^*_y(t)\cdot(x_{t+1}-x^*_{t+1})\Big].$$
  2. Regroup by date of the state. Write the sum out EE p.15. The \(t=0\) term \(F^*_x(0)\cdot(x_0-x^*_0)\) is zero because the initial condition is common. Each deviation \(x_{s}-x^*_{s}\) with \(1\le s\le T\) then appears exactly twice: as tomorrow's state in the date-\((s-1)\) term (coefficient \(\beta^{s-1}F^*_y(s-1)\)) and as today's state in the date-\(s\) term (coefficient \(\beta^sF^*_x(s)\)). The last deviation, \(x_{T+1}-x^*_{T+1}\), appears only once. So $$D_T\le\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\beta^t\Big[F^*_y(t)+\beta F^*_x(t+1)\Big]\cdot(x_{t+1}-x^*_{t+1})\;+\;\beta^TF^*_y(T)\cdot(x_{T+1}-x^*_{T+1}).$$
  3. Use the Euler equations. Every bracket is \(F_y(x^*_t,x^*_{t+1})+\beta F_x(x^*_{t+1},x^*_{t+2})=0\) EE p.16. Only the terminal term survives: $$D_T\le\beta^TF^*_y(T)\cdot(x_{T+1}-x^*_{T+1}).$$
  4. Use the Euler equation once more, at \(T\). It says \(F^*_y(T)=-\beta F^*_x(T+1)\), so $$D_T\le-\beta^{T+1}F^*_x(T+1)\cdot x_{T+1}\;+\;\beta^{T+1}F^*_x(T+1)\cdot x^*_{T+1}.$$
  5. Drop the alternative's terminal stock [assumptions 2 and 3]. \(F^*_x(T+1)\ge0\) and \(x_{T+1}\ge0\) give \(F^*_x(T+1)\cdot x_{T+1}\ge0\), so the first term is \(\le0\) EE p.17: $$D_T\le\beta^{T+1}F_x(x^*_{T+1},x^*_{T+2})\cdot x^*_{T+1}\qquad\text{for every }T.$$ The bound no longer involves the alternative at all.
  6. Use the TC. The right side is the TC expression at date \(T+1\), which goes to 0. Hence \(\lim_TD_T\le0\) (or \(\limsup_TD_T\le0\), if the alternative's objective has no limit): no feasible path does better than \(\{x^*_t\}\). ∎

Two remarks that examiners like:

  • The TC is a condition on the candidate only. Step 5 removes the alternative from the bound, so you check \(\lim\beta^tF_x\cdot x^*_t=0\) along your proposed path, never along competitors. Stokey–Lucas note that the theorem places no restriction on \(F\) or \(\beta\) beyond these assumptions, because it applies only once a path satisfying EE and TC has been found. Showing that such a path exists is a separate problem.
  • What the proof does not use. It doesn't use interiority, strict concavity, \(F_y\lt0\), Inada conditions, or compactness of \(\Gamma\). Strict concavity buys uniqueness of the optimum (Lesson 28). Interiority is what makes the EE necessary.

6. Why each assumption is there

Each hypothesis is used in exactly one step, and each can fail in an economically meaningful way.

Without concavity: Step 1 fails

Take \(\Gamma(x)=[0,1]\) and \(F(x,y)=(y-\tfrac12)^2\), a convex return. The constant path \(x^*_{t+1}=\tfrac12\) satisfies every EE (\(F_y=2(y-\tfrac12)=0\) and \(F_x=0\)) and the TC (\(F_x\equiv0\)), and \(X=[0,1]\subset\mathbb R_+\). Yet it is the worst path: \(x_{t+1}=1\) gives \(\tfrac14\) every period instead of 0. The EE is a first-order condition, and without concavity first-order conditions can pick out minima and saddles.

Without a lower bound on the state: Step 5 fails

Consider the savings problem of Lesson 29: \(F(x,y)=U(w+(1+r)x-y)\) with \(\beta(1+r)=1\), where \(x\) is assets, and suppose any \(x\in\mathbb R\) is allowed. The candidate "consume \(c^*=w+rx_0\) forever" satisfies EE and TC. Now try \(\tilde c=c^*+\Delta\): borrow \(\Delta\) more each period and roll the debt over. Assets follow \(x_t=x_0-\Delta\frac{(1+r)^t-1}{r}\to-\infty\). Then \(\beta^{T+1}x_{T+1}\to-\Delta/r\), so the term dropped in Step 5, \(-\beta^{T+1}F_x\,x_{T+1}\), tends to \((1+r)U'(c^*)\Delta/r>0\). It is not \(\le0\), and indeed the alternative is strictly better: more consumption in every period. This is a Ponzi scheme, and it shows why problems with debt need a borrowing limit. With \(X=[x_{\text{low}},\infty)\), the proof goes through with the TC rewritten as \(\lim\beta^tF_x\cdot(x_t-x_{\text{low}})=0\) (Exercise 27.4). Lesson 29 runs the mirror-image argument: a path that saves too much satisfies the EE but fails the TC.

Without \(F_x\ge0\): Step 5 fails again

If the state were a "bad" (\(F_x\lt0\)), the dropped term \(-\beta^{T+1}F_x\cdot x_{T+1}\) would be positive and large for alternatives that end with a big stock, and nothing would bound it. Strictly, what Step 5 needs is \(F_x\ge0\) along the candidate path. A return with \(F_x\lt0\) elsewhere is harmless. Example: \(F=-(x-y)^2\) on \(\Gamma(x)=[0,1]\). Every constant path has \(F_x=-2(x-y)=0\) and is optimal by the theorem. Here every \(x\in[0,1]\) is a steady state, a continuum, just like the savings problem of slide 28.

7. Steady states (slide 18)

Definition (steady state) EE p.18

A steady state is an \(\bar x\) solving \(F_y(\bar x,\bar x)+\beta F_x(\bar x,\bar x)=0\): the EE with \(x_t=x_{t+1}=x_{t+2}=\bar x\).

Slide 18, first exercise: when is staying at \(\bar x\) optimal?

Claim. If \(F\) is concave, \(F_x(\bar x,\bar x)\ge0\), \(X\subseteq\mathbb R^m_+\) and \(\bar x\in\Gamma(\bar x)\), then starting from \(x_0=\bar x\) the constant path \(x_{t+1}=\bar x\) is optimal.

  1. EE. For every \(t\), the EE is evaluated at \((\bar x,\bar x)\) and \((\bar x,\bar x)\), so it holds by the definition of \(\bar x\).
  2. TC. \(\beta^tF_x(\bar x,\bar x)\cdot\bar x\) is a constant times \(\beta^t\), so it goes to 0. For a constant path the TC is automatic.
  3. Sufficiency. The theorem of §5 applies. ∎

So "for what kind of problems" has a precise answer: convex ones, where EE + TC are sufficient. In non-convex problems a root of the stationary EE need not be optimal: in the \(F=(y-\frac12)^2\) example of §6, \(\bar x=\tfrac12\) solves the steady-state equation and staying there is the worst thing you can do. Fall 2005 used this definition directly: "a steady state is \(x\) such that \(x_{t+1}=x\) is optimal when \(x_0=x\)". The claim above is what makes the two definitions coincide.

Slide 18, second exercise: the growth model

At \((\bar k,\bar k)\), \(\bar c=f(\bar k)-\bar k\) and $$F_y(\bar k,\bar k)+\beta F_x(\bar k,\bar k)=U'(\bar c)\big[-1+\beta f'(\bar k)\big]=0.$$ With \(U'>0\) the bracket must vanish: $$\beta f'(\bar k)=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G_k(\bar k,1)=\frac1\beta-(1-\delta)=\rho+\delta. \tag{MGR}$$

  1. Existence. \(G_k(\cdot,1)\) is continuous on \((0,\infty)\), and by Inada \(G_k\to\infty\) as \(k\to0\) and \(G_k\to0\) as \(k\to\infty\). Since \(\rho+\delta\in(0,\infty)\), the intermediate value theorem gives a solution.
  2. Uniqueness. \(G\) is strictly concave in \(k\), so \(G_k\) is strictly decreasing and crosses \(\rho+\delta\) once.
  3. Positive consumption. \(\bar c=G(\bar k,1)-\delta\bar k\). Strict concavity and \(G(0,1)=0\) give \(G(\bar k,1)>G_k(\bar k,1)\,\bar k=(\rho+\delta)\bar k>\delta\bar k\), so \(\bar c>0\) and \(\bar k\in\Gamma(\bar k)\).
  4. Optimality from \(k_0=\bar k\). \(F=U(f(x)-y)\) is concave (a concave increasing \(U\) of the concave function \(f(x)-y\)), \(F_x=U'f'>0\), and \(X=\mathbb R_+\). The first exercise applies.
  5. The trivial rest point. \(k=0\): \(f(0)=0\) makes \(\Gamma(0)=\{0\}\), so \(k_t\equiv0\) is the only feasible path from 0. It is a rest point of the dynamics but not an interior solution of the EE (with \(U'(0)=\infty\) the EE is not even defined there).

Golden rule versus modified golden rule

The golden rule maximizes steady-state consumption \(c=f(k)-k\): \(f'(k_{GR})=1\), i.e. \(G_k(k_{GR},1)=\delta\). The planner's steady state (MGR) is the modified golden rule \(f'(\bar k)=1/\beta>1\). Because \(f'\) is decreasing, \(\bar k\lt k_{GR}\) for every \(\beta\lt1\). The planner deliberately stops short of the consumption-maximizing capital stock. Building the extra capital would cost consumption in the near future, which is worth more than the small steady-state gain far away. The net return at \(\bar k\) is \(f'(\bar k)-1=\rho>0\): the interest rate exceeds the growth rate (zero here).

Compare Lesson 15. In the Diamond OLG model, the competitive steady state can sit above the golden rule, and then the economy is dynamically inefficient and a pay-as-you-go transfer helps everyone. A planner solving this problem never puts the economy there: above \(k_{GR}\) it could eat the excess capital and have more consumption today and in every later period. Perpetual youth (Lesson 19) breaks the link in a different way: with \(p>0\), turnover of generations moves the steady state away from the modified golden rule, and it converges to it as \(p\to0\) (with \(\alpha=0\)).

8. What the steady state does not depend on (the exam favourite)

Every past exam with folder-4 material asks some version of "show that the steady state does not change when…". The trick is always the same: the steady-state equation evaluates both terms of the EE at the same point \((\bar x,\bar x)\). A modification that multiplies both terms by the same positive number, or that adds terms vanishing on the diagonal, cannot move \(\bar x\).

Three invariance results
  1. The form of \(U\). (Fall 2005 MT, Ex. 1 Q6, in continuous time.) In the growth model the steady state solves \(U'(\bar c)[\beta f'(\bar k)-1]=0\). As long as \(U'(\bar c)>0\), \(\bar k\) depends only on \(\beta\) and technology, and so does \(\bar c=f(\bar k)-\bar k\).
  2. A concave transformation \(\tilde F=U\circ F\). (Fall 2025 MT1, Q II.5–6.) \(\tilde F_x=U'(F)F_x\) and \(\tilde F_y=U'(F)F_y\). At \((\bar x,\bar x)\) the common factor \(U'(F(\bar x,\bar x))>0\) comes out: \(\tilde F_y+\beta\tilde F_x=U'(F(\bar x,\bar x))[F_y+\beta F_x]\). Same roots.
  3. A quadratic adjustment cost \(-\frac b2(y-x)^2\). (Fall 2016 MT2, Q2 part 2.2.) The cost adds \(+b(y-x)\) to \(F_x\) and \(-b(y-x)\) to \(F_y\), and both vanish at \(y=x\). Same steady-state equation.
Same steady state ≠ same dynamics

Off the steady state, the two EE terms are evaluated at different points: \(U'(F(x_t,x_{t+1}))\neq U'(F(x_{t+1},x_{t+2}))\) and \(x_{t+1}-x_t\neq x_{t+2}-x_{t+1}\). The factors no longer cancel, so the optimal path from \(x_0\neq\bar x\) changes. Typically convergence slows, because both modifications penalize uneven paths. Exam questions pair "show the steady state is unchanged" with "show the speed of convergence falls", and the second half is folder 5 (linearization). Exercise 27.2(d) proves the path changes.

Comparative statics go through the same equation. Differentiating \(\beta f'(\bar k)=1\): \(f'\,d\beta+\beta f''\,d\bar k=0\), so \(d\bar k/d\beta=-f'(\bar k)/(\beta f''(\bar k))>0\). More patient planners accumulate more. With \(f'=G_k+1-\delta\): \(\beta(G_{kk}\,d\bar k-d\delta)=0\), so \(d\bar k/d\delta=1/G_{kk}\lt0\). In the Fall 2005 human-capital version, \(g'(\bar h)=p(\rho+\delta)\): \(\bar h\) rises with \(\beta\) and falls with \(p\) and \(\delta\) (Exercise 27.1).

On past exams this material is worth 13–28% of the points whenever folder 4 is covered. Answer True or False and justify in at most four lines.

  1. In the neoclassical growth model \(\max\sum_t\beta^tU(c_t)\), \(c_t+k_{t+1}=f(k_t)\), the steady-state level of consumption does not depend on the form of \(U\), as long as \(U\) is strictly increasing and concave. Fall 2005 Midterm, Ex. 1 Q6 (restated in discrete time)

    True. The steady-state EE is \(U'(\bar c)[-1+\beta f'(\bar k)]=0\), and \(U'(\bar c)>0\) gives \(\beta f'(\bar k)=1\), which involves only \(\beta\) and \(f\). Then \(\bar c=f(\bar k)-\bar k\) is pinned down too EE p.18. (In continuous time: \(f'(\bar k)=\rho+\delta\).)

    Answer

    False. \(\tilde F_x=U'(F)F_x\), \(\tilde F_y=U'(F)F_y\). At \((\bar x,\bar x)\): \(\tilde F_y+\beta\tilde F_x=U'(F(\bar x,\bar x))\,[F_y+\beta F_x]=0\) since \(U'>0\). Both EE terms are evaluated at the same point, so the common factor cancels.

  3. In the setting of item 2, the optimal path starting from any \(x_0\) is also unchanged, because the two problems have the same steady state and both are strictly concave. exam-inspired
    Answer

    False. Off the steady state the EE of the new problem is \(U'(F(x_t,x_{t+1}))F_y(t)+\beta U'(F(x_{t+1},x_{t+2}))F_x(t+1)=0\). A path solving the old EE solves this one only if \(U'(F(x_t,x_{t+1}))=U'(F(x_{t+1},x_{t+2}))\), i.e. the period return is constant along the path, which fails during the transition. The steady state is the same; the dynamics differ (Exercise 27.2).

  4. Adding a quadratic adjustment cost, \(F(x,y;b)=F(x,y)-\frac b2(y-x)^2\) with \(b>0\), lowers the steady state, because it makes holding capital more costly. Fall 2016 MT2, Q2 part 2.2
    Answer

    False. \(F_x(\cdot;b)=F_x+b(y-x)\) and \(F_y(\cdot;b)=F_y-b(y-x)\). Both extra terms vanish at \(y=x\), so the steady-state equation is identical: \(x^*(b)=x^*(0)\). The cost is on changing the state, not on holding it; it only slows the approach.

  5. A feasible path that satisfies the Euler equation at every date and the transversality condition is optimal. exam-inspired
    Answer

    False without further assumptions. Sufficiency needs \(F\) concave, \(F_x\ge0\) along the path, and states bounded below EE p.12. Counterexample: \(\Gamma(x)=[0,1]\), \(F=(y-\frac12)^2\). The path \(x_t=\frac12\) satisfies EE and TC (all derivatives are 0) but is the worst feasible path.

  6. A twice-differentiable concave function has a negative definite Hessian at every point. exam-inspired (slide 13 wording)
    Answer

    False. Concavity is equivalent to a negative semidefinite Hessian. \(f(x)=-x^4\) is even strictly concave with \(f''(0)=0\), and linear functions are concave with a zero Hessian. Negative definiteness is sufficient for strict concavity, not necessary.

  7. Consider two growth models that differ only in the discount factor, \(\beta_1\lt\beta_2\). The steady-state capital stock is higher in the second one. exam-inspired
    Answer

    True. \(f'(\bar k_i)=1/\beta_i\), so \(f'(\bar k_2)\lt f'(\bar k_1)\). Since \(f\) (including undepreciated capital) is strictly concave, \(\bar k_2>\bar k_1\). Equivalently \(d\bar k/d\beta=-f'/(\beta f'')>0\).

  8. If the planner is patient enough (\(\beta\) close to 1), the steady state of the growth model lies above the golden rule, as in a dynamically inefficient Diamond economy. exam-inspired (links Lesson 15)
    Answer

    False. \(f'(\bar k)=1/\beta>1=f'(k_{GR})\) for every \(\beta\lt1\), so \(\bar k\lt k_{GR}\), with \(\bar k\to k_{GR}\) only as \(\beta\to1\). Over-accumulation is a feature of some competitive OLG equilibria (Lesson 15), never of this planner's optimum.

  9. At a steady state, the optimal policy function satisfies \(g(\bar x)=0\). exam-inspired (a circulating drill)
    Answer

    False. A steady state is a fixed point of the policy: \(g(\bar x)=\bar x\). What is zero at \(\bar x\) is the EE residual \(F_y(\bar x,\bar x)+\beta F_x(\bar x,\bar x)\) (and, in the quadratic example of Lesson 30, \(\bar x\) itself happens to be 0).

  10. To apply the sufficiency theorem, the transversality condition must be checked along every feasible alternative path, not only along the candidate. exam-inspired
    Answer

    False. In Step 5 of the proof, \(F_x\ge0\) and \(x_{T+1}\ge0\) remove the alternative from the bound: \(D_T\le\beta^{T+1}F_x(x^*_{T+1},x^*_{T+2})\cdot x^*_{T+1}\). Only the candidate's TC is needed.

Show solution

(a) \(g(h_t)+m=c_t+p\,i_t\).
(b) \(\mathcal L=\sum_{t\ge0}\beta^t\Big[u\big(g(h_t)+m-p\,i_t\big)+\lambda_t\big(i_t+(1-\delta)h_t-h_{t+1}\big)\Big]\).

(b) \(f(x)=-x^4\): strictly concave, \(f''(0)=0\). Negative definiteness is sufficient for strict concavity, not necessary, and concavity is equivalent to semidefiniteness. The slide's "negative definite" should read "negative semidefinite".
(c) For \(\lambda\in(0,1]\), concavity gives \(f(x^0+\lambda(x-x^0))\ge f(x^0)+\lambda[f(x)-f(x^0)]\). So \(f(x)-f(x^0)\le\frac{f(x^0+\lambda(x-x^0))-f(x^0)}{\lambda}\to\nabla f(x^0)\cdot(x-x^0)\) as \(\lambda\downarrow0\).
(d) Let \(z=\lambda x+(1-\lambda)x'\). Apply the inequality at \(z\): \(f(x)\le f(z)+\nabla f(z)\cdot(x-z)\) and \(f(x')\le f(z)+\nabla f(z)\cdot(x'-z)\). Multiply by \(\lambda\) and \(1-\lambda\) and add. Since \(\lambda(x-z)+(1-\lambda)(x'-z)=0\), we get \(\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(x')\le f(z)\). (Geometrically: \(f\) is the lower envelope of its tangent planes, and a minimum of affine functions is concave.)
(e) Step 1. It is applied to \(F\) as a function of \((x,y)\in\mathbb R^{2m}\), at the candidate's point \((x^*_t,x^*_{t+1})\), and evaluated at the alternative's point \((x_t,x_{t+1})\). Joint concavity in \((x,y)\) is needed, not concavity in each argument separately.

Exercise 27.7 Exam-inspired (steady states that exist, and ones that don't)

For each return function: compute \(F_x,F_y\), write the EE, and find all steady states. When there is no steady state, find the optimal path with EE + TC instead. Take \(X=\mathbb R_+\) and \(x_0>0\) throughout.

  1. \(F(k,k')=\log(Ak^\alpha-k')\), \(\Gamma(k)=[0,Ak^\alpha]\), \(\alpha\in(0,1)\). Also show \(F\) is strictly concave. (15 pts)
  2. Cake eating: \(F(x,y)=\log(x-y)\), \(\Gamma(x)=[0,x]\). Verify that \(x_{t+1}=\beta x_t\) is optimal. (15 pts)
  3. \(F(x,y)=-(x-y)^2+bx\) with \(b>0\), \(\Gamma(x)=\mathbb R_+\). Show there is no steady state, and find the optimal path. (15 pts)
Hint

For (c), let \(\Delta_t=x_{t+1}-x_t\) and write the EE as a first-order difference equation in \(\Delta\). Look for a constant solution, and check the TC with a linearly growing \(x_t\).

Show solution

(a) With \(c=Ak^\alpha-k'\): \(F_x=\alpha Ak^{\alpha-1}/c\), \(F_y=-1/c\). Second derivatives: \(F_{yy}=-1/c^2\), \(F_{xy}=\alpha Ak^{\alpha-1}/c^2\), \(F_{xx}=\alpha(\alpha-1)Ak^{\alpha-2}/c-(\alpha Ak^{\alpha-1})^2/c^2\lt0\). The determinant is \(F_{xx}F_{yy}-F_{xy}^2=\alpha(1-\alpha)Ak^{\alpha-2}/c^3>0\), so \(F\) is strictly concave. EE: \(1/c_t=\alpha\beta Ak_{t+1}^{\alpha-1}/c_{t+1}\). Steady state: \(\alpha\beta A\bar k^{\alpha-1}=1\), so \(\bar k=(\alpha\beta A)^{1/(1-\alpha)}\). This is slide 35's model (100% depreciation), solved in closed form in Lesson 30.
(b) \(F_x=1/(x-y)\), \(F_y=-1/(x-y)\). EE: \(-1/c_t+\beta/c_{t+1}=0\), i.e. \(c_{t+1}=\beta c_t\). Steady state: at \(x=y\), \(c=0\) and \(F=-\infty\), so there is no interior steady state; the cake must shrink. Try \(x_{t+1}=\beta x_t\): \(c_t=(1-\beta)x_t\) and \(c_{t+1}=(1-\beta)\beta x_t=\beta c_t\) ✓. TC: \(\beta^tF_x(x_t,x_{t+1})x_t=\beta^tx_t/((1-\beta)x_t)=\beta^t/(1-\beta)\to0\) ✓. \(F\) is concave, \(F_x>0\), \(X=\mathbb R_+\), so the path is optimal by sufficiency.
(c) \(F_x=-2(x-y)+b\), \(F_y=2(x-y)\). At \(x=y\): \(F_y+\beta F_x=\beta b>0\), never zero, so there is no steady state. The return rewards a high state (\(+bx\)), and that pull never dies out. EE: \(-2\Delta_t+\beta(2\Delta_{t+1}+b)=0\), i.e. \(\Delta_{t+1}=\Delta_t/\beta-b/2\). This is unstable (coefficient \(1/\beta>1\)), so any \(\Delta_0\) other than the fixed point \(\bar\Delta=\frac{\beta b}{2(1-\beta)}\) explodes. Take \(\Delta_t\equiv\bar\Delta\), so \(x_t=x_0+t\bar\Delta\). \(F\) is concave (its Hessian is \(\begin{pmatrix}-2&2\\2&-2\end{pmatrix}\), negative semidefinite), \(F_x=2\bar\Delta+b>0\) along the path, \(X=\mathbb R_+\). TC: \(\beta^t(2\bar\Delta+b)(x_0+t\bar\Delta)\to0\) ✓. So the state grows linearly forever, and that is optimal. The EE + TC method needs neither a steady state nor bounded paths.

Key takeaways

  • EE: \(F_y(x_t,x_{t+1})+\beta F_x(x_{t+1},x_{t+2})=0\), from a one-date perturbation of \(x_{t+1}\): marginal cost today equals discounted marginal benefit tomorrow. TC: \(\lim\beta^tF_x\cdot x_t=0\), so the present value of the stock vanishes and no value is left unused.
  • Growth model: \(U'(c_t)=\beta U'(c_{t+1})f'(k_{t+1})\), and the TC is \(\lim\beta^tU'(c_t)f'(k_t)k_t=0\), equivalently \(\lim\beta^tU'(c_t)k_{t+1}=0\).
  • Sufficiency: \(F\) concave (Step 1), EE (Steps 3–4), \(F_x\ge0\) and \(X=\mathbb R^m_+\) (Step 5), TC on the candidate only (Step 6). Without concavity you can land on a minimum. Without a lower bound, Ponzi schemes break the proof.
  • Concavity ⇔ tangent planes lie above ⇔ (for \(C^2\)) a negative semidefinite Hessian.
  • Steady state: \(F_y(\bar x,\bar x)+\beta F_x(\bar x,\bar x)=0\). In convex problems, staying there from \(x_0=\bar x\) is optimal. Growth model: \(\beta f'(\bar k)=1\), unique, and below the golden rule.
  • The steady state ignores anything that scales both EE terms equally or vanishes on the diagonal: the form of \(U\), \(U\circ F\), and costs on \(y-x\). The dynamics do not ignore them.
