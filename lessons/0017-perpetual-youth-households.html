Perpetual Youth I: Households with Uncertain Lifetimes

Covers Blanchard–Yaari slides 2–10 · About 40 min · Prerequisites: Lesson 8 (OLG), Lesson 14 (budget constraints & Ricardian logic)
By the end you can:
  1. derive the cohort-size function \(N(s,t)=pe^{-p(t-s)}\) and explain why everyone alive has the same life expectancy;
  2. explain why a fair annuity pays \(r+p\), and why that turns expected utility with death into ordinary discounting at \(\theta+p\);
  3. solve the log-utility household problem, \(c=(\theta+p)(v+h)\), and translate the present-value budget into ODEs plus a boundary condition;
  4. derive the Euler equation \(\dot c=(r-\theta)c\) and explain why the death rate \(p\) drops out of it.
Primary source Blanchard, O. J. (1985), "Debt, Deficits, and Finite Horizons," Journal of Political Economy 93(2): 223–247, §§I–II. The annuity idea comes from Yaari, M. E. (1965), "Uncertain Lifetime, Life Insurance, and the Theory of the Consumer," Review of Economic Studies 32(2): 137–150. Alvarez's perpetual-youth slides follow Blanchard's notation closely.
Notation alert: same letters, new meanings
  • \(p\) is the instantaneous death probability (a hazard rate), not a price. Prices are now \(R(t,z)\): the price at \(t\) of one good delivered at \(z\).
  • \(\theta\) is the subjective discount rate. In Class Note III, θ was the PAYG tax (Lesson 14).
  • \(\alpha\) (next lesson) is the rate at which labour income declines with age, not an endowment share or a capital share.
  • \(s\) is a birth date (cohort index) and \(t\) is calendar time, so age is \(t-s\). In Notes II–III, \(s\) meant saving.

1. Why this model?

The two-period OLG model of Part II has a sharp young/old split, which makes dynamics with capital and debt awkward: every policy question turns into an infinite sequence of cohort-specific budgets. Blanchard's "perpetual youth" model keeps the essential OLG ingredient, namely that people alive today are not the same as people alive tomorrow, so new cohorts keep arriving with no assets. It drops the age structure that makes OLG intractable.

The trick is that every agent faces the same constant hazard of death, whatever their age. Nobody is "young" or "old" in terms of their horizon BY slide 3. That is unrealistic but tractable: with log utility (and, in Lesson 18, an exponential income profile) individual decisions aggregate exactly. We get a two-equation system in aggregate consumption and wealth that looks like the Ramsey model, plus one extra term that captures the turnover of generations.

2. Demographics

Time is continuous. In any interval of length \(\Delta\), each living agent dies with probability \(p\Delta\) (to first order), independently of age, with \(p\in(0,\infty)\) BY slide 2.

Derivation: the size of a cohort
  1. Discrete step. Let \(N(s,t)\) be the number of people born at \(s\) still alive at \(t\). A fraction \(1-p\Delta\) of them survive another \(\Delta\): \(N(s,t+\Delta) = N(s,t)(1-p\Delta)\).
  2. Take the limit. Rearranging, \(\dfrac{N(s,t+\Delta)-N(s,t)}{\Delta} = -pN(s,t)\). Letting \(\Delta\downarrow 0\) gives the ODE \(\dfrac{\partial N(s,t)}{\partial t} = -p\,N(s,t)\).
  3. Boundary condition. Newborns replace the dead. With a total population of 1, deaths per unit of time are \(p\cdot 1\), so the cohort born at \(s\) has initial size \(N(s,s)=p\).
  4. Solve. The ODE \(\dot N = -pN\) has solution \(N(s,t) = N(s,s)e^{-p(t-s)} = p\,e^{-p(t-s)}\).
  5. Consistency check. The total population alive at \(t\) is $$\int_{-\infty}^{t} p\,e^{-p(t-s)}\,ds = \Big[e^{-p(t-s)}\Big]_{s=-\infty}^{s=t} = 1 - 0 = 1 .$$ The population stays constant at 1, so the boundary condition in Step 3 is self-consistent.
∎

Two consequences follow. (i) The probability of surviving \(x\) more years is \(e^{-px}\) for anyone, whatever their age: the exponential distribution is memoryless. (ii) Expected remaining lifetime is therefore the same for everyone: $$\int_0^\infty e^{-px}\,dx = \frac1p .$$ With \(p = 0.02\), everyone alive, at every age, expects 50 more years. That is the "perpetual youth."

Interactive: the age distribution. The curve is the size of each cohort by age, \(N(s,t)=pe^{-p(t-s)}\). The shaded area is the population alive, which equals 1 whatever \(p\) is (the plot is truncated at age 120). Raising \(p\) makes the population younger and turnover faster: more newborns per year (the curve starts higher) and fewer survivors to old age. The mean age of the population, \(1/p\), equals each person's expected remaining life.

3. Annuities: why wealth earns \(r+p\)

With an uncertain lifetime and no bequest motive, holding wealth at the moment of death is pure waste. Following Yaari (1965), assume competitive insurance companies sell actuarially fair annuities. You hand the company your wealth. Each instant it pays you a premium while you live, and when you die it keeps what you held. With negative wealth the contract runs the other way: you pay a premium, and your debt is cancelled when you die, which is life insurance BY slide 3.

Derivation: the fair annuity rate
  1. The contract. An agent invests \(v\) at \(t\). The company pays \(v\,\dfrac{1+\Delta r}{1-p\Delta}\) at \(t+\Delta\) if the agent is alive, and nothing if the agent is dead.
  2. Zero profit. The company invests the \(v\) at the riskless rate and holds \(v(1+\Delta r)\) at \(t+\Delta\). With a continuum of independent agents (law of large numbers), exactly a fraction \(1-p\Delta\) of buyers survive. Paying each survivor \(v(1+\Delta r)/(1-p\Delta)\) costs \((1-p\Delta)\cdot v(1+\Delta r)/(1-p\Delta) = v(1+\Delta r)\), which is exactly what the company holds. It breaks even.
  3. Expand for small Δ. Since \(\dfrac{1}{1-p\Delta} = 1 + p\Delta + o(\Delta)\), $$v\,\frac{1+\Delta r}{1-p\Delta} = v\big(1 + r\Delta\big)\big(1+p\Delta + o(\Delta)\big) = v + v\,(r+p)\Delta + o(\Delta).$$
  4. Read off the rate. Conditional on survival, wealth grows at instantaneous rate \(r+p\). The extra \(p\) is the survivors' share of the wealth of those who died.
∎
Intuition: survivors inherit from the dead, via the insurer

Every instant, a fraction \(p\) of wealth holders die. Their wealth doesn't vanish: the insurer redistributes it to survivors in proportion to their holdings. A survivor's return is therefore \(r\) plus \(p\). For the economy as a whole, though, no wealth is created; it is only moved from the dead to the living. That is why, in Lesson 18, aggregate wealth grows at \(r\) while each individual's grows at \(r+p\).

4. Preferences: death adds to impatience

An agent at \(t\) maximizes expected discounted utility, getting utility only while alive. The expectation is only over the date of death \(T\) BY slide 4: $$E_t\left[\int_t^{T} u(c(z))\,e^{-\theta(z-t)}\,dz\right], \qquad \Pr(T > z\mid T>t) = e^{-p(z-t)} .$$ One util at \(t+\Delta\) is worth \(1/(1+\Delta\theta)\) utils at \(t\), so \(\theta\in(0,\infty)\) is the pure rate of time preference.

Derivation: expected utility = discounting at \(\theta+p\)
  1. Write the random upper limit with an indicator: \(\int_t^T f(z)\,dz = \int_t^\infty f(z)\,\mathbf 1\{T>z\}\,dz\), where \(f(z) = u(c(z))e^{-\theta(z-t)}\).
  2. Take expectations inside the integral (Fubini–Tonelli; fine when \(u\) is bounded below on the relevant path, or integrable): \(E_t\int_t^\infty f(z)\mathbf 1\{T>z\}dz = \int_t^\infty f(z)\Pr(T>z\mid T>t)\,dz\).
  3. Substitute the survival probability: $$E_t\left[\int_t^{T} u(c(z))e^{-\theta(z-t)}dz\right] = \int_t^\infty u(c(z))\,e^{-(\theta+p)(z-t)}\,dz .$$
∎

So a mortal agent behaves like an immortal one with a higher discount rate, \(\theta+p\). But the same agent also earns a higher return, \(r+p\). Section 7 shows that the two \(p\)'s cancel in the Euler equation but not in the consumption function.

5. The household problem and its solution

Define the intertemporal price \(R(t,z)\), the price at \(t\) of one unit of the good delivered at \(z\) conditional on being alive (the annuity market prices exactly this). Let \(v(t)\) be non-human (financial) wealth and \(y(z)\) labour income. The present-value budget is BY slide 5 $$\int_t^\infty \big[c(z)-y(z)\big]R(t,z)\,dz = v(t).$$ Define human wealth \(h(t)\equiv\int_t^\infty y(z)R(t,z)\,dz\), the market value of future labour income. The budget becomes \(\int_t^\infty c(z)R(t,z)\,dz = v(t) + h(t)\): the present value of consumption equals total wealth.

Result: log consumption function BY slide 5

With \(u=\log\), the optimal plan satisfies $$c(t) = (\theta+p)\big(v(t)+h(t)\big).$$ The marginal propensity to consume out of total wealth is \(\theta+p\). It is the same for every agent, whatever their age and whatever the path of interest rates.

The proof uses a lemma you already know from Cobb–Douglas demand BY slide 6.

Lemma: log utility spends fixed shares

The solution to \(\max_{c_1,\dots,c_n}\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\log c_i\) subject to \(\sum_{i=1}^n R_ic_i = W\) (with \(a_i>0\)) is $$R_i c_i = \frac{a_i}{\sum_{k=1}^n a_k}\,W \qquad\text{for each } i.$$ The expenditure share on good \(i\) depends only on its preference weight.

Proof of the lemma, then the consumption function
  1. FOC. The objective is strictly concave and the constraint linear, so the FOCs are necessary and sufficient: \(a_i/c_i = \lambda R_i\), i.e. \(R_ic_i = a_i/\lambda\).
  2. Pin down λ. Sum over \(i\): \(W = \sum_i R_ic_i = \big(\sum_i a_i\big)/\lambda\), so \(1/\lambda = W/\sum_k a_k\). Substituting back gives the lemma.
  3. Continuous analogue. In our problem the "goods" are consumption at each date \(z\ge t\). The weight is \(a(z) = e^{-(\theta+p)(z-t)}\), the price is \(R(t,z)\), and the wealth is \(W = v(t)+h(t)\). The weights sum to $$\int_t^\infty e^{-(\theta+p)(z-t)}\,dz = \frac{1}{\theta+p}.$$
  4. Apply at \(z=t\). \(R(t,t)=1\) by definition and \(a(t)=1\), so the lemma gives \(1\cdot c(t) = \dfrac{1}{1/(\theta+p)}\,(v(t)+h(t)) = (\theta+p)(v(t)+h(t))\).
  5. Directly in continuous time (same argument): the FOC is \(e^{-(\theta+p)(z-t)}/c(z) = \lambda R(t,z)\), so \(R(t,z)c(z) = e^{-(\theta+p)(z-t)}/\lambda\). Integrating over \(z\) gives \(v+h = 1/(\lambda(\theta+p))\). At \(z=t\), \(c(t) = 1/\lambda = (\theta+p)(v+h)\).
∎
Typos on slide 6

The slide writes the objective as "\(a_1\log c_1 + a_1\log c_2+\dots\)" and the budget as "\(R_1c_1+R_1c_2+\dots\)". The second subscripts should be \(a_2\) and \(R_2\) (and so on), as in the lemma above.

6. From present values to ODEs

6.1 Prices as accumulated interest

Define the instantaneous rate \(r(\mu)\) by writing BY slide 7 $$R(t,z) = \exp\left(-\int_t^z \big[r(\mu)+p\big]\,d\mu\right).$$ Why this form? Take the discrete case with periods of length Δ and \(p=0\) BY slide 8. The price of a good \((z-t)/\Delta\) periods ahead is the inverse of the compounded gross return: $$R(t,z)^{-1} = \prod_{i=1}^{(z-t)/\Delta}\big(1+r_{t+i}\Delta\big)\quad\Rightarrow\quad -\log R(t,z) = \sum_{i=1}^{(z-t)/\Delta}\log\big(1+r_{t+i}\Delta\big) = \sum_{i}\big[r_{t+i}\Delta + o(\Delta)\big].$$ The sum is a Riemann sum, so as \(\Delta\downarrow0\), \(\log R(t,z)\to-\int_t^z r(\mu)d\mu\). With mortality, the per-period gross return on annuities is \((1+r\Delta)/(1-p\Delta)\). Its log is \((r+p)\Delta + o(\Delta)\), which adds the \(p\).

A fact we will use repeatedly: \(\dfrac{\partial R(t,z)}{\partial t} = \big[r(t)+p\big]R(t,z)\). Moving the start date forward removes one instant of discounting.

6.2 The sequential budget constraint

Derivation: \(\dot v = (r+p)v + y - c\) BY slide 9
  1. Truncate the horizon. For any \(T>t\), wealth at \(t\) finances net consumption up to \(T\) plus the wealth left at \(T\): $$v(t) = \int_t^T \big[c(z)-y(z)\big]R(t,z)\,dz + R(t,T)\,v(T).$$
  2. Differentiate in \(t\) (Leibniz rule: the lower limit contributes \(-[c(t)-y(t)]R(t,t)\), and the integrand's \(t\)-derivative uses \(\partial_tR = (r(t)+p)R\)): $$\dot v(t) = -\big[c(t)-y(t)\big] + \big[r(t)+p\big]\left\{\int_t^T [c(z)-y(z)]R(t,z)\,dz + R(t,T)v(T)\right\}.$$
  3. Recognize the braces. They are \(v(t)\) by Step 1. Therefore $$\boxed{\;\dot v(t) = \big[r(t)+p\big]v(t) + y(t) - c(t)\;}$$ Wealth earns the annuity rate, plus labour income, minus consumption.
  4. Human wealth, same way. Differentiating \(h(t) = \int_t^\infty y(z)R(t,z)\,dz\) gives $$\dot h(t) = \big[r(t)+p\big]h(t) - y(t),\qquad \lim_{z\to\infty}R(t,z)h(z) = 0 .$$ Rearranged, \((r+p)h = y + \dot h\): human wealth is an asset whose "dividend" is \(y\) and whose required return is \(r+p\).
∎

6.3 The boundary condition

The ODE alone does not pin down the budget; it holds for any \(v(T)\). Letting \(T\to\infty\) in Step 1, the sequential constraint is equivalent to the present-value constraint if and only if BY slide 10 $$\lim_{T\to\infty} R(t,T)\,v(T) = \lim_{T\to\infty} v(T)\,e^{-\int_t^T (r(\mu)+p)d\mu} = 0 .$$

If the limit is…then…because…
\(>0\)the plan is not optimalthe agent "dies holding" wealth of positive present value. Spending that amount on extra consumption at any finite date is affordable and raises utility.
\(<0\)the plan is not feasibledebt is rolled over forever and never repaid in present value (a Ponzi scheme). No lender would accept it, and it violates the PV budget.
\(=0\)ODE + limit ⇔ PV budgetthis is the condition we impose.

Human wealth must also be finite, which requires \(\lim_{T\to\infty}y(T)e^{-\int_t^T(r+p)} = 0\) (in fact the integral must converge). If human wealth were infinite, the agent could borrow without bound against it and the problem would have no solution. With constant \(y\) and \(r\), finiteness needs \(r+p>0\). So negative interest rates are fine as long as \(r>-p\), a point that matters in Lessons 18–20.

7. The Euler equation: \(p\) cancels

Derivation: \(\dot c = (r-\theta)c\), two ways BY slide 7
  1. From the FOC. \(e^{-(\theta+p)(z-t)}/c(z) = \lambda\exp\big(-\int_t^z[r(\mu)+p]d\mu\big)\). Taking logs, \(-(\theta+p)(z-t) - \log c(z) = \log\lambda - \int_t^z[r(\mu)+p]d\mu\). Differentiating in \(z\): \(-(\theta+p) - \dot c/c = -(r(z)+p)\), so \(\dot c(z) = [r(z)-\theta]c(z)\).
  2. From the consumption function (a good consistency check). Differentiate \(c = (\theta+p)(v+h)\) and use the two ODEs: $$\dot c = (\theta+p)\big[(r+p)v + y - c + (r+p)h - y\big] = (\theta+p)(r+p)(v+h) - (\theta+p)c = (r+p)c - (\theta+p)c = (r-\theta)c .$$
∎
Intuition: why mortality changes the level, not the slope

Death makes the agent more impatient (discount \(\theta+p\)), but annuities raise the return by exactly the same \(p\) (return \(r+p\)). In the Euler equation only the gap between return and impatience matters, \((r+p)-(\theta+p) = r-\theta\), so \(p\) cancels. The level is different: the MPC out of wealth is \(\theta+p\), not \(\theta\), because a shorter expected horizon means total wealth is spread over fewer expected years. Even so, when all agents face the same \(r\), the cross-section of consumption growth is identical for everyone. Differences between cohorts come only from their different wealth levels, and newborns arrive with \(v=0\).

See it: one agent's life

Take constant \(r\) and constant labour income \(y\) (this is \(\alpha=0\) in the next lesson's notation) and an agent born with \(v=0\). Then \(h = y/(r+p)\), initial consumption is \(c_0 = (\theta+p)y/(r+p)\), and \(c(x) = c_0e^{(r-\theta)x}\) at age \(x\). Since \(h\) is constant, \(v(x) = c(x)/(\theta+p) - h\), or $$\frac{v(x)}{y} = \frac{e^{(r-\theta)x}-1}{r+p}.$$

Interactive: consumption and wealth over the life cycle (constant \(y=1\), \(v=0\) at birth). Left: consumption \(c\) (blue) against labour income \(y\) (black). Right: financial wealth \(v\) (orange). Try: set \(r=\theta\). Consumption is flat and equal to income, and the agent never trades. With \(r>\theta\), the agent starts below income and saves. With \(r<\theta\), the agent starts above income and borrows, and its debt approaches \(-h=-y/(r+p)\): it pledges almost all of its human wealth. The debt is safe for lenders because life insurance cancels it at death.

8. Check your understanding

1. Why does a fair annuity pay \(r+p\) rather than \(r\)?
  • Insurers charge a premium p on every contract they sell
  • Survivors receive the wealth of those who die each instant
  • Mortality risk raises the riskless interest rate by p
  • Agents discount the future at rate p on top of θ
A fraction \(p\Delta\) of annuitants die each instant. Their wealth reverts to the insurer, which (breaking even) pays it out to survivors: \(v(1+r\Delta)/(1-p\Delta)\approx v(1+(r+p)\Delta)\). The riskless rate \(r\) itself is unchanged.
2. With log utility, how does the death rate \(p\) affect an individual's consumption?
  • It lowers the growth rate but leaves the MPC at θ
  • It raises both the growth rate and the MPC equally
  • It has no effect on either the level or the growth
  • It raises the MPC but leaves the growth rate r − θ
\(c = (\theta+p)(v+h)\), so the MPC rises with \(p\). The Euler equation \(\dot c/c = (r+p)-(\theta+p) = r-\theta\) is independent of \(p\). Note that \(h\) itself also depends on \(p\), through the discount rate \(r+p\).
3. A plan satisfies \(\dot v=(r+p)v+y-c\) but has \(\lim_{T\to\infty}R(t,T)v(T) < 0\). What is wrong with it?
  • It is a Ponzi scheme and violates the PV budget
  • It leaves wealth unspent and so is not optimal
  • Nothing, because the ODE already implies the budget
  • It requires a negative interest rate to be feasible
A negative limit means debt is never repaid in present value. The sequential ODE holds for any terminal wealth, so the boundary condition is what makes it equivalent to the PV budget. A positive limit would be the "unspent wealth, not optimal" case.
4. Why can each agent's age be ignored when forecasting their remaining lifetime?
  • All cohorts are born with exactly the same size p
  • The total population is normalized to equal one
  • The death hazard p is constant, so it is memoryless
  • Annuities pay r + p to every agent who is alive
A constant hazard gives exponential survival, \(\Pr(\text{alive at }t+x\mid\text{alive at }t)=e^{-px}\), independent of age. So expected remaining life is \(1/p\) for everyone: "perpetual youth."

9. Exercises

Exercise 17.1 Slides p.2 Demographics
  1. Derive \(N(s,t)=pe^{-p(t-s)}\) from \(N(s,t+\Delta)=N(s,t)(1-p\Delta)\) and \(N(s,s)=p\), and show the population is constant at 1.
  2. Show that the share of the population older than \(A\) is \(e^{-pA}\), and that the mean age of the population is \(1/p\).
  3. With \(p=0.02\), what share of the population is older than 50?
Show solution

(a) See §2. (b) People older than \(A\) were born before \(t-A\): \(\int_{-\infty}^{t-A}pe^{-p(t-s)}ds = e^{-pA}\). The mean age is \(\int_0^\infty x\,pe^{-px}dx = 1/p\). (c) \(e^{-0.02\cdot 50} = e^{-1}\approx 0.368\).

Exercise 17.2 Slides p.3 Annuities and life insurance
  1. Show that an insurer paying survivors \(v(1+\Delta r)/(1-p\Delta)\) breaks even, and expand to first order in Δ.
  2. Interpret the same contract when \(v<0\). Who pays whom, and what happens at death?
  3. Why does an agent with no bequest motive put all wealth into annuities? (Hint: compare the return conditional on survival.)
Show solution

(a) See §3. (b) With \(v<0\) the agent is a borrower. Debt grows at \(r+p\): the agent pays interest \(r\) plus a premium \(p\) to an insurer, who repays the lender if the agent dies. At death the debt is cancelled, which is life insurance, so lenders bear no default risk. (c) A plain bond pays \(r\) and leaves wealth at death, which the agent doesn't value. An annuity pays \(r+p>r\) in every state the agent cares about (alive). It dominates, so a utility maximizer holds wealth only as annuities (Yaari's result).

Exercise 17.3 Slides p.6 The log lemma

Let \(a=(1/2,1/3,1/6)\), \(R=(1,\,0.5,\,0.25)\) and \(W=12\).

  1. Compute \(c_1\), \(c_2\) and \(c_3\).
  2. Double every \(a_i\). What changes? What does that imply for normalizing the continuous-time weights \(e^{-(\theta+p)(z-t)}\)?
Show solution

(a) The shares sum to one, so \(R_ic_i = a_iW\): \(c_1 = 6\), \(c_2 = 4/0.5 = 8\), \(c_3 = 2/0.25 = 8\). (b) Nothing: only \(a_i/\sum a_k\) matters. In continuous time the weights integrate to \(1/(\theta+p)\), which is why \(c(t) = (\theta+p)(v+h)\). Dividing by the total weight is the same as multiplying by \(\theta+p\).

Exercise 17.4 Proof PV budget ⇔ ODE + boundary

Show that if \(v(\cdot)\) satisfies \(\dot v = (r+p)v + y - c\) and \(\lim_{T\to\infty}R(t,T)v(T)=0\), then \(\int_t^\infty[c-y]R(t,z)dz = v(t)\). (Hint: compute \(\frac{d}{dz}\big[R(t,z)v(z)\big]\).)

Show solution

\(\partial_zR(t,z) = -(r(z)+p)R(t,z)\), so \(\frac{d}{dz}[R(t,z)v(z)] = R(t,z)[\dot v - (r+p)v] = R(t,z)[y(z)-c(z)]\). Integrating from \(t\) to \(T\): \(R(t,T)v(T) - v(t) = \int_t^T[y-c]R\,dz\). Letting \(T\to\infty\) and using the boundary condition gives \(v(t) = \int_t^\infty[c-y]R\,dz\). ∎ The converse direction is §6.2.

Exercise 17.5 Exam-style A life-cycle calculation

An agent is born with \(v=0\) and earns constant \(y=1\). The interest rate is constant at \(r=0.03\), \(\theta=0.04\) and \(p=0.02\).

  1. Compute human wealth and consumption at birth.
  2. Find financial wealth at age 50 (closed form, then a number).
  3. Verify the boundary condition \(\lim_{x\to\infty}e^{-(r+p)x}v(x)=0\). Is this agent a saver or a borrower, and how do lenders get repaid?
Show solution

(a) \(h = 1/(r+p) = 20\) and \(c_0 = (\theta+p)h = 0.06\cdot 20 = 1.2\). (b) \(v(x) = h\big(e^{(r-\theta)x}-1\big)\), so \(v(50) = 20(e^{-0.5}-1)\approx -7.87\). (c) \(e^{-0.05x}\cdot 20(e^{-0.01x}-1)\to 0\). Since \(r<\theta\) the agent is a borrower, and debt tends to \(-20 = -h\). Consumption exceeds income until age \(\ln(1.2)/0.01\approx 18\), so debt builds up. After that \(c<y\), and the surplus services part of the interest-plus-premium \((r+p)|v|\), so debt keeps growing but converges to \(-h\). Debt outstanding at death is extinguished by the life-insurance component, which the premium \(p\) pays for.

Exercise 17.6 Exam-style Unbounded human wealth

Suppose \(y\) is constant and \(r+p\le0\). Show that \(h=\infty\) and explain why the household problem has no solution. Can the equilibrium interest rate in this model be negative? What bound must it respect?

Show solution

\(h = \int_t^\infty y\,e^{-(r+p)(z-t)}dz\) diverges when \(r+p\le0\). With infinite human wealth, any consumption plan can be raised further while remaining "affordable," so the supremum is not attained. Negative \(r\) is allowed, but it must satisfy \(r>-p\) with flat income. With income declining at rate α (Lesson 18) the bound becomes \(r>-(p+\alpha)\) BY slide 18.

Exercise 17.7 Proof CRRA instead of log

Replace \(\log c\) with \(c^{1-\sigma}/(1-\sigma)\). Derive the Euler equation. Does \(p\) still cancel? Is the MPC still independent of the interest-rate path?

Show solution

The FOC is \(e^{-(\theta+p)(z-t)}c(z)^{-\sigma} = \lambda R(t,z)\). Taking logs and differentiating, \(\dot c/c = (r-\theta)/\sigma\), so \(p\) still cancels. But the MPC is no longer independent of interest rates. With constant \(r\), \(c(z)=c(t)e^{(r-\theta)(z-t)/\sigma}\). Substituting into \(\int_t^\infty c(z)e^{-(r+p)(z-t)}dz = v+h\) gives $$c(t) = \Big[(r+p) - \tfrac{r-\theta}{\sigma}\Big](v+h),$$ which equals \((\theta+p)(v+h)\) only when \(\sigma=1\). With time-varying \(r\), the MPC depends on the whole future path of rates. This is why Blanchard uses log utility: it keeps the MPC common and constant, so individual consumption functions aggregate.

Key takeaways

  • Constant death hazard \(p\): cohort size \(pe^{-p(t-s)}\), population 1, everyone expects \(1/p\) more years.
  • Fair annuities pay \(r+p\). Survivors inherit the wealth of the dead via insurers.
  • Expected utility with death = discounting at \(\theta+p\).
  • Log utility gives \(c=(\theta+p)(v+h)\), with \(h=\int y\,R\) and \(R(t,z)=e^{-\int_t^z(r+p)}\).
  • Sequential form: \(\dot v=(r+p)v+y-c\), \(\dot h=(r+p)h-y\), plus \(\lim R(t,T)v(T)=0\) (no unspent wealth, no Ponzi).
  • Euler equation \(\dot c=(r-\theta)c\): mortality raises both impatience and the return by \(p\), so it cancels.
