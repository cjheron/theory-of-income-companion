OLG with Capital: Social Security and Capital Accumulation

Covers Class Note III, pp. 15–19 · About 40 min · Prerequisites: Lesson 11, Lesson 14
By the end you can:
  1. explain the timing of capital, rental rates and interest rates, and derive \(r_t = F_K(K_{t+1},1)-\delta\);
  2. show that household budgets, the government budget and goods-market clearing imply \(B_t + s_t = K_{t+1}\) (Walras' law with capital);
  3. sign the effect of pay-as-you-go (PAYG) social security on saving and on the steady-state capital stock, and explain why the sign flips at an unstable steady state;
  4. work through the Cobb–Douglas example: show that generation 1 can gain even when \(r>0\), and that steady-state generations lose exactly when \(r>0\).
Primary source Alvarez, Class Note III, pp. 15–19. The model with capital is due to Diamond, P. (1965), “National Debt in a Neoclassical Growth Model,” American Economic Review 55(5): 1126–1150 (free PDF from the AEA). Sections I–III are the relevant part.
Notation alert
  • In this lesson \(\alpha\) is the capital share in \(F(K,L)=K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}\). It is not an endowment parameter as in Class Note II (Lesson 9).
  • Households now have one unit of labour when young and none when old. Their income is the wage \(w_t\), not an endowment of goods.
  • The PAYG tax is \(\tau\): \(\tau^t_t=\tau\) when young, \(\tau^t_{t+1}=-\tau\) when old. The privatization slides (Lesson 14) called the same object \(\theta\).
  • \(r_t\) is indexed by the date the saving is made: it is the return from \(t\) to \(t+1\).

1. From endowments to production

So far the OLG economy had no way to move goods through time except trade between generations. Now we add a storable, productive asset: capital. One generation is born each period, of constant size one (no population growth). There is a constant-returns production function \(F(K,L)\), and capital depreciates at rate \(\delta\) III p.15:

$$K_{t+1} = I_t + (1-\delta)K_t, \qquad g_t + I_t + C_t = F(K_t, L_t), \qquad C_t = c^t_t + c^{t-1}_t .$$

Labour is supplied inelastically by the young, so \(L_t = 1\) throughout.

2. Factor prices and the timing of the interest rate

A competitive firm rents capital at rental rate \(v_t\) and hires labour at wage \(w_t\). With constant returns and price-taking,

$$w_t = F_L(K_t, L_t), \qquad v_t = F_K(K_t, L_t).$$

Now track one unit of goods saved by a young household at date \(t\). There are two ways to carry it to \(t+1\):

Asset bought at \(t\)Payoff at \(t+1\)
Bond (government debt or a private loan)\(1 + r_t\)
One unit of capital, installed for production at \(t+1\)rental \(v_{t+1}\) + undepreciated capital \(1-\delta\)

If both are held in equilibrium, no-arbitrage requires equal payoffs: \(1 + r_t = v_{t+1} + 1 - \delta\), i.e. \(v_{t+1} = r_t + \delta\). With \(L_{t+1}=1\):

Interest rate with capital III p.15
$$r_t = F_K(K_{t+1}, 1) - \delta .$$

The interest rate between \(t\) and \(t+1\) depends on next period's capital stock. That stock is exactly what today's young save.

This timing is what makes the equilibrium law of motion implicit (§5). Savers' return depends on how much they save in total.

3. Households

Generation \(t\) earns \(w_t\) when young, pays \(\tau^t_t\) when young and \(\tau^t_{t+1}\) when old, and saves \(s_t\):

$$s_t + c^t_t = w_t - \tau^t_t, \qquad c^t_{t+1} = (1+r_t)s_t - \tau^t_{t+1}.$$

Eliminating \(s_t\) gives the lifetime budget constraint III p.15:

$$c^t_t + \frac{c^t_{t+1}}{1+r_t} = w_t - \tau^t_t - \frac{\tau^t_{t+1}}{1+r_t} \;\equiv\; W_t .$$

Optimal saving depends on three things: the interest rate, first-period disposable income, and lifetime wealth \(W_t\). Alvarez writes

$$s_t = s\!\left(r_t,\; w_t - \tau^t_t,\; w_t - \tau^t_t - \frac{\tau^t_{t+1}}{1+r_t}\right).$$

The second argument matters because saving is disposable first-period income minus first-period consumption: \(s = (w-\tau^t_t) - c_y(r, W)\).

4. Walras' law with capital

With a government that issues assets \(B_t\) (\(B_t<0\) is debt) and buys \(g_t\), the government budget is \(B_t + g_t = (1+r_{t-1})B_{t-1} + \tau^t_t + \tau^{t-1}_t\), as in III p.3. Household saving now has two uses: lending to the government and holding capital.

Asset-market clearing is implied III p.16

If household budgets, the government budget and goods-market clearing hold at every date, and \(B_0 + s_0 = K_1\), then $$B_t + s_t = K_{t+1}\quad\text{for all } t\ge 1 .$$

Proof (the slides only assert this step)
  1. Add the two household budgets alive at \(t\). Young: \(s_t + c^t_t = w_t - \tau^t_t\). Old: \(c^{t-1}_t = (1+r_{t-1})s_{t-1} - \tau^{t-1}_t\). Summing, $$s_t + c^t_t + c^{t-1}_t = w_t + (1+r_{t-1})s_{t-1} - \tau^t_t - \tau^{t-1}_t .$$
  2. Use goods-market clearing. \(c^t_t + c^{t-1}_t = F(K_t,1) - g_t - K_{t+1} + (1-\delta)K_t\).
  3. Use constant returns (Euler's theorem). \(F(K_t,1) = w_t + v_t K_t = w_t + (r_{t-1}+\delta)K_t\). Output is exhausted by factor payments, so firms make zero profit. Substituting steps 2 and 3 into step 1, the \(w_t\) and \(\delta K_t\) terms cancel: $$s_t - K_{t+1} + \tau^t_t + \tau^{t-1}_t - g_t = (1+r_{t-1})(s_{t-1} - K_t).$$
  4. Add the government budget written as \(B_t - \tau^t_t - \tau^{t-1}_t + g_t = (1+r_{t-1})B_{t-1}\): $$\big(B_t + s_t - K_{t+1}\big) = (1+r_{t-1})\big(B_{t-1} + s_{t-1} - K_t\big).$$
  5. Induction. The term in brackets is zero at \(t=0\), so it is zero for all \(t\).
∎
Intuition

The young are the only savers. Whatever they don't lend to the government must end up as next period's capital. This is the same accounting as in Lesson 14, with \(K_{t+1}\) replacing zero on the right-hand side.

5. The equilibrium law of motion

With no taxes, government spending or debt (\(\tau=g=B=0\)), asset-market clearing becomes \(s_t = K_{t+1}\). Substituting factor prices III p.16:

$$K_{t+1} = s\big(F_K(K_{t+1},1) - \delta,\; F_L(K_t,1),\; F_L(K_t,1)\big).$$

Given \(K_1>0\), owned by the initial old, this equation pins down \(K_2\), then \(K_3\), and so on. It is implicit because \(K_{t+1}\) appears on both sides through \(r_t\). By the implicit function theorem it defines a map \(K_{t+1} = s^*(K_t)\) locally, provided

$$1 - s_r\,F_{KK}(K_{t+1},1) \neq 0 .$$

Since \(F_{KK}<0\), this holds automatically whenever saving is non-decreasing in \(r\) (\(s_r\ge 0\)). The slides' "local conditions in the problem set" amount to this denominator being positive.

6. Social security lowers saving, and (usually) capital

Introduce PAYG: \(\tau^t_t = \tau\), \(\tau^t_{t+1} = -\tau\). Each old person receives the tax paid by the one young person alive. Holding \(r_t\) and \(w_t\) fixed, lifetime wealth changes by III p.17

$$\frac{\partial W_t}{\partial \tau} = -\Big(1 - \frac{1}{1+r_t}\Big) = -\frac{r_t}{1+r_t} < 0 \quad\text{when } r_t>0 .$$

The household pays \(\tau\) today and gets back \(\tau\) tomorrow, which is a zero net return. Saving privately would have earned \(r_t>0\).

Claim: saving falls with \(\tau\)

If old-age consumption is a normal good and \(r_t>0\), then at fixed prices \(\partial s_t/\partial\tau < -\tfrac{1}{1+r_t} < 0\).

Proof
  1. From the old-age budget, \(c_o = (1+r)s + \tau\), so \(s = \dfrac{c_o(r,W) - \tau}{1+r}\).
  2. Differentiate: \(\dfrac{\partial s}{\partial\tau} = \dfrac{1}{1+r}\Big(\dfrac{\partial c_o}{\partial W}\dfrac{\partial W}{\partial\tau} - 1\Big)\).
  3. Normality gives \(\partial c_o/\partial W > 0\), and \(\partial W/\partial\tau = -r/(1+r)<0\), so the term in brackets is less than \(-1\). ∎

The economics: PAYG directly replaces private saving for retirement, since the transfer \(\tau\) arrives when old anyway, and the wealth loss reduces old-age consumption further. Both forces cut \(s\).

From saving to the capital stock

In general equilibrium, write the law of motion with the policy as \(K_{t+1} = s^*(K_t,\tau)\). Applying the implicit function theorem to \(K' - s\big(F_K(K',1)-\delta,\,\cdot\,\big) = 0\) gives

$$\frac{\partial s^*}{\partial\tau} = \frac{s_\tau}{1 - s_r F_{KK}} < 0,$$

using the claim and the positive denominator from §5. A steady state solves \(K^* = s^*(K^*,\tau)\). Differentiate both sides with respect to \(\tau\):

$$\frac{dK^*}{d\tau} = \frac{\partial s^*}{\partial K}\frac{dK^*}{d\tau} + \frac{\partial s^*}{\partial \tau} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\frac{dK^*}{d\tau} = \frac{\partial s^*/\partial\tau}{1 - \partial s^*/\partial K}} .$$
Steady-state comparative statics III p.17
  • If \(0<\partial s^*/\partial K<1\), the steady state is stable: the map crosses the 45° line from above. Then \(dK^*/d\tau<0\): social security lowers steady-state capital.
  • If \(\partial s^*/\partial K > 1\), the steady state is unstable: the map crosses from below. Then \(dK^*/d\tau>0\). The sign flips, but this steady state is never reached from nearby initial conditions.

Graphically, a higher \(\tau\) shifts the map \(s^*(\cdot,\tau)\) down. Where the map crosses the 45° line from above, the crossing moves left. Where it crosses from below, the crossing moves right. The figure below shows both cases at once.

7. A Cobb–Douglas example

Let \(F(K,L) = K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}\) and suppose consumers care only about old-age consumption III p.18. Then the young save everything they have after tax, \(s_t = w_t - \tau\), and saving does not depend on \(r_t\). That makes the law of motion explicit:

  1. Wage. \(w_t = F_L(K_t,1) = (1-\alpha)K_t^\alpha\).
  2. Law of motion. \(K_{t+1} = s_t = (1-\alpha)K_t^\alpha - \tau\).
  3. Steady state without social security. \(K^* = (1-\alpha)K^{*\alpha}\), so \(K^{*\,1-\alpha} = 1-\alpha\) and $$K^* = (1-\alpha)^{1/(1-\alpha)} .$$ A fact used repeatedly below: \(K^{*\,\alpha-1} = \dfrac{1}{1-\alpha}\). Also \(w^* = (1-\alpha)K^{*\alpha} = K^*\), since the young save the whole wage.
  4. Stability. The slope of the map at \(K^*\) is \(\alpha(1-\alpha)K^{*\,\alpha-1} = \alpha\in(0,1)\), so \(K^*\) is stable.
  5. Steady-state interest rate. \(r^* = \alpha K^{*\,\alpha-1} - \delta = \dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} - \delta\).

Introducing PAYG at \(K_1 = K^*\): who gains?

Generation 0 (initial old) owns \(K_1\), whose return is already fixed, and receives \(\tau\) from the young. It gains \(\tau\).

Generation 1 earns \(w_1 = K^*\), pays \(\tau\) and saves \(K_2 = K^* - \tau\). When old it receives the gross return on its capital plus the transfer from generation 2:

$$c^1_2(\tau) = (K^*-\tau)\big[1 - \delta + \alpha (K^*-\tau)^{\alpha-1}\big] + \tau .$$

Differentiate and evaluate at \(\tau=0\):

  1. Write \(K_2 = K^*-\tau\), so \(dK_2/d\tau=-1\), and \(c^1_2 = K_2\big[1-\delta+\alpha K_2^{\alpha-1}\big] + \tau\). By the product rule, \(\dfrac{d}{dK_2}\Big(K_2\big[1-\delta+\alpha K_2^{\alpha-1}\big]\Big) = 1-\delta+\alpha K_2^{\alpha-1} + \alpha(\alpha-1)K_2^{\alpha-1}\). Hence $$\frac{dc^1_2}{d\tau} = -\big[1-\delta+\alpha K_2^{\alpha-1}\big] - \alpha(\alpha-1)K_2^{\alpha-1} + 1 .$$
  2. Collect terms: \(= \delta - \alpha K_2^{\alpha-1} + \alpha(1-\alpha)K_2^{\alpha-1} = \delta - \alpha^2 K_2^{\alpha-1}\).
  3. At \(\tau=0\), \(K_2 = K^*\) and \(K^{*\,\alpha-1}=1/(1-\alpha)\): $$\boxed{\left.\frac{dc^1_2}{d\tau}\right|_{\tau=0} = \delta - \frac{\alpha^2}{1-\alpha}} .$$

So for \(\delta>0\), generation 1 gains when \(\alpha\) is small enough, namely when \(\alpha^2/(1-\alpha) < \delta\) III p.18.

Why can generation 1 gain? III p.19

In the pure-endowment economy, social security only reshuffles goods; nothing about production changes. With capital, taxing the young lowers \(K_2\), which raises the marginal product of capital \(\alpha K_2^{\alpha-1}\). Generation 1 is the owner of \(K_2\). It loses \(1+r\) per unit of foregone saving but gets \(\tau\) back from generation 2, and every remaining unit earns more. The term \(-\alpha(\alpha-1)K_2^{\alpha-1} = \alpha(1-\alpha)K_2^{\alpha-1}>0\) in step 1 is exactly this price effect. It is a pecuniary transfer from generation 2, whose wage \(w_2 = (1-\alpha)K_2^\alpha\) is lower.

But steady-state generations lose whenever \(r^*>0\)

Let \(K(\tau)\) be the stable steady state of \(K = (1-\alpha)K^\alpha - \tau\). A steady-state generation consumes \(c_{ss}(\tau) = K(\tau)\big[1-\delta+\alpha K(\tau)^{\alpha-1}\big] + \tau\).

  1. Implicit differentiation of the steady state at \(\tau=0\): \(K' = \alpha(1-\alpha)K^{\alpha-1}K' - 1 = \alpha K' - 1\), so \(K'(0) = -\dfrac{1}{1-\alpha}\).
  2. Differentiate \(c_{ss}\): \(c_{ss}' = \big[1-\delta+\alpha K^{\alpha-1} + \alpha(\alpha-1)K^{\alpha-1}\big]K' + 1 = \big[1+r^* - \alpha\big]K' + 1\), using \(\alpha(\alpha-1)K^{*\,\alpha-1}=-\alpha\).
  3. Substitute \(K'(0)\): \(c_{ss}'(0) = 1 - \dfrac{1 + r^* - \alpha}{1-\alpha} = \dfrac{(1-\alpha) - (1+r^*-\alpha)}{1-\alpha}\), so $$\boxed{c_{ss}'(0) = -\frac{r^*}{1-\alpha}} .$$

Steady-state generations gain from PAYG if and only if \(r^*<0\). That is the dynamic-inefficiency condition of Lesson 12, with no population growth. The gain to generation 1 is a transition effect. When \(r^*>0\), the CE has finite aggregate value and the First Welfare Theorem applies (Lesson 12). So no feasible reallocation, PAYG included, can make every generation better off: someone, here the generations far in the future, must lose III p.19.

Interactive: who gains from PAYG? Old-age consumption as a function of the tax \(\tau\), starting from \(K_1 = K^*\). Blue: generation 1, \(c^1_2(\tau)\). Orange: a generation in the new stable steady state, \(c_{ss}(\tau)\). Try: \(\alpha=0.2\), \(\delta=0.1\). Here \(r^*=0.15>0\), yet the blue curve rises at first while the orange curve falls. Then raise \(\delta\) above \(\alpha/(1-\alpha)\), so \(r^*<0\): now both rise, and PAYG is a Pareto improvement for small \(\tau\).

8. Check your understanding

1. Why does \(r_t\) depend on \(K_{t+1}\) rather than \(K_t\)?
  • Because the old own the capital stock at date t
  • Because saving at t becomes capital used at t+1
  • Because the wage at t depends only on capital K_t
  • Because depreciation happens before the production
A unit saved at \(t\) is installed and earns the rental \(v_{t+1} = F_K(K_{t+1},1)\) at \(t+1\), plus \(1-\delta\). No-arbitrage with bonds gives \(1+r_t = F_K(K_{t+1},1)+1-\delta\).
2. Which property of \(F\) is used to derive \(B_t + s_t = K_{t+1}\)?
  • Inada conditions at zero and infinity for capital
  • Strict concavity of F in capital, holding labour fixed
  • Constant returns, so factor payments exhaust output
  • A capital share that is constant over all of time
Euler's theorem \(F = w L + vK\) means there are no profits left over. With decreasing returns, profits would appear and someone would have to own the firms, adding a term to household budgets.
3. Raising \(\tau\) shifts the map \(s^*(\cdot,\tau)\) down. At a steady state where \(\partial s^*/\partial K > 1\), what happens?
  • K* rises, but that steady state is unstable anyway
  • K* falls, just as it does at the stable steady state
  • K* is unchanged because the slope exceeds one there
  • The steady state disappears for any positive τ value
\(dK^*/d\tau = s^*_\tau/(1-s^*_K)\). With \(s^*_\tau<0\) and \(s^*_K>1\), the denominator is negative, so \(dK^*/d\tau>0\). Visually, the map crosses the 45° line from below, so shifting it down moves the crossing right. Check the hollow point in the first figure.
4. In the Cobb–Douglas example with \(r^*>0\), generation 1 can gain from PAYG. Why is this consistent with the First Welfare Theorem?
  • Because the First Welfare Theorem fails with capital
  • Because generation 0 loses the transfer it receives
  • Because preferences in the example are not convex
  • Because later steady-state generations lose instead
With \(r^*>0\) the equilibrium is Pareto optimal, so PAYG cannot be a Pareto improvement. It helps generations 0 and 1 at the expense of the generations far in the future: \(c_{ss}'(0) = -r^*/(1-\alpha) < 0\).

9. Exercises

Exercise 15.1 Slides III p.15

Suppose instead that households own capital directly, rent it to firms, and sell the undepreciated stock to the next young generation. Show that the return on this strategy equals \(F_K(K_{t+1},1)+1-\delta\). Conclude that if the government also issued bonds paying \(1+r_t > F_K(K_{t+1},1)+1-\delta\), no one would hold capital. What would that imply for \(K_{t+1}\) and for \(F_K\)?

Show solution

Buy one unit of capital at \(t\). At \(t+1\), rent it for \(v_{t+1}=F_K(K_{t+1},1)\), then sell the \(1-\delta\) that remains (one-for-one with goods, since installed capital and goods trade at par). The gross return is \(F_K(K_{t+1},1)+1-\delta\). If bonds paid strictly more, every saver would hold only bonds and \(K_{t+1}=0\). But then \(F_K(0,1) = \infty\) under Inada conditions, which contradicts the inequality. So in equilibrium with \(K_{t+1}>0\), the returns must be equal. This is the content of \(v_{t+1}=r_t+\delta\).

Exercise 15.2 Proof

Redo the Walras' law argument of §4 when \(F\) has decreasing returns, with profits \(\pi_t = F(K_t,1)-w_t - v_tK_t>0\) paid to the young. Show that the conclusion \(B_t+s_t=K_{t+1}\) survives once household budgets include \(\pi_t\).

Show solution

The young budget becomes \(s_t+c^t_t = w_t+\pi_t-\tau^t_t\). In step 3, \(F(K_t,1) = w_t + \pi_t + (r_{t-1}+\delta)K_t\). The extra \(\pi_t\) appears on both sides and cancels. Everything else is unchanged, so the same recursion and the same induction apply. The point: Walras' law needs every unit of output to be someone's income, which is the role of \(\sum_i\theta^i_j=1\) in Lesson 2.

Exercise 15.3 Slides III p.17

The claim in §6 assumed \(r_t>0\). Suppose instead \(r_t<0\), so PAYG raises lifetime wealth. Show that if both \(c_y\) and \(c_o\) are normal, saving still falls with \(\tau\) at fixed prices.

Hint

From the lifetime budget \(c_y + c_o/(1+r) = W\), normality of \(c_y\) gives \(\partial c_o/\partial W \le 1+r\).

Show solution

Again \(\partial s/\partial\tau = \frac{1}{1+r}\big(c_{o,W}\cdot(-\tfrac{r}{1+r}) - 1\big)\). With \(r<0\), the first term is positive, but \(c_{o,W}\le 1+r\) implies \(c_{o,W}\cdot\frac{-r}{1+r} \le -r\). So the bracket is at most \(-r-1 = -(1+r) < 0\). Saving falls. Intuitively, the transfer received when old directly substitutes for private saving one-for-one. The wealth effect can offset at most a fraction of that.

Exercise 15.4 Exam-style (Diamond model with log utility)

Generation \(t\) has utility \((1-\beta)\log c^t_t + \beta\log c^t_{t+1}\), supplies one unit of labour when young, and \(F(K,L)=K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}\) with full depreciation (\(\delta=1\)). There is no government.

  1. Show that \(s_t = \beta w_t\), independent of \(r_t\), and that \(K_{t+1} = \beta(1-\alpha)K_t^\alpha\).
  2. Find the positive steady state \(\bar K\) and the steady-state interest rate \(\bar r\). For \(\beta = 0.5\), \(\alpha = 0.3\):
  3. Is this steady state dynamically inefficient? Give the condition on \((\alpha,\beta)\) in general.
  4. Now let the population of young grow at rate \(n\), so that per-young capital satisfies \((1+n)k_{t+1} = s_t\). Find \(1+\bar r\) and show that \(\bar r < n\) if and only if \(\alpha<\beta(1-\alpha)\), whatever \(n\) is.
  5. Introduce PAYG with tax \(\tau\) per young (and \(n=0\)). Write the new law of motion. (You may leave it implicit.)
Show solution

(a) Lifetime wealth is \(w_t\); log utility gives \(c^t_t = (1-\beta)w_t\), so \(s_t = \beta w_t\). With \(\delta=1\) and \(s_t = K_{t+1}\), \(K_{t+1} = \beta(1-\alpha)K_t^\alpha\).
(b) \(\bar K = (\beta(1-\alpha))^{1/(1-\alpha)} = 0.35^{1/0.7}\approx 0.2232\). \(\bar r = \alpha\bar K^{\alpha-1} - 1 = \dfrac{\alpha}{\beta(1-\alpha)} - 1 = 0.3/0.35 - 1 = -1/7\).
(c) With \(n=0\), the golden rule is \(F_K - \delta = 0\), i.e. \(r=0\). Here \(\bar r<0\), so capital exceeds the golden rule: the steady state is dynamically inefficient. In general, \(\bar r<0 \iff \alpha < \beta(1-\alpha)\).
(d) \(\bar k = \big(\beta(1-\alpha)/(1+n)\big)^{1/(1-\alpha)}\), so \(1+\bar r = \alpha\bar k^{\alpha-1} = \dfrac{\alpha(1+n)}{\beta(1-\alpha)}\). Hence \(1+\bar r<1+n \iff \alpha<\beta(1-\alpha)\). With full depreciation the condition is independent of \(n\). (For \(\beta=0.5\), \(\alpha=0.3\), \(n=0.2\): \(\bar r\approx 0.029 < 0.2\).)
(e) Lifetime wealth is \(w_t - \tau + \tau/(1+r_t)\), so \(c_y = (1-\beta)W_t\) and \(s_t = w_t-\tau-c_y = \beta(w_t-\tau) - (1-\beta)\tau/(1+r_t)\). Then $$K_{t+1} = \beta\big((1-\alpha)K_t^\alpha - \tau\big) - \frac{(1-\beta)\tau}{\alpha K_{t+1}^{\alpha-1}} .$$ Saving is lower for every \(K_t\). This is the implicit law of motion of §5.

Exercise 15.5 Exam-style (Cobb–Douglas example)

In the example of §7, let \(\alpha = 0.2\) and \(\delta=0.1\).

  1. Compute \(K^*\) and \(r^*\).
  2. Compute \(dc^1_2/d\tau\) at \(\tau=0\). Does generation 1 gain?
  3. Compute \(c_{ss}'(0)\) for the steady-state generations.
  4. Who, besides generation 1, gains? Explain in one sentence why this does not contradict the First Welfare Theorem.
Show solution

(a) \(K^* = 0.8^{1.25}\approx 0.7566\) and \(r^* = 0.2/0.8 - 0.1 = 0.15\).
(b) \(\delta - \alpha^2/(1-\alpha) = 0.1 - 0.04/0.8 = 0.05 > 0\): generation 1 gains.
(c) \(-r^*/(1-\alpha) = -0.15/0.8 = -0.1875\): steady-state generations lose.
(d) Generation 0 gains \(\tau\). Because \(r^*>0\), the value of the aggregate endowment is finite and the First Welfare Theorem applies. The pre-reform equilibrium is Pareto optimal, so gains for early generations must be paid for by losses for later ones.

Exercise 15.6 Slides III p.18

In the same example, explain why generation 1's gain is larger when \(\alpha\) is smaller, even though a smaller \(\alpha\) makes capital less important in production. Which term in \(dc^1_2/d\tau\) does \(\alpha\) control?

Show solution

\(dc^1_2/d\tau = \underbrace{-(1+r^*)}_{\text{lost return}} + \underbrace{\alpha(1-\alpha)K^{*\,\alpha-1}}_{\text{price effect}} + \underbrace{1}_{\text{transfer}} = \delta - \alpha^2/(1-\alpha)\). The price effect equals \(\alpha\), but the lost return \(1+r^*=1-\delta+\alpha/(1-\alpha)\) also rises with \(\alpha\). The net cost \(\alpha/(1-\alpha) - \alpha = \alpha^2/(1-\alpha)\) is increasing in \(\alpha\). With a small capital share, the steady-state return to saving is low, so giving up saving costs little, and a zero-return PAYG system competes well. When \(\delta > \alpha/(1-\alpha)\), so that \(r^*<0\), PAYG beats saving outright.

Key takeaways

  • Timing: \(r_t = F_K(K_{t+1},1)-\delta\). Today's saving determines tomorrow's return, so the law of motion \(K_{t+1}=s(r(K_{t+1}),w(K_t),\dots)\) is implicit.
  • Walras' law with capital: \(B_t + s_t = K_{t+1}\). It needs constant returns (or profits paid out) so that every unit of output is someone's income.
  • PAYG lowers saving (if \(c_o\) is normal), and lowers the stable steady-state capital stock: \(dK^*/d\tau = s^*_\tau/(1-s^*_K)\).
  • Cobb–Douglas example: generation 1 gains iff \(\delta>\alpha^2/(1-\alpha)\), through a higher return on scarcer capital. Steady-state generations gain iff \(r^*<0\). With \(r^*>0\), the First Welfare Theorem rules out a Pareto improvement.
