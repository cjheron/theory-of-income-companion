When Aggregation Fails, and How to Rescue It

Notation alert

The slide footer says "Class Notes III", but this is folder 3 (Aggregation, Social Planner), not Class Note III on OLG extensions (Lessons 14–16). In this lesson:

  • \(\theta^i\in\mathbb R^m\) is a household-specific translation vector: a subsistence point (Stone–Geary) or a satiation point. In Class Note III, \(\theta\) was the PAYG tax, and in Blanchard–Yaari it was the discount rate. Here \(\bar\theta\equiv\sum_i\theta^i\).
  • \(\lambda_i\) are Pareto weights, \(\gamma_l\) is the planner's multiplier on good \(l\), and \(\mu_i\) is agent \(i\)'s marginal utility of income. From Lesson 23, \(\lambda_i=\kappa/\mu_i\) and \(\gamma=\kappa p\).
  • \(\delta^i\) is a share (of the endowment, or of the translated aggregate). \(\beta^i_l\) are taste weights, not discount factors. On slide 33, \(\rho\) and \(\beta\) are curvature parameters.

1. The aggregation test

Lesson 24 proved that if every household has \(u^i=g^i(h(x))\) with a common HD1 function \(h\), then relative prices are \(p_l/p_k=h_l(\bar e)/h_k(\bar e)\), whatever the distribution of wealth. Both hypotheses in that sentence, identical and homothetic, do real work. This lesson shows what happens when each one is dropped, and how a change of origin rescues a large class of non-homothetic, non-identical preferences.

All examples use the same recipe. It is the skeleton of every past exam question on this note.

Recipe: is there aggregation? N3 p.31
  1. Planner FOC. For an interior optimum with \(\lambda\gg0\): \(\lambda_i\,\partial u^i(x^i)/\partial x_l=\gamma_l\) for every \(i,l\).
  2. Invert. Solve each FOC for \(x^i_l\) as a function of \(\lambda_i\) and \(\gamma_l\).
  3. Aggregate. Sum over \(i\) and impose feasibility \(\sum_ix^i_l=\bar e_l\). Solve for \(\gamma_l(\lambda)\).
  4. Test. If \(\gamma_l/\gamma_k\) does not depend on \(\lambda\), there is aggregation: the shadow prices depend only on aggregates. Since CE prices equal \(\gamma\) for the weights \(\lambda_i=1/\mu_i\) (Lesson 23), equilibrium relative prices are then independent of the distribution of endowments. If \(\gamma_l/\gamma_k\) does depend on \(\lambda\), the CE relative prices generally move with the wealth distribution.

The logic of step 4 deserves a sentence. Every CE is a planner optimum for some weights, and redistributing endowments moves the supporting weights. If the planner's relative shadow values are the same for all weights, redistribution cannot move relative prices. If they differ across weights, it generically does. Section 2 shows this directly from the CE in one case.

2. Counterexample 1: homothetic, but not identical

Let household \(i\) have Cobb–Douglas preferences with its own taste weights N3 p.31:

$$u^i(x^i)=\sum_{l=1}^m\beta^i_l\log x^i_l,\qquad \beta^i_l>0,\quad\sum_l\beta^i_l=1 .$$

Each \(u^i\) is homothetic: it is a strictly increasing transformation of the HD1 function \(\prod_l x_l^{\beta^i_l}\). But the HD1 function differs across households.

Derivation (planner side)
  1. FOC. \(\lambda_i\,\beta^i_l/x^i_l=\gamma_l\).
  2. Invert. \(x^i_l=\lambda_i\beta^i_l/\gamma_l\).
  3. Aggregate. \(\bar e_l=\sum_ix^i_l=\frac{1}{\gamma_l}\sum_i\lambda_i\beta^i_l\), so $$\gamma_l=\frac{\sum_i\lambda_i\beta^i_l}{\bar e_l},\qquad x^i_l=\frac{\lambda_i\beta^i_l}{\sum_j\lambda_j\beta^j_l}\,\bar e_l .$$
  4. Test. $$\frac{\gamma_l}{\gamma_k}=\frac{\bar e_k}{\bar e_l}\cdot\frac{\sum_i\lambda_i\beta^i_l}{\sum_i\lambda_i\beta^i_k}.$$ The second factor is a ratio of two \(\lambda\)-weighted averages of tastes. It is independent of \(\lambda\) iff \(\beta^i_l/\beta^i_k\) is the same for every \(i\). Since each \(\beta^i\) sums to one, that means \(\beta^i=\beta\) for all \(i\): identical preferences. In that case \(\gamma_l/\gamma_k=\beta_l\bar e_k/(\beta_k\bar e_l)\), as in Lesson 24.

The same failure, seen in the competitive equilibrium

Let agent \(i\) have wealth \(w^i=p\cdot e^i\). Cobb–Douglas demand is \(x^i_l=\beta^i_lw^i/p_l\). Market clearing for good \(l\) gives

$$p_l\bar e_l=\sum_i\beta^i_l\,w^i .$$

The value of good \(l\) equals the total spending on it, and that is a wealth-weighted average of the taste weights. Take even proportional endowments \(e^i=\delta^i\bar e\), so that \(w^i=\delta^i\,p\cdot\bar e\). Then

$$\frac{p_l\bar e_l}{p_k\bar e_k}=\frac{\sum_i\delta^i\beta^i_l}{\sum_i\delta^i\beta^i_k},$$

which moves with the distribution \(\delta\). Moving wealth toward households that like good \(l\) raises its relative price. The two formulas match exactly. With log utility and \(\sum_l\beta^i_l=1\), the marginal utility of income is \(\mu_i=1/w^i\), so the Negishi weight is \(\lambda_i=1/\mu_i=w^i\propto\delta^i\).

Lesson: homotheticity alone is not enough N3 p.32

Each household's Engel curves are rays from the origin, so each spends a fixed share of a marginal dollar on each good. But the shares differ across households. A transfer from \(j\) to \(i\) therefore changes the composition of aggregate demand, and relative prices must adjust. What exact aggregation needs is a single preference ordering \(h\) shared by everyone, up to individual increasing transformations \(g^i\).

3. Counterexample 2: identical, but not homothetic

Now give everyone the same preferences, \(I=m=2\) N3 p.33:

$$u^i(x_1,x_2)=\frac{x_1^{1-\rho}}{1-\rho}+\frac{x_2^{1-\beta}}{1-\beta},\qquad\rho,\beta>0,\quad\rho\ne\beta .$$

(Read \(\frac{x^{1-1}}{1-1}\) as \(\log x\).) Good 1 has curvature \(\rho\) and good 2 has curvature \(\beta\). These preferences are not homothetic: along a ray \(x=t\bar x\), the MRS \(\frac{x_1^{-\rho}}{x_2^{-\beta}}\) scales like \(t^{\beta-\rho}\), so it changes with \(t\) unless \(\rho=\beta\).

Derivation
  1. FOCs. \(\lambda_i(x^i_1)^{-\rho}=\gamma_1\) and \(\lambda_i(x^i_2)^{-\beta}=\gamma_2\).
  2. Invert. \(x^i_1=(\lambda_i/\gamma_1)^{1/\rho}\) and \(x^i_2=(\lambda_i/\gamma_2)^{1/\beta}\).
  3. Aggregate good 1. \(\bar e_1=\gamma_1^{-1/\rho}\sum_i\lambda_i^{1/\rho}\), so \(\gamma_1=\Big(\frac{\sum_i\lambda_i^{1/\rho}}{\bar e_1}\Big)^{\rho}\). Likewise \(\gamma_2=\Big(\frac{\sum_i\lambda_i^{1/\beta}}{\bar e_2}\Big)^{\beta}\).
  4. Sharing rules. Substituting back, $$x^i_1=\frac{\lambda_i^{1/\rho}}{\sum_j\lambda_j^{1/\rho}}\,\bar e_1,\qquad x^i_2=\frac{\lambda_i^{1/\beta}}{\sum_j\lambda_j^{1/\beta}}\,\bar e_2 .$$ Each agent gets a constant share of each good, but the shares differ across goods. A high-\(\lambda\) agent's share is more extreme in the good with lower curvature.
  5. Test. $$\frac{\gamma_1}{\gamma_2}=\frac{\big(\sum_i\lambda_i^{1/\rho}\big)^{\rho}}{\big(\sum_i\lambda_i^{1/\beta}\big)^{\beta}}\cdot\frac{\bar e_2^{\,\beta}}{\bar e_1^{\,\rho}} .$$ Scaling every \(\lambda_i\) by \(k\) multiplies both the numerator and the denominator by \(k\), so the ratio is HD0 in \(\lambda\): the level of the weights is irrelevant, as it must be. But it depends on their distribution. With \(\rho=1\), \(\beta=2\), \(\bar e=(1,1)\), the ratio is \(1/2\) at \(\lambda=(1,1)\) and \(5/9\) at \(\lambda=(1,4)\).
  6. When it works. If \(\rho=\beta\), the numerator and denominator are the same power mean and cancel: \(\gamma_1/\gamma_2=(\bar e_2/\bar e_1)^{\rho}\). Now preferences are identical and homothetic, since additively separable CRRA with a common curvature is homothetic of degree \(1-\rho\).
Why non-homotheticity breaks aggregation

With identical non-homothetic preferences, rich and poor households spend a marginal dollar differently: Engel curves bend. Suppose \(\rho>\beta\). Good 1 is then more of a necessity, because its marginal utility falls off faster. A rich agent's extra dollar goes disproportionately to good 2. Shifting wealth from poor to rich raises aggregate demand for good 2 and lowers it for good 1, so \(p_1/p_2\) falls. Identical preferences do not help when the slope of the Engel curve depends on the level of wealth.

5. The rescue: Stone–Geary and translation

Now let preferences be neither identical nor homothetic N3 p.34:

$$u^i(x^i)=\sum_{l=1}^m\beta^i_l\log\big(x^i_l-\theta^i_l\big),\qquad x^i_l>\theta^i_l .$$

\(\theta^i_l\) is a subsistence level. Agent \(i\) needs at least \(\theta^i_l\) of good \(l\) before it gets any utility from the good, and marginal utility explodes as \(x^i_l\downarrow\theta^i_l\). The quantity \(x^i_l-\theta^i_l\) is supernumerary consumption, the consumption above subsistence.

Derivation
  1. FOC. \(\lambda_i\,\dfrac{\beta^i_l}{x^i_l-\theta^i_l}=\gamma_l\).
  2. Invert. \(x^i_l-\theta^i_l=\lambda_i\beta^i_l/\gamma_l\).
  3. Aggregate. Sum over \(i\) and write \(\bar\theta_l=\sum_i\theta^i_l\): \(\bar e_l-\bar\theta_l=\frac1{\gamma_l}\sum_i\lambda_i\beta^i_l\), so $$\gamma_l=\frac{\sum_i\lambda_i\beta^i_l}{\bar e_l-\bar\theta_l}.$$ This requires \(\bar e_l>\bar\theta_l\): the economy must be able to feed everyone's subsistence.
  4. Test with common tastes \(\beta^i_l=\beta_l\). \(\gamma_l=\beta_l\frac{\sum_i\lambda_i}{\bar e_l-\bar\theta_l}\), and $$\frac{\gamma_l}{\gamma_k}=\frac{\beta_l}{\beta_k}\cdot\frac{\bar e_k-\bar\theta_k}{\bar e_l-\bar\theta_l},$$ which is independent of \(\lambda\) even though the \(\theta^i\) differ across households.
  5. Sharing rule. \(x^i_l=\theta^i_l+\dfrac{\lambda_i}{\sum_j\lambda_j}\big(\bar e_l-\bar\theta_l\big)\). Each agent gets its own subsistence bundle plus a common share \(\delta^i=\lambda_i/\sum_j\lambda_j\) of the aggregate supernumerary endowment \(\bar e-\bar\theta\).

Why it works: shift each household's origin N3 p.35

Define translated consumption \(\hat x^i\equiv x^i-\theta^i\). In these coordinates, \(u^i=\sum_l\beta_l\log\hat x^i_l\) is the same homothetic function for everyone. The feasibility constraint becomes

$$\sum_i\hat x^i=\bar e-\bar\theta,$$

an exchange economy with identical homothetic preferences and aggregate endowment \(\bar e-\bar\theta\). Lesson 24 applies verbatim with \(\bar e\) replaced by \(\bar e-\bar\theta\). In the original coordinates the preferences are not homothetic (Engel curves start at \(\theta^i\), not at 0) and not identical (the \(\theta^i\) differ). They are homothetic around a household-specific point, which is what economists call quasi-homothetic.

Two traps
  • The taste weights must be common. With \(\beta^i_l\) varying across \(i\), step 3 gives \(\gamma_l/\gamma_k=\frac{\sum_i\lambda_i\beta^i_l}{\sum_i\lambda_i\beta^i_k}\cdot\frac{\bar e_k-\bar\theta_k}{\bar e_l-\bar\theta_l}\), and the first factor depends on \(\lambda\), exactly as in §2. Translation can absorb different origins, but not different shapes.
  • Translated preferences are not homothetic. A commonly circulated review sheet labels translated CRRA "non-identical and homothetic". The right description is non-identical, non-homothetic, and quasi-homothetic. Doubling wealth does not double every good, because the subsistence part stays fixed.

6. The CES generalization

Replace the logs by power functions N3 p.36:

$$u^i(x^i)=\sum_{l=1}^m\beta_l\,\frac{\big(x^i_l-\theta^i_l\big)^{1-\sigma}}{1-\sigma},\qquad\sigma>0 .$$
  1. FOC. \(\lambda_i\beta_l\big(x^i_l-\theta^i_l\big)^{-\sigma}=\gamma_l\).
  2. Invert. \(x^i_l-\theta^i_l=\Big(\dfrac{\lambda_i\beta_l}{\gamma_l}\Big)^{1/\sigma}=\lambda_i^{1/\sigma}\Big(\dfrac{\beta_l}{\gamma_l}\Big)^{1/\sigma}\).
  3. Aggregate. \(\bar e_l-\bar\theta_l=\big(\beta_l/\gamma_l\big)^{1/\sigma}\sum_i\lambda_i^{1/\sigma}\). Solving, $$\gamma_l=\beta_l\left(\frac{\sum_i\lambda_i^{1/\sigma}}{\bar e_l-\bar\theta_l}\right)^{\sigma}.$$
  4. Test. The \(\lambda\) term \(\big(\sum_i\lambda_i^{1/\sigma}\big)^\sigma\) is common to every good, so it cancels: $$\frac{\gamma_l}{\gamma_k}=\frac{\beta_l}{\beta_k}\left(\frac{\bar e_k-\bar\theta_k}{\bar e_l-\bar\theta_l}\right)^{\sigma}.$$
  5. Sharing rule. Substituting \(\gamma_l\) back: $$x^i_l=\theta^i_l+\delta^i\big(\bar e_l-\bar\theta_l\big),\qquad\delta^i=\frac{\lambda_i^{1/\sigma}}{\sum_j\lambda_j^{1/\sigma}},\quad\sum_i\delta^i=1 .$$ The share \(\delta^i\) is the same for every good. That is the hallmark of aggregation: the planner hands out slices of one aggregate "pie" \(\bar e-\bar\theta\). \(\sigma=1\) recovers Stone–Geary.

Compare §3: there the exponent was good-specific (\(\rho\) versus \(\beta\)), so the shares \(\lambda_i^{1/\rho}/\sum\lambda^{1/\rho}\) and \(\lambda_i^{1/\beta}/\sum\lambda^{1/\beta}\) differed across goods, and the \(\lambda\) terms did not cancel. A common curvature \(\sigma\) across goods is what makes \(u\) homothetic in \(\hat x\).

7. The general statement, completed

Slide 37 sets up the general case and stops. Let \(\theta^i\in\mathbb R^m\) for every \(i\), and N3 p.37

$$u^i(x)=g^i\big(h(x-\theta^i)\big),$$

with \(h\) common, HD1, increasing and concave, and \(g^i\) strictly increasing and concave. If \(\theta^i_l>0\), good \(l\) is a necessity for household \(i\). Redefine consumption as \(\hat x^i=x^i-\theta^i\), so feasibility reads \(\sum_i\hat x^i=\bar e-\bar\theta\). Here is the conclusion the slide leaves implicit.

Proposition: aggregation with translated homothetic preferences

Suppose \(\bar e-\bar\theta\gg0\). In any interior competitive equilibrium, whatever the endowments \(\{e^i\}\) (provided each agent can afford its translation point, \(p\cdot e^i>p\cdot\theta^i\)), $$p=\kappa\,\nabla h\big(\bar e-\bar\theta\big)\quad\text{for some }\kappa>0,\qquad x^i=\theta^i+\delta^i(\bar e-\bar\theta),\quad\delta^i=\frac{p\cdot(e^i-\theta^i)}{p\cdot(\bar e-\bar\theta)} .$$ On the planner side (with strictly concave \(g^i\)), \(\gamma_l/\gamma_k=h_l(\bar e-\bar\theta)/h_k(\bar e-\bar\theta)\) for every \(\lambda\gg0\).

Proof (competitive-equilibrium route)
  1. Translate the budget. \(p\cdot x^i\le p\cdot e^i\iff p\cdot\hat x^i\le\hat w^i\), where \(\hat w^i\equiv p\cdot(e^i-\theta^i)\) is supernumerary wealth: what is left after buying the translation bundle.
  2. Demand is linear in \(\hat w^i\) with a common direction. Since \(g^i\) is increasing, agent \(i\) maximizes \(h(\hat x)\) subject to \(p\cdot\hat x\le\hat w^i\). Because \(h\) is HD1, if \(d(p)\) solves the problem with wealth 1, then \(\hat w^i d(p)\) solves it with wealth \(\hat w^i\). So \(\hat x^i=\hat w^i\,d(p)\), with the same \(d(p)\) for every \(i\). The \(g^i\) play no role in demand.
  3. Aggregate. \(\sum_i\hat x^i=\big(\sum_i\hat w^i\big)d(p)=p\cdot(\bar e-\bar\theta)\,d(p)\). Market clearing requires this to equal \(\bar e-\bar\theta\). So the equilibrium \(p\) must make \(\bar e-\bar\theta\) the optimal translated bundle at wealth \(p\cdot(\bar e-\bar\theta)\).
  4. Read off prices. The FOC for the translated problem at \(\hat x=\bar e-\bar\theta\) is \(\nabla h(\bar e-\bar\theta)=\nu\,p\) for a multiplier \(\nu>0\). So \(p=\kappa\nabla h(\bar e-\bar\theta)\) with \(\kappa=1/\nu\). Concavity of \(h\) makes this FOC sufficient. This does not depend on how \(\bar e\) is split, nor on how \(\bar\theta\) is split.
  5. Allocation. \(\hat x^i=\hat w^i d(p)\) and \(\sum_i\hat x^i=\bar e-\bar\theta\) give \(\hat x^i=\frac{\hat w^i}{\sum_j\hat w^j}(\bar e-\bar\theta)\), which is the stated \(\delta^i\).
∎
Proof (planner route, for strictly concave \(g^i\))
  1. Guess that every \(\hat x^i\) lies on the ray through \(Z\equiv\bar e-\bar\theta\): \(\hat x^i=s_iZ\) with \(s_i>0\) and \(\sum_is_i=1\).
  2. FOC. \(\lambda_i\,g^{i\prime}\big(h(\hat x^i)\big)\nabla h(\hat x^i)=\gamma\). Since \(\nabla h\) is HD0, \(\nabla h(s_iZ)=\nabla h(Z)\), and the FOC becomes \(\lambda_i\,g^{i\prime}\big(s_ih(Z)\big)\nabla h(Z)=\gamma\).
  3. Choose the shares so that \(\lambda_ig^{i\prime}(s_ih(Z))\) equals a common constant \(\kappa\). Each left side is strictly decreasing in \(s_i\), so there is a unique such \(s\) with \(\sum_is_i=1\) (under Inada-type conditions on \(g^i\)). Then \(\gamma=\kappa\nabla h(Z)\).
  4. Verify. The objective is concave and the constraints are linear, so FOCs are sufficient: the guess is optimal. \(\gamma_l/\gamma_k=h_l(Z)/h_k(Z)\) for every \(\lambda\). The weights move who gets how much (\(s_i\)), but not relative shadow values.
∎
"We can dispense with \(g^i\)": harmless for the CE, degenerate for the planner

The slide notes that one can take \(g^i\) to be the identity. For the CE that is fine (step 2 above never used \(g^i\)). For the planner it changes the problem: with \(u^i=h(x-\theta^i)\) linear along rays, utility is transferable, and with unequal weights the planner hands the whole surplus \(Z\) to a top-weight agent. With equal weights every split on the ray is optimal. This is the source of a wrong answer to a recent final (Exercise 25.3).

8. Satiation points: the mirror image

Two past exams use a translation in the other direction. Let

$$u^i(x^i)=-\sum_{l=1}^m\pi_l\,\frac{\big(\theta^i_l-x^i_l\big)^{1+\sigma}}{1+\sigma},\qquad x^i_l\le\theta^i_l,\quad\pi_l,\sigma>0 .$$

Now \(\theta^i_l\) is a satiation (bliss) point, and \(\theta^i_l-x^i_l\ge0\) is the agent's shortfall. Utility is a common homothetic function of the shortfall, decreasing in each shortfall. With \(\sigma=1\) it is quadratic utility.

  1. FOC. \(\lambda_i\pi_l\big(\theta^i_l-x^i_l\big)^{\sigma}=\gamma_l\).
  2. Invert. \(\theta^i_l-x^i_l=\big(\gamma_l/\pi_l\big)^{1/\sigma}\lambda_i^{-1/\sigma}\).
  3. Aggregate. \(\bar\theta_l-\bar e_l=\big(\gamma_l/\pi_l\big)^{1/\sigma}\sum_j\lambda_j^{-1/\sigma}\), so $$\gamma_l=\pi_l\,\frac{\big(\bar\theta_l-\bar e_l\big)^{\sigma}}{\big(\sum_j\lambda_j^{-1/\sigma}\big)^{\sigma}},\qquad\frac{\gamma_l}{\gamma_k}=\frac{\pi_l}{\pi_k}\left(\frac{\bar\theta_l-\bar e_l}{\bar\theta_k-\bar e_k}\right)^{\sigma}.$$
  4. Sharing rule. \(x^i_l-\theta^i_l=\delta^i\big(\bar e_l-\bar\theta_l\big)\) with \(\delta^i=\lambda_i^{-1/\sigma}\big/\sum_j\lambda_j^{-1/\sigma}\). A higher \(\lambda_i\) lowers \(\delta^i\), i.e. lowers \(i\)'s share of the aggregate shortfall, so \(i\) consumes closer to its bliss point.

The condition \(\bar e\lt\bar\theta\) is essential, and the exam leaves it unstated. Since \(x^i\le\theta^i\), feasibility with equality needs \(\bar e_l\le\bar\theta_l\). If \(\bar e_l=\bar\theta_l\), everyone is satiated in good \(l\) and \(\gamma_l=0\). With free disposal and \(\bar e_l>\bar\theta_l\), good \(l\) is free. Interior, positive prices need \(\bar e\ll\bar\theta\). Relative prices \(p_l/p_k\) then fall with \(\bar e_l\) and rise with \(\bar e_k\): the less scarce good \(l\) is relative to satiation, the cheaper it is.

9. The unifying principle: the Gorman polar form

Why do translations work while §2 and §3 fail? Look at demand. Gorman (1953) showed that aggregate demand depends only on aggregate wealth, for every wealth distribution, iff each household's indirect utility has the form (MWG Prop. 4.B.1)

$$v^i(p,w)=a^i(p)+b(p)\,w ,$$

with \(b(p)\) common to all households. By Roy's identity, demand is then \(x^i_l(p,w)=-\frac{a^i_l(p)}{b(p)}-\frac{b_l(p)}{b(p)}\,w\): linear Engel curves with the same slope for everyone. A dollar moved from \(j\) to \(i\) changes \(i\)'s demand by exactly as much as it changes \(j\)'s, so aggregate demand cannot move.

Translation is therefore one example of the Gorman polar form, not the whole of it. Two versions of the result

Gorman's condition delivers aggregation for every wealth distribution (with interior demands). The course's version (slides 26–30) assumed identical homothetic preferences and proportional endowments, then noted that proportionality is not needed: sufficient is \(p\cdot e^i=\delta^ip\cdot\bar e\), and with identical homothetic preferences any distribution satisfies this for \(\delta^i\equiv p\cdot e^i/p\cdot\bar e\). One widely shared review sheet calls this condition "necessary". It is sufficient, not necessary: Stone–Geary aggregates without it.

10. Where exams use this: inside an OLG cohort

Put heterogeneous types inside each generation of a two-period OLG economy (Lesson 16). Each cohort's "goods" are consumption at \(t\) and at \(t+1\). Two facts combine:

  1. No trade across generations (Lesson 8's induction, applied to cohort totals). Each cohort consumes its aggregate endowment \((\bar\alpha_y,\bar\alpha_o)\), although types trade with each other.
  2. Within the cohort, the equilibrium is a CE of a finite two-good exchange economy, so the aggregation result applies. If preferences are of the translated-homothetic form, the intertemporal price \(p_{t+1}/p_t\) equals the MRS of a representative agent with translation \(\bar\theta\), evaluated at \((\bar\alpha_y,\bar\alpha_o)\).

With the satiation utility of §8 and weights \((\pi_y,\pi_o)\), this gives the Spring 2023 answer:

$$\frac{1}{1+r}=\frac{p_{t+1}}{p_t}=\frac{\pi_o}{\pi_y}\left(\frac{\bar\theta_o-\bar\alpha_o}{\bar\theta_y-\bar\alpha_y}\right)^{\sigma}.$$

The interest rate depends only on cohort aggregates. Whether \(r\) is positive then decides efficiency, exactly as in the homogeneous model (Lesson 10). With log utility and heterogeneous endowments (the 2025 problem set), the same logic gave \(\bar r=\frac{\bar\alpha-\beta}{\beta(1-\bar\alpha)}\): only the mean \(\bar\alpha\) matters.

Exercise 25.1 True or false? Justify. Exam format

For each statement, say whether it is true or false and justify in one to four lines.

  1. Suppose each of \(I\) households has \(u^i(x)=-\sum_{l=1}^m\frac{1}{1+\sigma}(\theta^i_l-x_l)^{1+\sigma}\) on \(x\le\theta^i\), with \(\theta^i_l>0\) and \(\sigma>0\). These utility functions imply Gorman (strong) aggregation. (Past exam: Fall 2025 Midterm 1 I.5, reworded)
    Show answer

    True. This is §8 with \(\pi_l=1\). The planner FOC \(\lambda_i(\theta^i_l-x^i_l)^\sigma=\gamma_l\) aggregates to \(\gamma_l\propto(\bar\theta_l-\bar e_l)^\sigma\), with a \(\lambda\) factor common to all goods. So \(\gamma_l/\gamma_k=\big(\frac{\bar\theta_l-\bar e_l}{\bar\theta_k-\bar e_k}\big)^\sigma\) is independent of \(\lambda\). On the demand side, \(x^i_l=\theta^i_l-\frac{p_l^{1/\sigma}}{\sum_kp_k^{1+1/\sigma}}(p\cdot\theta^i-w^i)\) is linear in \(w^i\) with a common slope. (This assumes an interior solution, \(w^i\lt p\cdot\theta^i\).)

  2. If all households have the same preferences, Gorman aggregation always holds. (Exam-inspired; review drill)
    Show answer

    False. Slide 33: identical \(\frac{x_1^{1-\rho}}{1-\rho}+\frac{x_2^{1-\beta}}{1-\beta}\) with \(\rho\ne\beta\) gives \(\gamma_1/\gamma_2\propto\big(\sum\lambda_i^{1/\rho}\big)^\rho/\big(\sum\lambda_i^{1/\beta}\big)^\beta\), which depends on the distribution of \(\lambda\). Engel curves are non-linear, so a transfer from rich to poor changes aggregate demand.

  3. If every household has homothetic preferences, equilibrium relative prices do not depend on the distribution of endowments. (Slides N3 p.32)
    Show answer

    False. Non-identical Cobb–Douglas: \(p_l\bar e_l=\sum_i\beta^i_lw^i\), so moving wealth toward households with high \(\beta^i_l\) raises \(p_l\). Exact aggregation needs a common homothetic ordering \(h\).

  4. The translated CRRA utility \(\sum_l\beta_l\frac{(x_l-\theta^i_l)^{1-\sigma}}{1-\sigma}\) with \(\theta^i\ne0\) is homothetic, and that is why it aggregates. (Exam-inspired; corrects a review-sheet label)
    Show answer

    False. It is not homothetic: the MRS at \(tx\) differs from the MRS at \(x\) because \(\theta^i\) does not scale. It is homothetic in \(x-\theta^i\) (quasi-homothetic). It aggregates because, after translating each origin, everyone shares the same homothetic preferences over \(\hat x^i\) and feasibility becomes \(\sum\hat x^i=\bar e-\bar\theta\).

  5. With identical homothetic preferences, proportional endowments \(e^i=\delta^i\bar e\) are necessary for equilibrium prices to be independent of the wealth distribution. (Slides N3 p.30; corrects a review sheet)
    Show answer

    False. Proportionality is sufficient. Demand is \(x^i=w^id(p)\), so aggregate demand is \(\big(\sum_iw^i\big)d(p)\) for any endowments, and clearing gives \(p\propto\nabla h(\bar e)\). Slide 30 notes that all that is needed is \(p\cdot e^i=\delta^ip\cdot\bar e\), which holds with \(\delta^i\equiv p\cdot e^i/p\cdot\bar e\).

  6. Aggregation requires that all households have identical preferences, up to increasing transformations. (Exam-inspired)
    Show answer

    False. Stone–Geary with common \(\beta\) and different \(\theta^i\) aggregates (§5). So do CARA expected utilities \(\sum_s\pi_s(-e^{-\rho_ix_s}/\rho_i)\) with different \(\rho_i\) and common beliefs, whose wealth slope \(1/\sum_sp_s\) is common (Exercise 25.7). What is needed is the Gorman form: linear Engel curves with a common slope.

  7. In the Stone–Geary economy \(u^i=\sum_l\beta^i_l\log(x_l-\theta^i_l)\), aggregation holds as long as the subsistence vectors \(\theta^i\) are identical across households, even if the \(\beta^i\) differ. (Exam-inspired)
    Show answer

    False. \(\gamma_l=\sum_i\lambda_i\beta^i_l/(\bar e_l-\bar\theta_l)\), so \(\gamma_l/\gamma_k\) contains \(\sum_i\lambda_i\beta^i_l/\sum_i\lambda_i\beta^i_k\), which depends on \(\lambda\) when the \(\beta^i\) differ. Common \(\theta\) does not help. It is the \(\beta\)'s (the shape) that must be common, while the \(\theta\)'s (the origins) may differ.

  8. With satiation-point utilities and free disposal, if \(\bar e_l>\bar\theta_l\) for some good \(l\), then good \(l\) has a zero equilibrium price. (Exam-inspired)
    Show answer

    True. Every household can be given its bliss level \(\theta^i_l\), and the remaining \(\bar e_l-\bar\theta_l\) is disposed of. Nobody's marginal utility of good \(l\) is positive there, so the planner's multiplier is \(\gamma_l=0\), and so is \(p_l=\gamma_l/\kappa\). This is why the exam's formulas need \(\bar e\ll\bar\theta\).

Hint

Change variables to \(z^i=c^i-\theta^i\). An HD1 quasi-concave function that is positive on the cone is concave, hence superadditive: \(H(a+b)\ge H(a)+H(b)\). For (d), compute the indirect utility \(v^i(p,w)\) and differentiate in \(w\).

Show solution

(a) \(U(\bar e;\lambda)=\max_{\{c^i\}}\sum_i\lambda_iH(c^i-\theta^i)\) subject to \(\sum_ic^i=\bar e\).

(b) Let \(z^i=c^i-\theta^i\ge0\), so the constraint becomes \(\sum_iz^i=Z\equiv\bar e-\bar\theta\). The "simple transformation" is the sum \(\bar\theta=\sum_i\theta^i\). Either way, \(\nabla_{\bar e}U\propto\nabla H(\bar e-\bar\theta)\), independent of \(\lambda\), which is aggregation.

(c) One agent: with \(I=1\), the constraint forces \(c^1=\bar e\), so \(U=\lambda_1H(\bar e-\theta^1)\). The proposed formula gives \(H(\bar e-\lambda_1\theta^1)\), which differs whenever \(\lambda_1\ne1\). The underlying mistake is to write \(\lambda_iH(z^i)=H(\lambda_iz^i)\) and then act as if the constraint were on \(\sum_i\lambda_iz^i\). It is on \(\sum_iz^i\).

The Negishi weights \(\lambda_i=1/\mu_i=P(p)\) are therefore equal. With linear \(H\)-utility, utility is transferable at the same rate for everyone. By §7, prices are \(p=\kappa\nabla H(\bar e-\bar\theta)\) for any endowment distribution with \(p\cdot e^i\ge p\cdot\theta^i\).

Let \(u^i(x)=g^i(h(x-\theta^i))\) with \(h\) HD1, increasing and concave, and \(g^i\) strictly increasing.

  1. [10] Show that household \(i\)'s demand has the form \(x^i(p,w)=\theta^i+\big(w-p\cdot\theta^i\big)d(p)\) for a function \(d(p)\) common to all households.
  2. [10] Conclude that in an interior CE, \(p\propto\nabla h(\bar e-\bar\theta)\), whatever the distribution of endowments.
  3. [5] Which household-level condition on endowments do you need, and why?
  4. [5] If \(\theta^i_l>0\), in what sense is good \(l\) a necessity for \(i\)? Compute \(i\)'s wealth elasticity of demand for good \(l\) and show that it is below one.
Show solution

(a) The budget is \(p\cdot\hat x\le w-p\cdot\theta^i\equiv\hat w\) with \(\hat x=x-\theta^i\). Since \(g^i\) is increasing, \(i\) maximizes \(h(\hat x)\) subject to it. If \(d(p)\) solves this at \(\hat w=1\), then for \(\hat w>0\) the bundle \(\hat w\,d(p)\) is affordable, and any affordable \(\hat x\) satisfies \(h(\hat x)=\hat w\,h(\hat x/\hat w)\le\hat w\,h(d(p))=h(\hat w\,d(p))\). So \(\hat x^i=\hat w\,d(p)\).

(b) Summing, \(\sum_i\hat x^i=\big(\sum_i\hat w^i\big)d(p)=p\cdot(\bar e-\bar\theta)\,d(p)\). Clearing requires this to equal \(\bar e-\bar\theta\), so \(\bar e-\bar\theta\) must be the optimal translated bundle at prices \(p\). Its FOC is \(\nabla h(\bar e-\bar\theta)=\nu p\). Hence \(p\propto\nabla h(\bar e-\bar\theta)\), which involves only aggregates.

(c) \(\hat w^i=p\cdot(e^i-\theta^i)>0\): each household must be able to afford its own translation bundle at the equilibrium prices. Otherwise its problem has no interior solution (Stone–Geary utility is \(-\infty\) below subsistence), the linear demand formula fails, and aggregation can break. Gorman aggregation is always "for interior demands".

(d) \(x^i_l=\theta^i_l+(w-p\cdot\theta^i)d_l(p)\) contains a fixed component \(\theta^i_l\) that is bought regardless of wealth. The elasticity is \(\frac{\partial x^i_l}{\partial w}\frac{w}{x^i_l}=\frac{d_l w}{\theta^i_l+(w-p\cdot\theta^i)d_l}\), which is below 1 iff \(\theta^i_l>p\cdot\theta^i\,d_l\). With a single translated good this holds automatically, since then \(p\cdot\theta^i=p_l\theta^i_l\) and \(p_ld_l\lt1\). Generally, goods with relatively large subsistence requirements have wealth elasticities below one: they are necessities.

Exercise 25.7 Gorman form: who passes, who fails Exam-inspired
  1. [10] For \(u^i=h(x-\theta^i)\) with \(h\) HD1, let \(P(p)=\min\{p\cdot z:h(z)\ge1\}\). Show that \(v^i(p,w)=\frac{w-p\cdot\theta^i}{P(p)}\). Use Roy's identity to find demand, and verify that \(\partial x^i_l/\partial w\) is common to all \(i\).
  2. [5] For non-identical Cobb–Douglas \(u^i=\sum_l\beta^i_l\log x_l\), compute \(\partial x^i_l/\partial w\) and explain why aggregation fails.
  3. [10] Two states, common beliefs \(\pi_s\), and expected utility with CARA \(v^i(x)=-e^{-\rho_ix}/\rho_i\), where risk aversion \(\rho_i\) differs across agents. Show that \(\partial x^i_s/\partial w=1/\sum_{s'}p_{s'}\) for every \(i\). Conclude that relative state prices do not depend on the distribution of wealth, even though preferences are not identical.
Hint

For (c), solve \(\pi_se^{-\rho_ix_s}=\mu_ip_s\) for \(x_s\), substitute into the budget to find \(\log\mu_i\), and substitute back. (This is inspired by a 2022 problem set on risk sharing.)

Show solution

(a) Optimal translated consumption is \(\hat x=\hat w\,d(p)\) with \(h(d(p))=1/P(p)\): the cheapest unit of \(h\) costs \(P\), so a dollar buys \(1/P\) units. Hence \(v^i=h(\hat w\,d)=\hat w/P=(w-p\cdot\theta^i)/P(p)\). Roy's identity: \(\partial v/\partial w=1/P\) and \(\partial v/\partial p_l=-\theta^i_l/P-(w-p\cdot\theta^i)P_l/P^2\), so $$x^i_l=-\frac{\partial v/\partial p_l}{\partial v/\partial w}=\theta^i_l+(w-p\cdot\theta^i)\frac{P_l(p)}{P(p)},\qquad\frac{\partial x^i_l}{\partial w}=\frac{P_l(p)}{P(p)},$$ which is common to all \(i\) (the Gorman form with \(b=1/P\)).

(b) \(x^i_l=\beta^i_lw/p_l\), so \(\partial x^i_l/\partial w=\beta^i_l/p_l\), which differs across \(i\). A dollar moved from \(j\) to \(i\) changes aggregate demand for \(l\) by \((\beta^i_l-\beta^j_l)/p_l\ne0\), so prices must adjust.

(c) The FOC gives \(x^i_s=-\frac{1}{\rho_i}\log\frac{\mu_ip_s}{\pi_s}\). The budget \(\sum_sp_sx^i_s=w\) gives \(\log\mu_i=-\frac{\rho_iw+\sum_sp_s\log(p_s/\pi_s)}{\sum_sp_s}\). Substituting back, $$x^i_s=\frac{w}{\sum_{s'}p_{s'}}+\frac{1}{\rho_i}\Big[\frac{\sum_{s'}p_{s'}\log(p_{s'}/\pi_{s'})}{\sum_{s'}p_{s'}}-\log\frac{p_s}{\pi_s}\Big].$$ The wealth slope \(1/\sum_{s'}p_{s'}\) is the same for everyone: the Gorman form. So aggregate demand depends only on aggregate wealth, and so do equilibrium prices. On the planner side, \(\gamma_s/\gamma_{s'}=\frac{\pi_s}{\pi_{s'}}\exp\big(-(\bar e_s-\bar e_{s'})/\sum_j\rho_j^{-1}\big)\), independent of \(\lambda\). The representative agent is CARA with risk tolerance \(\sum_j1/\rho_j\). (The domain must allow negative consumption, which CARA does.)

Exercise 25.8 Negishi weights when aggregation fails Exam-inspired

Two goods, households with \(u^i=\beta^i\log x_1+(1-\beta^i)\log x_2\) and proportional endowments \(e^i=\delta^i\bar e\), with \(\sum_i\delta^i=1\).

  1. [10] Find the CE relative price \(p_1/p_2\). Show that it depends on \(\delta\) unless all \(\beta^i\) are equal.
  2. [5] Find \(\mu_i\) and the supporting Pareto weights \(\lambda_i=1/\mu_i\).
  3. [10] Plug those weights into the planner's \(\gamma_1/\gamma_2\) from §2 and verify that it equals your \(p_1/p_2\).
  4. [5] Is the CE allocation equal to the endowment, as in Lesson 24? Explain.
Show solution

(a) Let \(W=p\cdot\bar e\), so \(w^i=\delta^iW\). Clearing good 1: \(p_1\bar e_1=\sum_i\beta^iw^i=\bar\beta_\delta W\), where \(\bar\beta_\delta\equiv\sum_i\delta^i\beta^i\). Similarly \(p_2\bar e_2=(1-\bar\beta_\delta)W\). So $$\frac{p_1}{p_2}=\frac{\bar\beta_\delta}{1-\bar\beta_\delta}\cdot\frac{\bar e_2}{\bar e_1},$$ which depends on the distribution \(\delta\) through the wealth-weighted taste \(\bar\beta_\delta\), unless \(\beta^i\equiv\beta\).

(b) With log utility and taste weights summing to one, \(V^i=\log w^i+\text{terms in }p\), so \(\mu_i=\partial V^i/\partial w=1/w^i\) and \(\lambda_i=w^i=\delta^iW\). Richer households get larger weights, as in Lesson 23.

(c) From §2, \(\frac{\gamma_1}{\gamma_2}=\frac{\sum_i\lambda_i\beta^i}{\sum_i\lambda_i(1-\beta^i)}\frac{\bar e_2}{\bar e_1}=\frac{W\bar\beta_\delta}{W(1-\bar\beta_\delta)}\frac{\bar e_2}{\bar e_1}\), which matches (a).

(d) No. Household \(i\) consumes \(x^i_1=\beta^iw^i/p_1=\frac{\beta^i}{\bar\beta_\delta}\delta^i\bar e_1\), which is not \(\delta^i\bar e_1\) unless \(\beta^i=\bar\beta_\delta\). Households with a stronger taste for good 1 trade good 2 for it. With identical tastes, nobody wants to trade away from a proportional endowment, which is why the Lesson 24 CE is autarky.

Key takeaways

  • Test: solve \(\lambda_iu^i_l=\gamma_l\) for \(x^i_l\), sum to get \(\gamma_l(\lambda)\), and check whether \(\gamma_l/\gamma_k\) depends on \(\lambda\). If it does not, CE relative prices do not depend on the wealth distribution.
  • Homothetic but not identical fails (non-identical Cobb–Douglas: \(\gamma_l\propto\sum_i\lambda_i\beta^i_l/\bar e_l\)). Identical but not homothetic fails (curvatures \(\rho\ne\beta\): the shares \(\lambda_i^{1/\rho}\) and \(\lambda_i^{1/\beta}\) differ across goods).
  • Translation rescues aggregation: with \(u^i=g^i(h(x-\theta^i))\) and common \(h\), \(x^i=\theta^i+\delta^i(\bar e-\bar\theta)\) and \(p\propto\nabla h(\bar e-\bar\theta)\). Stone–Geary, translated CES, and satiation-point utilities are all instances. Origins may differ, shapes may not.
  • Satiation versions need \(\bar e\ll\bar\theta\), and subsistence versions need \(\bar e\gg\bar\theta\) and \(p\cdot e^i>p\cdot\theta^i\).
  • The deep condition is Gorman's: \(v^i=a^i(p)+b(p)w\), i.e. linear Engel curves with a common slope. Translated preferences are quasi-homothetic, not homothetic.
  • In OLG cohorts, aggregation plus "no trade across generations" pins \(1+r\) to cohort aggregates. Then the sign of \(r\) decides efficiency.
