The First Welfare Theorem

Covers Slides 1, pp. 14–19 · About 35 min · Prerequisite: Lesson 3
By the end you can:
  1. state the First Welfare Theorem (1WT) and reproduce its proof from memory, in five steps;
  2. point to the exact line where each assumption is used: LNS, utility maximization, profit maximization, feasibility, and finitely many agents;
  3. say what the theorem does not need (convexity, continuity, differentiability) and test it on examples.
Primary source Alvarez, Slides 1, pp. 15–18 (statement and proof). The same argument appears in Mas-Colell, Whinston & Green, Proposition 16.C.1, and in Debreu, Theory of Value, §6.3. Keep this lesson's proof next to Lesson 12: the OLG counterexample breaks exactly one line of it.

1. The theorem

First Welfare Theorem S1 p.15

Let \(\big(p,\{\bar x^i,\bar y^j\}\big)\) be a competitive equilibrium. Assume that the preferences of every agent satisfy local non-satiation. Then \(\{\bar x^i,\bar y^j\}\) is Pareto optimal.

In one sentence: if prices are such that everyone is optimizing and markets clear, there is no way to make someone better off without hurting someone else. This is Adam Smith's invisible hand made precise. It is remarkable mostly for how little it assumes. There is nothing about convexity, continuity, or the shape of technology; the CE just has to exist.

2. The idea in one picture

The heart of the proof is the Lemma from Lesson 3: at equilibrium prices, everything a household likes at least as much as \(\bar x^i\) costs at least as much as \(\bar x^i\). Geometrically, the upper contour set \(\{x: u^i(x)\ge u^i(\bar x^i)\}\) lies entirely on or above the budget hyperplane through \(\bar x^i\). A Pareto improvement would make every household's bundle weakly more expensive and one strictly so. Its total value would then exceed the value of what the economy can produce, which is impossible.

3. The proof, step by step S1 pp.16–18

Strategy: contradiction. Suppose the CE allocation is not PO. Then some feasible allocation \(\{x^i,y^j\}\) Pareto dominates it: $$u^i(x^i)\ge u^i(\bar x^i)\ \ \forall i\in I,\qquad u^{i'}(x^{i'})>u^{i'}(\bar x^{i'})\ \text{ for some } i'\in I.$$ We will value both allocations at the equilibrium prices \(p\) and show that the dominating one would have to be worth more than the economy can supply.

Proof
  1. Weakly better costs weakly more (uses LNS). $$p\cdot x^i\ \ge\ p\cdot\bar x^i\quad\text{for all } i\in I. \tag{1}$$ Suppose instead \(p\cdot x^i0\) and choose \(\varepsilon\) with \(\|p\|\varepsilon<\delta\). LNS gives \(\hat x\in X^i\) with \(\|\hat x-x^i\|<\varepsilon\) and \(u^i(\hat x)>u^i(x^i)\ge u^i(\bar x^i)\). By Cauchy–Schwarz, \(p\cdot\hat x
  2. Strictly better costs strictly more (uses only optimality). $$p\cdot x^{i'}\ >\ p\cdot\bar x^{i'}. \tag{2}$$ If \(p\cdot x^{i'}\le p\cdot\bar x^{i'}\), then \(x^{i'}\) is affordable for \(i'\) and strictly better than \(\bar x^{i'}\), contradicting optimality.
  3. Add up over households (uses finitely many terms / finite values). Summing (1) over all \(i\), with the strict inequality (2) for \(i'\), and using linearity of \(p\cdot\): $$p\cdot\sum_{i\in I}x^i\ >\ p\cdot\sum_{i\in I}\bar x^i. \tag{3}$$ Flag this step. Adding infinitely many inequalities and keeping the strict one only makes sense if the sums are finite numbers. If \(p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i=\infty\), (3) degenerates into "∞ > ∞". That is the OLG loophole (Lesson 12).
  4. Firms were already doing the best they could (uses profit maximization). Each \(\bar y^j\) maximizes \(p\cdot y\) on \(Y^j\), and the dominating plan \(y^j\) is in \(Y^j\) (feasibility), so \(p\cdot\bar y^j\ge p\cdot y^j\). Summing over \(j\): $$p\cdot\sum_{j\in J}\bar y^j\ \ge\ p\cdot\sum_{j\in J}y^j. \tag{4}$$
  5. Contradiction with feasibility. Both allocations satisfy material balance, so taking values at \(p\): $$p\cdot\sum_i x^i=p\cdot\sum_j y^j+p\cdot\sum_i e^i,\qquad p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i=p\cdot\sum_j\bar y^j+p\cdot\sum_i e^i .$$ Subtract the second from the first. The endowment terms cancel: $$\underbrace{p\cdot\sum_i x^i-p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i}_{>\,0\ \text{by (3)}}\;=\;\underbrace{p\cdot\sum_j y^j-p\cdot\sum_j\bar y^j}_{\le\,0\ \text{by (4)}} .$$ A strictly positive number cannot equal a non-positive one. So no Pareto-dominating allocation exists, and the CE allocation is PO.
∎
Intuition: the accounting behind the proof

Prices turn "better off" into "more expensive" (Steps 1–2). Profit maximization means no firm could have produced anything more valuable (Step 4). Feasibility says the value of what households consume equals the value of what firms produce plus endowments (Step 5). So a Pareto improvement would need households to consume more value than the economy can create at these prices. Every inequality is evaluated at the same \(p\); this is why everybody facing the same prices matters.

Proof map: what is used where

StepInequalityAssumption usedBreaks if…
1\(p x^i\ge p\bar x^i\)LNS + utility maximizationthick indifference curves, satiation (Lesson 3)
2\(p x^{i'}>p\bar x^{i'}\)utility maximization; \(u^{i'}\) depends only on \(x^{i'}\)consumption externalities (Lesson 5)
3\(p\sum x^i>p\sum\bar x^i\)sums are finiteinfinitely many agents and goods with \(p\sum\bar x^i=\infty\) (OLG)
4\(p\sum\bar y^j\ge p\sum y^j\)profit maximization at \(p\); \(Y^j\) independent of othersmarket power, production externalities (Lesson 5)

Not used anywhere

Convexity of preferences or technology, continuity of \(u\), differentiability, \(p\gg 0\), and the ownership shares \(\theta\) (they cancel because feasibility is valued directly) are all absent. Increasing returns, non-convex preferences and so on do not by themselves invalidate the 1WT. They may destroy existence of a CE, but whenever a CE exists, it is PO. That is the point of slide questions p.19 (convex \(u\)) and p.20 (increasing returns), developed in Lesson 5.

4. Check your understanding

1. Equation (1), \(p\cdot x^i\ge p\cdot\bar x^i\) for all \(i\), follows from…
  • feasibility of both allocations being compared
  • local non-satiation plus utility maximization
  • profit maximization by all firms in the economy
  • convexity of every household's preferences set
If a weakly preferred \(x^i\) were strictly cheaper, LNS would give a nearby strictly better and still affordable bundle, contradicting optimality of \(\bar x^i\). Convexity plays no role.
2. Which step would fail if \(p\cdot\sum_i \bar x^i=+\infty\)?
  • Step 1, the inequality derived from local non-satiation
  • Step 2, the strict inequality for the agent who gains
  • Step 4, the inequality derived from firms' profit maximization
  • Step 3, the summation that preserves the strict inequality
Each individual inequality still holds, but summing infinitely many of them gives "∞ > ∞", which says nothing. This is exactly the OLG failure (Lesson 12).
3. Every \(u^i\) is strictly convex (not concave) and strictly increasing, and a CE exists. Its allocation is…
  • Pareto optimal, since LNS still holds here
  • not Pareto optimal, since convexity fails
  • Pareto optimal, but only with one consumer
  • undetermined without differentiable utility
Strictly increasing implies LNS (Lesson 3), and the proof never uses quasi-concavity. Non-convex preferences can make a CE fail to exist, but if one exists it is PO. See Exercise 4.1.
4. In Step 5, why do the endowment terms drop out?
  • Endowments have zero value at equilibrium prices
  • Endowments are owned by the firms, not households
  • Both allocations share the same aggregate endowment
  • Walras' law sets the value of endowments to zero
Feasibility for \(x\) and for \(\bar x\) both contain \(\sum_i e^i\), the same physical resources, so subtracting the two valued constraints cancels \(p\cdot\sum_i e^i\).

5. Exercises

Exercise 4.1 Slides p.19 (convex utility)

"Suppose that all \(u\) are convex (instead of concave). Does the first welfare theorem hold?"

  1. Answer in general.
  2. Two agents, each endowed with \((1,1)\), each with \(u(x)=x_1^2+x_2^2\). Find a CE at \(p=(1,1)\) and verify that it is PO.
  3. Compare each agent's utility with the "equal split" allocation \((1,1),(1,1)\). What does this tell you about convexity and equal treatment?
Hint

A convex function on a budget line is maximized at an endpoint. Check whether the two agents can choose different corners that together clear the market.

Show solution

(a) Yes, whenever a CE exists and LNS holds. The proof never uses concavity or quasi-concavity. A strictly increasing convex \(u\) on \(\mathbb{R}^m_+\) satisfies LNS. The catch is existence: demands jump between corners, so a CE may fail to exist. But that is a separate question from efficiency.

(b) At \(p=(1,1)\), wealth is 2 and the budget is \(x_1+x_2\le2\). \(x_1^2+x_2^2\) is maximized at a corner, \((2,0)\) or \((0,2)\), each with utility 4. Let A choose \((2,0)\) and B choose \((0,2)\). Markets clear: \(2+0=2\) and \(0+2=2\). So this is a CE. By the 1WT it is PO. Directly: any feasible allocation has \(u^A+u^B=\|x^A\|^2+\|x^B\|^2\le\) its maximum on the box, which is 8 and is attained only at the corner allocations, so neither can gain without the other losing.

(c) The equal split gives each agent \(1+1=2<4\). With convex preferences, specializing is efficient and identical agents optimally consume different bundles. Equal treatment and efficiency can conflict. Convexity is what the Second Welfare Theorem will need (Lesson 6), not the first.

Exercise 4.3 Proof (weak PO for free)

Show that without any assumption on preferences (no LNS), every CE allocation is weakly Pareto optimal: no feasible allocation makes every household strictly better off. Which step of the proof are you avoiding?

Show solution

Suppose a feasible \(\{x^i,y^j\}\) has \(u^i(x^i)>u^i(\bar x^i)\) for all \(i\). Step 2's argument (which uses only optimality) now applies to every \(i\): \(p\cdot x^i>p\cdot\bar x^i\) for all \(i\). Summing gives (3). Steps 4–5 go through unchanged, giving a contradiction. We avoided Step 1, the only place LNS enters. LNS is needed only to handle the agents who are merely indifferent.

Exercise 4.4 Exam-style (locate the broken line)

For each modification, say whether the 1WT can fail and identify the first equation of the proof, (1)–(4) or Step 5, that can no longer be established:

  1. One household's utility is \(\min\{x_1+x_2,\,1\}\).
  2. A firm's chosen \(\bar y^j\) is feasible but does not maximize \(p\cdot y\) (say, a monopolist restricts output).
  3. Household 1's utility depends on household 2's consumption, \(u^1(x^1,x^2)\).
  4. There are countably many households and countably many goods, and \(p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i=\infty\).
Show solution

(a) Equation (1): the satiated household can have a weakly better, strictly cheaper bundle (Exercise 3.5).
(b) Equation (4): \(p\cdot\bar y^j\ge p\cdot y^j\) is no longer guaranteed. A dominating allocation can use a more valuable production plan.
(c) Equation (2) (and (1)): household 1's optimality was computed taking \(\bar x^2\) as given. In the dominating allocation, \(x^2\) changes too, so "\(u^1(x^1,x^2)>u^1(\bar x^1,\bar x^2)\)" no longer says \(x^1\) is better given \(\bar x^2\), and the revealed-preference argument says nothing about its cost.
(d) Equation (3): the strict inequality cannot be kept after summing infinite values. Lesson 12 shows this really happens in the OLG model.

Exercise 4.5 Proof (zero prices)

Show that in any CE where some household satisfies LNS, \(p\ne 0\). Where is this implicitly needed in Step 1?

Show solution

If \(p=0\), every bundle in \(X^i\) is affordable. LNS at \(\bar x^i\) gives a strictly better \(\hat x\in X^i\), which is affordable. This contradicts optimality, so \(p\ne0\). In the proof, \(p=0\) would make every valuation trivially zero, and (2) would read \(0>0\). The fact you just proved says this case never arises, so the price comparisons in Steps 1–5 are never vacuous. (Individual prices can still be zero, e.g. for a good nobody wants.)

Key takeaways

  • 1WT: CE + LNS ⇒ PO. The proof is by contradiction in five steps: (1) weakly better ⇒ weakly costlier [LNS]; (2) strictly better ⇒ strictly costlier; (3) sum over households; (4) profits were maximal; (5) feasibility gives \(+\) = \(\le 0\), a contradiction.
  • No convexity, continuity or differentiability is used. Non-convexities threaten existence, not efficiency.
  • Fragile points: satiation (1), externalities (2), infinite values (3), market power (4), missing markets (5).
