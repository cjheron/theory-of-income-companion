1. Where we are: one aggregate rule, three economies

Lesson 18 aggregated the households into three equations that hold for any path of interest rates \(r(t)\) and labour income \(Y(t)\) BY slide 16:

$$C = (p+\theta)(H+V),\qquad \dot V = rV + Y - C,\qquad \dot H = (r+p+\alpha)H - Y,$$

and, after eliminating \(H\),

$$\boxed{\;\dot C = [\,r + \alpha - \theta\,]\,C \;-\; (p+\alpha)(p+\theta)\,V\;}$$

To close the model we need to say what \(Y\), \(r\) and \(V\) are in general equilibrium. Alvarez does this three ways BY slide 19:

Notation: read before every Blanchard–Yaari formula
  • \(p\) is the death rate (expected lifetime \(1/p\)), not a price. \(\theta\) is the pure discount rate, not the PAYG tax of Lesson 14.
  • \(\alpha\) is the rate at which labour income declines with age, not the old-age endowment of Class Note II or a capital share. When we use Cobb–Douglas below, the capital share is called \(a\).
  • \(\mathcal F(K) \equiv F(K,1) - \delta K\) is output net of depreciation. So the golden rule is \(\mathcal F'(K_{GR}) = 0\), and \(r = \mathcal F'(K)\) with no "\(-\delta\)" in sight. (The slides write \(\mathcal F\) as a calligraphic F; plain \(F\) is the gross technology.)

2. Technology in continuous time

Take a neoclassical, constant-returns \(F(K,L)\) and labour supplied inelastically at \(L=1\) BY slide 22. The continuous-time equations are limits of discrete time with period length \(\Delta\):

  1. Feasibility at \(t\) (flows per unit time): \(I(t) + C(t) = F(K(t),1)\).
  2. Capital accumulation over a period of length \(\Delta\): \(K_{t+\Delta} = \Delta I_t + K_t(1-\Delta\delta)\). Rearranging, \(\dfrac{K_{t+\Delta}-K_t}{\Delta} = I_t - \delta K_t\), and letting \(\Delta\downarrow 0\) gives \(\dot K = I - \delta K\).
  3. Substitute \(I\) from feasibility: $$\dot K(t) = F(K(t),1) - \delta K(t) - C(t) \;=\; \mathcal F(K(t)) - C(t).$$

Factor prices

Competitive firms rent capital and hire labour BY slide 23:

  • Wage bill (aggregate labour income, \(L=1\)): \(Y = w = F_L(K,1) = F(K,1) - F_K(K,1)K = \mathcal F(K) - \mathcal F'(K)K\). The middle equality is Euler's theorem for CRS. The last one holds because the \(\delta K\) terms cancel: \(\mathcal F - \mathcal F'K = (F - \delta K) - (F_K - \delta)K\).
  • Interest rate. In discrete time, owning a unit of capital from \(t\) to \(t+\Delta\) pays the rental \(\nu_{t+\Delta}\Delta\) and loses \(\delta\Delta\) to depreciation. Arbitrage with a bond paying \(r_t\Delta\) gives \(\nu_{t+\Delta} = r_t + \delta\), and firms set \(F_K(K_{t+\Delta},1) = \nu_{t+\Delta}\). In the limit, \(F_K(K(t),1) = r(t)+\delta\), i.e. $$r(t) = \mathcal F'(K(t)).$$
  • Product exhaustion: \(rK + Y = \mathcal F'(K)K + \mathcal F(K) - \mathcal F'(K)K = \mathcal F(K)\). Net output is paid out entirely as interest and wages.

3. The equilibrium \((K,C)\) system

In a closed economy without government, the only asset households can hold in net terms is capital, so \(V(t) = \int_{-\infty}^t N(s,t)\,v(s,t)\,ds = K(t)\). Substituting \(V=K\), \(r=\mathcal F'(K)\) and \(Y = \mathcal F - \mathcal F'K\) into the two aggregate laws of motion BY slide 24:

  • \(\dot V = rV + Y - C\) becomes \(\dot K = \mathcal F'(K)K + \mathcal F(K) - \mathcal F'(K)K - C = \mathcal F(K) - C\). This is exactly the technology equation, as it must be (Walras' law again).
  • The \(\dot C\) equation becomes the "Euler equation" of this economy.
The Blanchard–Yaari dynamical system
$$\dot C = \big[\mathcal F'(K) + \alpha - \theta\big]\,C \;-\; (p+\alpha)(p+\theta)\,K, \qquad \dot K = \mathcal F(K) - C,$$

with \(K(0)\) given (predetermined) and \(C(0)\) free (a "jump" variable, since \(C(0) = (p+\theta)(K(0)+H(0))\) and \(H(0)\) depends on the whole future path of wages and interest rates).

Intuition: the extra term is generational turnover

Each individual obeys the Ramsey-like Euler equation \(\dot c/c = r - \theta\). Aggregate consumption grows more slowly, by the term \((p+\alpha)(p+\theta)K\). At every instant, a flow \(p\) of people die and are replaced by newborns who own no capital, so they consume less than the people they replace. With \(\alpha>0\) there is a second effect: the newborns' labour income is the highest anyone gets, while older people's income has decayed, so wealth shifts towards the old. Set \(p=\alpha=0\) and you get back the representative-agent Ramsey model, \(\dot C = (\mathcal F'(K)-\theta)C\).

4. The phase diagram

The recipe, from BY slide 25: draw the two loci, find the steady state, find the direction of motion everywhere, then discard the paths that eventually become infeasible.

The \(\dot K = 0\) locus: \(C = \mathcal F(K)\)

This curve is concave, starts at the origin with infinite slope (Inada), peaks at the golden rule \(\mathcal F'(K_{GR}) = 0\), and then falls, crossing zero at \(K_{\max}\) where depreciation eats all output BY slide 26. Above it, \(C > \mathcal F(K)\) and capital falls. Below it, capital rises.

The \(\dot C = 0\) locus: \(C = \dfrac{(p+\alpha)(p+\theta)}{\mathcal F'(K)+\alpha-\theta}\,K\)

This is defined where the denominator is positive, i.e. for \(K < \hat K\) with \(\mathcal F'(\hat K) = \theta - \alpha\). If \(\theta-\alpha \le -\delta\), no such \(\hat K\) exists and the locus is defined for all \(K\).

  • It passes through the origin, since \(\mathcal F'(K)\to\infty\) as \(K\to 0\).
  • It is increasing: the numerator \(K\) rises and the denominator falls because \(\mathcal F''<0\).
  • It shoots to \(+\infty\) as \(K\uparrow\hat K\) (the denominator goes to \(0^+\)).
  • It is convex. Alvarez states this (slide 26), and it holds for Cobb–Douglas; you can see it in the figure.

Direction: for \(K<\hat K\), the coefficient on \(C\) in \(\dot C\) is positive. So \(\dot C>0\) above the locus and \(\dot C<0\) below it. For \(K\ge\hat K\), both terms of \(\dot C\) are \(\le 0\), so consumption falls everywhere there.

The steady state is unique

Claim: unique interior steady state BY slide 26

There is exactly one \(\bar K\in(0,\min\{\hat K,K_{\max}\})\) with $$\frac{\mathcal F(\bar K)}{\bar K} \;=\; \frac{(p+\alpha)(p+\theta)}{\mathcal F'(\bar K)+\alpha-\theta}, \qquad \bar C = \mathcal F(\bar K).$$

Proof
  1. A steady state needs \(C = \mathcal F(K)\) and \(C\) on the \(\dot C=0\) locus. Divide by \(K>0\) to get the displayed equation. Since the right side is positive, we also need \(\mathcal F(K)>0\), i.e. \(K
  2. The left side \(\mathcal F(K)/K\) (average product) is strictly decreasing. Its derivative is \((\mathcal F'K - \mathcal F)/K^2 < 0\), because strict concavity with \(\mathcal F(0)=0\) gives \(\mathcal F(K) > \mathcal F'(K)K\). It tends to \(+\infty\) as \(K\to0\) (Inada) and to \(0\) at \(K_{\max}\).
  3. The right side is strictly increasing in \(K\) (its denominator falls). It tends to \(0\) as \(K\to 0\) and to \(+\infty\) as \(K\to\hat K\).
  4. A strictly decreasing function that starts above a strictly increasing one and ends below it crosses it exactly once.
∎

The same picture (LHS falling, RHS rising) drives every comparative static in §6: anything that raises the right-hand side at a given \(K\) lowers \(\bar K\).

The saddle path

The two loci cut the plane into four regions. In the region to the left of the steady state and above both loci, \(K\) falls and \(C\) rises: paths shoot up-left and hit \(K=0\) in finite time with \(C>0\), which is infeasible. Below and to the right, \(C\) falls while \(K\) first rises. Such paths eventually reach \(C = 0\) in finite time, because on the axis \(\dot C = -(p+\alpha)(p+\theta)K < 0\), so \(C\) goes negative, which is also infeasible BY slide 27. Between these two families there is exactly one upward-sloping curve \(C = S(K)\), the stable manifold or saddle path, that converges to \((\bar K,\bar C)\).

Perfect-foresight equilibrium

Given \(K(0)>0\), the equilibrium sets \(C(0) = S(K(0))\), and then \(C(t) = S(K(t))\) for all \(t\ge 0\), with \(K(t)\to\bar K\) monotonically.

Interactive phase diagram for \(F(K,1) = K^a\), so \(\mathcal F(K) = K^a - \delta K\). The black curve is \(\dot K=0\) (\(C=\mathcal F(K)\)). The blue curve is \(\dot C = 0\), with its asymptote at \(\hat K\) when it lies in view. The thick orange curve is the saddle path \(S(K)\), computed by integrating the system backwards in time from a point just off the steady state along the stable eigenvector. 5. Adjustment to the steady state

BY slide 29 asks: suppose \(0 < K(0) < \bar K\). Describe the paths of \(C\), \(K\), \(Y\), \(r\), \(H\). Are they monotone?

Transition from \(K(0)\), using the parameters of the phase diagram above. Left: \(K\), \(C\), labour income \(Y\) and human wealth \(H = C/(p+\theta) - K\), each divided by its steady-state value (so all converge to 1). Right: the interest rate \(r(t) = \mathcal F'(K(t))\) against its steady state \(\bar r\) (dashed). Slide \(K(0)/\bar K\) above 1 to see the reverse transition.
Answers, with reasons
  1. \(K(t)\) and \(C(t)\) both rise monotonically. The saddle path is upward sloping and lies below the \(\dot K = 0\) curve to the left of \(\bar K\), so \(\dot K>0\) all along it. \(K\) rises monotonically, and \(C = S(K)\) rises with it.
  2. \(Y(t)\) rises monotonically. \(Y = \mathcal F(K) - \mathcal F'(K)K\) has \(dY/dK = -\mathcal F''(K)K > 0\), and \(K\) is rising. (More capital per worker means a higher wage.)
  3. \(r(t)\) falls monotonically. \(r = \mathcal F'(K)\) with \(\mathcal F''<0\). Capital is scarce early, so its return is high.
  4. \(H(t)\) rises monotonically. Write \(H(t) = \int_0^\infty Y(t+u)\exp\!\big(-\!\int_0^u [r(t+v)+p+\alpha]\,dv\big)du\). Moving \(t\) later raises every future wage \(Y(t+u)\) and lowers every future interest rate \(r(t+v)\), so the integrand rises pointwise. (This needs \(\alpha\) and \(p\) constant, which they are.)
  5. Relation between \(S(K)\) and \(C = (\theta+p)(K+H)\) slide 27. The consumption rule \(C=(\theta+p)(K+H)\) holds at every date for any price path. In equilibrium the price path is itself a function of the current state, so human wealth is a function of \(K\) too: \(H(t) = h(K(t))\). The saddle path is just the consumption rule with this equilibrium human wealth plugged in: $$S(K) = (\theta+p)\,\big(K + h(K)\big).$$
  6. Is \(S\) linear? No, not in general. \(h(K)\) is the present value of the wages the economy will pay starting from \(K\), discounted at interest rates that depend on \(K\) nonlinearly through \(\mathcal F\). Numerically (\(a=0.33\), \(\delta=0.08\), \(\theta=0.03\), \(p=0.05\), \(\alpha=0\)), \(C/K\) falls from about \(0.48\) at \(K=0.3\bar K\) to \(0.29\) at \(\bar K\), so \(S\) is not a ray. It is close to linear only near \(\bar K\), where its slope is the stable eigenvector \(\mathcal F'(\bar K) - \lambda_s\).

6. Comparative statics of the steady state

Recall the steady-state equation \(\dfrac{\mathcal F(\bar K)}{\bar K} = \dfrac{(p+\alpha)(p+\theta)}{\mathcal F'(\bar K)+\alpha-\theta}\). Its left side is decreasing in \(K\), its right side increasing, and neither side depends on parameters except through the right side. So a parameter change that raises the right side at the old \(\bar K\) lowers \(\bar K\), and vice versa BY slide 30.

\(p\uparrow\)numerator \((p+\alpha)(p+\theta)\uparrow\)↓↑Shorter horizons: more of the capital stock is "lost" to newborns each instant, so there is less aggregate saving.
\(\theta\uparrow\)numerator ↑, denominator ↓↓↑More impatience.
\(\alpha\uparrow\)↓ if \(\mathcal F'(\bar K) < \theta+p\) (always true, see below)↑↓Income is front-loaded, so agents save when young to finance old age, even with flat consumption.
Proof that \(\bar K\) is increasing in \(\alpha\) BY slide 32
  1. Since \((p+\theta)\) does not involve \(\alpha\), it suffices to show \(\dfrac{p+\alpha}{\mathcal F'(\bar K)+\alpha-\theta}\) is decreasing in \(\alpha\) at the steady state.
  2. Differentiate, holding \(K\) fixed: \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\dfrac{p+\alpha}{\mathcal F'+\alpha-\theta} = \dfrac{(\mathcal F'+\alpha-\theta)-(p+\alpha)}{(\mathcal F'+\alpha-\theta)^2} = \dfrac{\mathcal F'(\bar K)-\theta-p}{(\mathcal F'+\alpha-\theta)^2}.\) This is negative iff \(\mathcal F'(\bar K) < \theta+p\).
  3. At \(\alpha=0\) we have \(\mathcal F'(\bar K) < \theta + p\) (proved next). So at \(\alpha=0\), raising \(\alpha\) lowers the RHS and raises \(\bar K\).
  4. A higher \(\bar K\) means an even lower \(\mathcal F'(\bar K)\), so \(\mathcal F'(\bar K)<\theta+p\) continues to hold as \(\alpha\) rises. Therefore the derivative stays negative and \(\bar K\) is increasing for all \(\alpha\ge0\).
∎

  2. Define \(K^{**}\) by \(\mathcal F'(K^{**}) = \theta+p\) and \(K^*\) by \(\mathcal F'(K^*) = \theta\). Then \(K^{**}
  3. Let \(g(K) \equiv \mathcal F(K)[\mathcal F'(K)-\theta] - p(p+\theta)K\). By the uniqueness argument, \(g>0\) to the left of \(\bar K\) and \(g<0\) between \(\bar K\) and \(\hat K\).
  4. Evaluate at \(K^{**}\): \(g(K^{**}) = p\,\mathcal F(K^{**}) - p(p+\theta)K^{**} = p\,[\mathcal F(K^{**}) - \mathcal F'(K^{**})K^{**}]\).

Watch the direction of the concavity inequality

Slide 31 states the concavity step as a comparison between \(\mathcal F(K^{**})\) and \((p+\theta)K^{**} = \mathcal F'(K^{**})K^{**}\). The direction that concavity delivers, and that the argument needs, is \(\mathcal F(K^{**}) > \mathcal F'(K^{**})K^{**}\). If your copy shows "\(<\)", read it as "\(>\)". You can check: for \(\mathcal F = \sqrt K\), \(\sqrt K > \tfrac{1}{2\sqrt K}K = \tfrac12\sqrt K\).

This is Blanchard's Figure 2 (BY slide 28): with \(\alpha=0\) the steady state lies strictly between \(K^{**}\) (\(\mathcal F'=\theta+p\)) and \(K^*\) (\(\mathcal F'=\theta\)). The individual consumption profile has \(\dot c/c = \bar r - \theta > 0\), so people consume more as they age and therefore save when young. That saving is what builds the capital stock.

Golden rule and dynamic (in)efficiency

  • \(\alpha = 0\): \(\bar r = \mathcal F'(\bar K) > \theta > 0\), so \(\bar K < K_{GR}\). The economy is dynamically efficient; compare the positive-interest case of Lesson 10.
  • \(\alpha>\theta\): \(\hat K\) satisfies \(\mathcal F'(\hat K) = \theta-\alpha<0\), so \(\hat K > K_{GR}\) and \(\bar K\) can exceed \(K_{GR}\), giving \(\bar r<0\). This is Blanchard's Figure 4 (BY slide 33). Agents save heavily for the years when their labour income has decayed.
Exact condition for \(\bar r < 0\)

\(\bar K > K_{GR}\) iff \(g\) is still positive at \(K_{GR}\), where now \(g(K) = \mathcal F(K)[\mathcal F'(K)+\alpha-\theta] - (p+\alpha)(p+\theta)K\). Since \(\mathcal F'(K_{GR})=0\), $$\bar r < 0 \iff (\alpha-\theta)\,\frac{\mathcal F(K_{GR})}{K_{GR}} \;>\; (p+\alpha)(p+\theta).$$ This requires \(\alpha>\theta\). Because the right side is increasing in \(p\), it holds when \(p\) is small enough relative to \(\alpha-\theta\).

A slide bullet to read carefully

Slide 31 says "if \(\alpha>\theta\), for \(p\) large enough, \(\bar K\) larger than golden rule." Since \(\bar K\) is decreasing in \(p\) (and \(\bar K\to0\) as \(p\to\infty\)), the condition above shows it is small \(p\) (long horizons) together with \(\alpha>\theta\) that pushes \(\bar K\) past the golden rule. The figure confirms this: with \(a=0.33\), \(\delta=0.08\), \(\theta=0.01\), \(\alpha=0.06\), you get \(\bar r\approx-0.024\) at \(p=0.02\) but \(\bar r\approx+0.082\) at \(p=0.2\). The bullet probably means "for \(\alpha\) large enough". Ask Alvarez or a TA if in doubt. Also remember that \(p\to0\) forces \(\alpha=0\) (§7), so the inefficient region needs \(p>0\).

7. The infinite-lifetime limit \(p\downarrow 0\)

BY slide 34 With \(p=0\) nobody dies and nobody is born. If individual labour income decayed at rate \(\alpha>0\), aggregate labour income would decay too, so a stationary economy requires \(\alpha=0\). The system becomes the Ramsey–Cass–Koopmans model:

$$\dot C = [\mathcal F'(K)-\theta]\,C, \qquad \dot K = \mathcal F(K) - C.$$
  • The \(\dot C=0\) locus is the vertical line \(\mathcal F'(\bar K)=\theta\) (the modified golden rule). In the figure, the blue curve steepens towards this line as \(p,\alpha\to0\).
  • A slightly different argument rules out the non-saddle paths. Paths below the saddle path now have \(C\to0\) only asymptotically: \(\dot C/C = \mathcal F'-\theta\) is bounded once \(K>K^*\), so \(C\) never actually hits zero. These paths are feasible, so infeasibility cannot rule them out. Instead they violate the household's transversality condition: they converge to \(K_{\max}\) with \(r=\mathcal F'(K_{\max})<0\), so wealth discounted at \(r\) explodes. With \(p>0\), the extra term \(-(p+\alpha)(p+\theta)K\) makes \(C\) hit zero in finite time, which is simpler.
  • The adjustment from \(K(0)<\bar K\) looks the same: \(K, C, Y\) rise and \(r\) falls.

8. Check your understanding

1. Why does aggregate consumption grow more slowly than individual consumption \(\dot c/c = r-\theta\)?
  • Old agents face a higher interest rate on their annuities
  • Dying agents are replaced by newborns who hold no wealth
  • Newborns discount the future at a higher rate than adults
  • Firms retain part of profits instead of paying dividends
The term \(-(p+\alpha)(p+\theta)V\) is generational turnover. Wealthy dying cohorts are replaced by newborns with \(v(t,t)=0\). With \(\alpha>0\), wealth also tilts towards the old, whose labour income has decayed. Everyone faces the same \(r+p\) on annuities and has the same \(\theta\).
2. With \(\alpha=0\) and \(p>0\), where does the steady-state interest rate lie?
  • Exactly at θ, as in the Ramsey model
  • Below zero, past the golden-rule level
  • Between zero and θ, below impatience
  • Strictly between θ and θ plus p
\(\theta<\mathcal F'(\bar K)<\theta+p\). The lower bound comes from the sign of the steady-state equation; the upper bound from concavity (\(\mathcal F > \mathcal F'K\)) evaluated at \(K^{**}\). So the economy is below the golden rule and dynamically efficient.
3. Along the transition from \(K(0)<\bar K\), which variable falls?
  • The interest rate r
  • Human wealth H
  • Labour income Y
  • Consumption C
\(K\) rises along the saddle path, so \(r=\mathcal F'(K)\) falls while \(Y = \mathcal F - \mathcal F'K\) rises. \(H\) rises because future wages rise and future discount rates fall. \(C=S(K)\) rises.
4. Which parameter change raises steady-state capital \(\bar K\)?
  • A higher death rate p
  • A higher discount rate θ
  • A faster income decline α
  • A higher depreciation δ
Raising \(\alpha\) lowers the right side \((p+\alpha)(p+\theta)/(\mathcal F'+\alpha-\theta)\) whenever \(\mathcal F'(\bar K)<\theta+p\), which always holds. So the intersection with the falling average-product curve moves right. Higher \(p\) or \(\theta\) raise the right side. Higher \(\delta\) lowers net average product and \(\mathcal F'\), which shrinks \(\bar K\).
5. Why must \(C(0)\) lie on the saddle path when \(p>0\)?
  • Paths elsewhere violate the government budget constraint
  • Paths elsewhere reach zero capital or consumption in finite time
  • Paths elsewhere converge to a second, unstable steady state
  • Paths elsewhere imply negative wages in the long run
Above \(S\), \(C\) keeps rising while \(K\) falls, so \(K\) hits 0 with \(C>0\). Below \(S\), \(\dot C<0\) with \(\dot C = -(p+\alpha)(p+\theta)K<0\) even at \(C=0\), so \(C\) turns negative in finite time. Both are infeasible. (With \(p=0\) we need the transversality condition instead.)

9. Exercises

Exercise 19.1 Proof (the steady state is a saddle)

Linearize the \((K,C)\) system around \((\bar K,\bar C)\). Show that the Jacobian has determinant \(<0\), so it has one negative and one positive eigenvalue. Then find the slope of the saddle path at the steady state.

Hint

Use the steady-state condition to replace \((p+\alpha)(p+\theta)\) by \(\mathcal F(\bar K)(\mathcal F'(\bar K)+\alpha-\theta)/\bar K\).

Show solution

\(J = \begin{pmatrix} \mathcal F' & -1 \\ \mathcal F''\bar C - (p+\alpha)(p+\theta) & \mathcal F'+\alpha-\theta \end{pmatrix}\), evaluated at \(\bar K\). Then $$\det J = \mathcal F'(\mathcal F'+\alpha-\theta) + \mathcal F''\bar C - (p+\alpha)(p+\theta).$$ Substitute \((p+\alpha)(p+\theta) = \tfrac{\mathcal F}{\bar K}(\mathcal F'+\alpha-\theta)\): $$\det J = (\mathcal F'+\alpha-\theta)\Big(\mathcal F' - \tfrac{\mathcal F}{\bar K}\Big) + \mathcal F''\bar C.$$ The first factor is \(>0\) (we are left of \(\hat K\)), the second is \(<0\) (marginal < average product), and \(\mathcal F''\bar C<0\). So \(\det J<0\). The eigenvalues \(\lambda_s<0<\lambda_u\) are real with opposite signs: a saddle. The first row gives the eigenvector: \((\mathcal F'-\lambda)\,dK - dC = 0\), so the saddle path has slope \(dC/dK = \mathcal F'(\bar K) - \lambda_s\). This is positive whenever \(\mathcal F'(\bar K)\ge0\), and the figure uses exactly this direction to start its backward integration.

  3. Interpret: why do longer expected lifetimes make overaccumulation possible here, while they make it impossible when \(\alpha=0\)?
Show solution

(a) The golden rule has \(aK^{a-1}=\delta\), so \(K_{GR}^{a-1} = \delta/a\) and \(\mathcal F/K = K^{a-1}-\delta = \delta/a-\delta = \delta(1-a)/a = 0.2\).
(b) We need \((\alpha-\theta)\cdot0.2 > (p+\alpha)(p+\theta)\), i.e. \(0.008 > p^2+0.06p+0.0005\). The positive root of \(p^2+0.06p-0.0075=0\) is \(\bar p = (-0.06+\sqrt{0.0336})/2\approx0.0617\). So \(\bar r<0\) iff \(p<0.0617\) (expected lifetime above about 16 years).
(c) With \(\alpha>\theta\), people want to save for the period when their earnings have decayed. The longer they expect to live, the more they save, and turnover (which redistributes capital to wealthless newborns) is slower. With \(\alpha=0\), saving comes only from the \(\bar r>\theta\) tilt, which requires \(\bar r>0\). As \(p\to0\), the economy converges to Ramsey's modified golden rule \(\bar r=\theta>0\), never above the golden rule.

Exercise 19.4 Exam-style (an unexpected rise in mortality)

The economy (\(\alpha=0\)) is in steady state. At \(t=0\) the death rate rises unexpectedly and permanently from \(p\) to \(p'>p\). Describe the paths of \(K\), \(C\) and \(r\) using the phase diagram. Does \(C\) jump up or down on impact? Check your answer with the interactive figure.

Show solution

The \(\dot K=0\) locus does not depend on \(p\). The \(\dot C=0\) locus shifts up (bigger numerator), so the new steady state has \(\bar K'<\bar K\), and \(\bar C' = \mathcal F(\bar K')<\bar C\) since we are left of the golden rule. \(K\) cannot jump, so on impact \(C\) jumps onto the new saddle path at the old \(K=\bar K\). To the right of the new steady state, the new saddle path lies above \(C=\mathcal F(K)\) (that is where \(\dot K<0\)). So \(C(0^+) > \mathcal F(\bar K) = \bar C\): consumption jumps up. Then \(K\) and \(C\) fall monotonically to the new steady state, and \(r\) rises monotonically. Intuition: higher mortality raises everyone's marginal propensity to consume out of wealth, \(p+\theta\), so current consumption rises and saving falls.

Exercise 19.5 Slides p.27 Proof

(a) Show that any path starting below the saddle path reaches \(C=0\) in finite time when \(p>0\). (b) Explain why this argument fails when \(p=\alpha=0\), and which condition replaces it.

Show solution

(a) Take a path below \(S\). It eventually enters the region \(\mathcal R = \{C < \mathcal F(K),\ C < \text{the } \dot C=0 \text{ locus}\}\), where \(\dot K>0\) and \(\dot C<0\). (If it starts left of \(\bar K\) above the \(\dot C=0\) locus, it moves up-right but passes below the steady state, so it crosses the locus.) \(\mathcal R\) is invariant. The path moves right and down, while the \(\dot C=0\) locus is increasing, so it cannot cross that locus again. At the \(\dot K=0\) boundary, \(\dot K=0\) and \(\dot C<0\), so the path moves straight down, away from the boundary. Inside \(\mathcal R\), \(K\) is increasing and bounded by \(K_{\max}\), and \(C\) is decreasing. If \(C\) stayed positive, \((K,C)\) would converge to a rest point of the system in the closure of \(\mathcal R\). The only candidates with \(K>0\) are \((\bar K,\bar C)\), which is not in that closure for a path strictly below \(S\), and points with \(C=0\). But at \(C=0\), \(\dot C = -(p+\alpha)(p+\theta)K<0\), so no such point is a rest point. Hence \(C\) reaches \(0\) in finite time with \(\dot C<0\), and the path becomes infeasible.
(b) With \(p=\alpha=0\), \(\dot C = (\mathcal F'-\theta)C\) is proportional to \(C\), so \(C\) decays only exponentially and never reaches 0. The path converges to \((K_{\max},0)\), which is feasible but violates the transversality condition \(\lim_{T\to\infty}K(T)e^{-\int_0^T r}=0\), since \(r=\mathcal F'(K_{\max})<0\). That is the "slightly different argument" of slide 34.

Exercise 19.6 Slides p.29

Starting from \(K(0) > \bar K\), describe the paths of \(C\), \(K\), \(Y\), \(r\), \(H\). Then explain why aggregate human wealth \(H\) can move even though nothing happens to any individual's labour endowment profile.

Show solution

Everything reverses: \(K\), \(C\), \(Y\) and \(H\) fall monotonically and \(r\) rises monotonically (set \(K(0)/\bar K>1\) in the transition figure). \(H\) is a price-weighted object: the present value of wages, which depend on \(K\), discounted at \(r+p+\alpha\), where \(r\) also depends on \(K\). It moves with general-equilibrium prices even though each person's labour services profile is fixed.

Key takeaways

  • With capital: \(\dot C = [\mathcal F'(K)+\alpha-\theta]C - (p+\alpha)(p+\theta)K\) and \(\dot K = \mathcal F(K)-C\). It is Ramsey plus a generational-turnover term.
  • \(\dot K=0\): \(C=\mathcal F(K)\), hump-shaped with its peak at the golden rule. \(\dot C=0\): increasing and convex, with an asymptote at \(\mathcal F'(\hat K)=\theta-\alpha\). The steady state is unique (falling average product vs rising RHS), and it is a saddle.
  • From \(K(0)<\bar K\): \(K,C,Y,H\) rise and \(r\) falls, monotonically. \(S(K) = (\theta+p)(K+h(K))\) is nonlinear in general.
  • \(\bar K\) falls with \(p\) and \(\theta\) and rises with \(\alpha\). With \(\alpha=0\): \(\theta<\bar r<\theta+p\), so the economy is dynamically efficient. With \(\alpha>\theta\) and long enough lifetimes, \(\bar r<0\) is possible.
  • As \(p\to0\) (forcing \(\alpha=0\)), we get Ramsey: a vertical \(\dot C=0\) locus at \(\mathcal F'=\theta\), and the transversality condition rules out the bad paths.
