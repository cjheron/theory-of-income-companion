Is the OLG Equilibrium Efficient? The Best Symmetric Allocation

Covers Class Note II, pp. 15–18 · About 35 min · Prerequisite: Lesson 9
By the end you can:
  1. define symmetric allocations and derive the best symmetric allocation \((c_y^*,c_o^*) = (1-\beta,\beta)\);
  2. compare it to the CE \((1-\alpha,\alpha)\) for every agent, including the initial old, and classify the three cases;
  3. state and explain the punchline: the CE is Pareto-dominated exactly when \(\bar r<0\), a counterexample to the First Welfare Theorem.
Primary source Alvarez, Class Note II, pp. 15–18. Samuelson's 1958 paper, §§III–IV, is where this "consumption-loan paradox" was first spotted. Shell, "Notes on the Economics of Infinity", JPE (1971), distils why it happens. We'll come back to it in Lesson 12.

1. The question

Lesson 4 proved that every CE with locally non-satiated preferences is Pareto optimal. Log utility is strictly increasing, hence LNS. So the First Welfare Theorem seems to say the autarky equilibrium of Lessons 8–9 is PO. Here we test that claim directly, by searching for a feasible allocation that everybody weakly prefers. To keep the search tractable, we restrict attention to allocations that treat every generation the same way.

2. Symmetric allocations

Definition: symmetric allocation II p.15

An allocation is symmetric if consumption depends only on age, not on the generation. It is described by two numbers \((c_y,c_o)\): $$x^i_i = c_y\ \ (i\ge1),\qquad x^i_{i+1} = c_o\ \ (i\ge0).$$ Note that the initial old also receive \(c_o\). Since \(\bar e_t=1\), feasibility at each date reads \(x^{t-1}_t + x^t_t = c_o + c_y = 1\): one condition, the same at every date.

The CE \((1-\alpha,\alpha)\) is itself a feasible symmetric allocation, since \(1-\alpha+\alpha = 1\). So the set we are searching contains the equilibrium, which is what makes the comparison clean.

3. The best symmetric allocation

"Best" here means best from the point of view of a generation \(i\ge1\), all of whom have the same \(v\) II p.16:

$$\max_{c_y,c_o}\;(1-\beta)\log c_y + \beta\log c_o\quad\text{s.t.}\quad c_y + c_o = 1 .$$
Derivation
  1. Substitute the constraint. Maximize \(f(c_y) = (1-\beta)\log c_y + \beta\log(1-c_y)\) over \(c_y\in(0,1)\). \(f\) is strictly concave, so the FOC is necessary and sufficient.
  2. FOC. \(\frac{1-\beta}{c_y} = \frac{\beta}{c_o}\), i.e. \(\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}\frac{c_y}{c_o} = 1\): the MRS equals the "technological" rate at which feasibility lets you swap youth for old-age goods, which is one-for-one.
  3. Solve with feasibility. \(c_y = \tfrac{1-\beta}{\beta}c_o\) and \(c_y+c_o=1\) give $$\boxed{\;c_y^* = 1-\beta,\qquad c_o^* = \beta.\;}$$
∎
Intuition: the "golden rule" of the endowment economy

Along the symmetric frontier, giving the old one more unit costs the young exactly one unit, because there is one old person per young person and no growth. The best allocation equates the MRS to this biological rate of return of 1. Equivalently, the implied interest rate at \((1-\beta,\beta)\) is \(r = v_1/v_2 - 1 = 0\), the population growth rate. In Lesson 13, with growth, the biological rate becomes \((1+n)(1+g)\).

5. Why Case 1 is a genuine Pareto improvement: the chain of transfers

Read the move from CE to best symmetric in Case 1 as a transfer. Each young person hands \(\beta-\alpha>0\) units to the contemporaneous old person:

  • The initial old receive \(\beta-\alpha\) and give nothing. They gain.
  • Each generation \(i\ge1\) gives \(\beta-\alpha\) when young and receives \(\beta-\alpha\) when old. This is a "loan" at a zero interest rate. In the CE their marginal rate of substitution at the endowment implies a rate \(\bar r<0\), so a zero return beats what the market offered: they would be willing to save at a negative return, and here they get a zero one.
  • The scheme is feasible at every date because aggregate consumption is still \(1\), and it never has to be "repaid", because there is no last generation.
The chain must never end

If the transfers stopped at some date \(T\), generation \(T-1\) would give when young and receive nothing when old. They would be worse off, so the scheme would no longer be a Pareto improvement. The gain to the initial old is financed by an infinite sequence of future generations, each passing the bill forward. This is possible only because the economy has infinitely many agents and dates. That double infinity is the heart of Lesson 12.

Case 3: why there's no free lunch when \(\bar r>0\)

When \(\bar r>0\), the young would rather borrow against their large old-age endowment. Moving toward \((1-\beta,\beta)\) means transferring from the old to the young. Generations \(i\ge1\) like it (they receive when young, repay when old at rate \(0<\bar r\)), but the initial old pay without being repaid. Someone always loses, so the CE is not dominated by any symmetric allocation (Exercise 10.3). In fact it is Pareto optimal among all allocations, as Lesson 12 will show using the finiteness of \(\sum_t p_t\).

6. Check your understanding

1. Why do generations \(i\ge 1\) always weakly prefer the best symmetric allocation to the CE?
  • Because the best allocation gives them strictly more of both goods
  • Because the CE is one of the symmetric allocations being maximized over
  • Because the interest rate is negative in every possible parametrization
  • Because initial old consumption is irrelevant to their own utility level
\((1-\alpha,\alpha)\) satisfies \(c_y+c_o=1\), so it is in the feasible set of the problem whose solution is \((1-\beta,\beta)\). The maximum is at least as good as any feasible point, and strictly better unless they coincide.
2. In Case 3 (\(\beta<\alpha\)), why is the CE not Pareto-dominated by the best symmetric allocation?
  • The young prefer their endowment to every other symmetric bundle
  • The best symmetric allocation is infeasible once β falls below α
  • The interest rate is zero, so every allocation gives equal utility
  • The initial old would consume β instead of α, which is strictly less
Generations \(i\ge1\) still strictly prefer \((1-\beta,\beta)\). But the initial old get \(\beta<\alpha\), so they are strictly worse off. One agent losing is enough to rule out Pareto dominance.
3. What implicit interest rate does generation \(i\ge1\) earn on the transfer scheme that implements the best symmetric allocation?
  • Zero, since it gives β − α young and gets β − α old
  • The market rate r̄, since the scheme mimics a bond
  • Negative, since it gives more than it later receives
  • Infinite, since only the initial old ever contribute
Each generation pays \(\beta-\alpha\) and receives \(\beta-\alpha\) one period later: gross return 1, so net return 0. In Case 1 the market rate \(\bar r\) is negative, so this beats the market. That's why generations \(i\ge1\) gain.
4. Which feature of the economy makes the Case 1 improvement possible?
  • Log utility, which makes savings increasing in rates
  • A constant aggregate endowment equal to one forever
  • Infinitely many generations, so no one is left holding the bill
  • The initial old caring only about consumption at date one
The transfer chain never ends, so no generation is left paying without being repaid. With a final generation \(T\), backward induction would unravel any such scheme. The double infinity of agents and goods is the key (Lesson 12).

7. Exercises

Exercise 10.1 Slides p.16–18

Let \(\alpha = 0.2\), \(\beta = 0.5\).

  1. Compute the utility gain of each generation \(i\ge1\) from moving from the CE to the best symmetric allocation, \(v(1-\beta,\beta)-v(1-\alpha,\alpha)\).
  2. Compute the consumption gain of the initial old.
  3. Which case is this, and what is \(\bar r\)?
Show solution

(a) \(\tfrac12\log\tfrac{0.5}{0.8} + \tfrac12\log\tfrac{0.5}{0.2} = \tfrac12\log(0.625\cdot2.5) = \tfrac12\log 1.5625 \approx 0.223\).
(b) \(\beta - \alpha = 0.3\).
(c) Case 1, since \(\beta>\alpha\). \(\bar r = -0.3/0.4 = -0.75\).

Exercise 10.2 Exam-style

Generalize the best symmetric allocation to arbitrary preferences: let \(v\) be strictly increasing, strictly quasi-concave and differentiable. (a) Characterize the best symmetric allocation. (b) Show that the CE is dominated by it whenever \(v_2/v_1>1\) at the endowment, i.e. whenever \(\bar r<0\). (c) Is \(\bar r<0\) also necessary for some symmetric allocation to dominate the CE?

Show solution

(a) \(\max v(c_y,1-c_y)\): FOC \(v_1 = v_2\), i.e. MRS \(=1\), implied rate \(r=0\).

(b) Write \(c_o = \alpha+\tau\), \(c_y = 1-\alpha-\tau\) along the line. Then \(\frac{d}{d\tau}v = -v_1+v_2\), which at \(\tau=0\) is \(v_1(v_2/v_1-1) = v_1\cdot(\tfrac{1}{1+\bar r}-1) > 0\) iff \(\bar r<0\). So a small \(\tau>0\) raises the utility of generations \(i\ge1\) and raises the initial old's consumption: a Pareto improvement. The best symmetric allocation, which maximizes \(v\) along the line, has \(v\) at least as large, and \(c_o^*>\alpha\) because \(v\) is still increasing in \(\tau\) at \(\tau=0\) and is quasi-concave along the line.

(c) Yes. If \(\bar r\ge0\), then the initial old need \(\tau\ge0\), but along the line \(v\) is quasi-concave in \(\tau\) with \(dv/d\tau\le0\) at \(\tau=0\). So it is non-increasing for all \(\tau\ge0\) and strictly decreasing unless \(\bar r = 0\) and \(\tau=0\). Generations \(i\ge1\) lose (or, in Case 2, nobody gains), so no symmetric improvement exists.

Exercise 10.3 Proof

Show that in Case 3 (\(\beta<\alpha\)) no feasible symmetric allocation Pareto-dominates the CE.

Show solution

Let \((c_y,c_o)\) be feasible (\(c_y+c_o\le1\)) and weakly preferred by everyone. The initial old need \(c_o\ge\alpha\), so \(c_y\le1-\alpha\). Generations \(i\ge1\) need \(v(c_y,c_o)\ge v(1-\alpha,\alpha)\). Since \(v\) is increasing, it suffices to check \(c_o = 1-c_y\). The function \(f(c_y) = v(c_y,1-c_y)\) is strictly concave with maximum at \(1-\beta > 1-\alpha\), so it is strictly increasing on \((0,1-\alpha]\). Hence for \(c_y\le 1-\alpha\), \(f(c_y)\le f(1-\alpha)\), with equality only at \(c_y = 1-\alpha\). So the only weakly preferred feasible symmetric allocation is the CE itself, and nobody is strictly better off.

Exercise 10.4 Exam-style

In Case 1, a planner proposes a one-time transfer: at date 1, generation 1 gives \(\varepsilon>0\) to generation 0, and nothing else changes. Is this a Pareto improvement? What about a transfer of \(\varepsilon\) from young to old at every date \(t=1,\dots,T\) and none after? Explain the general lesson.

Show solution

Neither is a Pareto improvement. In the one-time scheme, generation 1 loses \(\varepsilon\) when young and gets nothing back. In the \(T\)-period scheme, generations \(1,\dots,T-1\) gain (for \(\varepsilon\) small, since \(\bar r<0\)), but generation \(T\) pays when young and receives nothing when old, so it loses. Lesson: every Pareto improvement over the Case-1 CE must involve transfers from young to old at every date forever. In a finite-horizon OLG economy the CE would be PO, by the ordinary First Welfare Theorem.

Exercise 10.5 Proof (harder)

Show that the best symmetric allocation \((1-\beta,\beta)\) is Pareto optimal among all feasible allocations, not just symmetric ones.

Hint

Write any feasible allocation that doesn't waste goods as \(x^t_t = 1-\beta-\delta_t\) and \(x^{t-1}_t = \beta+\delta_t\), where \(\delta_t\) is the extra amount given to the old at date \(t\). Use \(\log(1+z)\le z\) and \(\log(1-z)\le -z - z^2/2\) for \(z\in[0,1)\) to bound each generation's utility gain.

Show solution

Wasting goods never helps, so take feasibility with equality and parametrize as in the hint. The initial old need \(\delta_1\ge0\). Generation \(t\)'s utility change is $$\Delta_t = (1-\beta)\log\Big(1-\tfrac{\delta_t}{1-\beta}\Big) + \beta\log\Big(1+\tfrac{\delta_{t+1}}{\beta}\Big).$$ Suppose \(\delta_t\ge0\) (true for \(t=1\)). Apply \(\log(1-z)\le -z-z^2/2\) with \(z=\delta_t/(1-\beta)\in[0,1)\), and \(\log(1+z)\le z\) (valid for every \(z>-1\)) with \(z = \delta_{t+1}/\beta\): $$\Delta_t \;\le\; -\delta_t - \kappa\,\delta_t^2 + \delta_{t+1},\qquad \kappa = \tfrac{1}{2(1-\beta)} > 0 .$$ So \(\Delta_t\ge0\) requires \(\delta_{t+1}\ge\delta_t + \kappa\delta_t^2\ge\delta_t\ge0\). By induction from \(\delta_1\ge0\), every \(\delta_t\ge0\) and the sequence is non-decreasing. If some \(\delta_k>0\), then for \(t\ge k\), \(\delta_{t+1}\ge\delta_t(1+\kappa\delta_k)\). The sequence grows geometrically and eventually exceeds \(1-\beta\), so young consumption becomes negative: infeasible. Hence all \(\delta_t = 0\), the allocation is the best symmetric one, and nobody is strictly better off. ∎

Remark: this "transfers must grow geometrically to keep everyone happy" logic is the essence of the Balasko–Shell/Cass characterization of efficiency in OLG economies. It reappears in the many-period argument of Lesson 16.

Key takeaways

  • Symmetric allocations: \((c_y,c_o)\) with \(c_y+c_o=1\). The CE \((1-\alpha,\alpha)\) is one of them.
  • Best symmetric: \((1-\beta,\beta)\). The MRS equals the biological return of 1, so the implied interest rate is 0.
  • Generations \(i\ge1\) always weakly prefer it. The initial old prefer it iff \(\beta>\alpha\). CE Pareto-dominated \(\iff\beta>\alpha\iff\bar r<0\).
  • The improvement is an endless chain of young-to-old transfers. It works only because there is no last generation.
