Why the First Welfare Theorem Fails in OLG

Covers Class Note II, pp. 21–27 · About 35 min · Prerequisites: Lesson 4, Lesson 10
By the end you can:
  1. point to the exact line of the First Welfare Theorem proof that breaks in the OLG economy, and say why;
  2. compute the value of the aggregate endowment \(p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i\) and classify the three cases of Lesson 10 by whether it is finite;
  3. prove that the 1WT survives with infinitely many agents or infinitely many goods, so it is the double infinity that causes the failure.
Primary source Alvarez, Class Note II, pp. 21–27. The classic diagnosis is Karl Shell, "Notes on the Economics of Infinity", JPE 79(5), 1971. It is ten pages long and very readable. The original model is Samuelson, "An Exact Consumption-Loan Model of Interest…", JPE 66(6), 1958.

1. Where we are

In Lesson 10 we found, in the log example with endowments \((1-\alpha,\alpha)\) and preferences \((1-\beta)\log c_y+\beta\log c_o\), that the unique CE is autarky, with $$\bar r = \frac{\alpha-\beta}{\beta(1-\alpha)}, \qquad p_t = \Big(\frac{1}{1+\bar r}\Big)^{t-1}.$$ When \(\beta>\alpha\) (Case 1, \(\bar r<0\)), the best symmetric allocation \((1-\beta,\beta)\) makes every generation, including the initial old, strictly better off. So the CE is not Pareto optimal. Yet log utility is strictly increasing, so local non-satiation holds. The theorem's only hypothesis is satisfied and its conclusion fails. Something in the proof must break N2 p.21.

2. Re-run the proof and watch it break

Recall the structure of the proof from Lesson 4 (pure exchange, no firms). Suppose a feasible \(\{x^i\}\) Pareto-dominates the CE \(\{\bar x^i\}\).

The 1WT proof, line by line, in the OLG economy N2 pp.24–25
  1. Agent by agent: still fine. LNS and utility maximization give \(p\cdot x^i\ge p\cdot\bar x^i\) for all \(i\), and \(p\cdot x^{i'}>p\cdot\bar x^{i'}\) for some \(i'\). Each generation lives two periods, so each \(p\cdot x^i\) is a sum of two finite numbers. Nothing infinite has appeared yet.
  2. Sum over agents: this is where it breaks. We want to conclude $$\sum_{i\in I} p\cdot x^i \;>\; \sum_{i\in I} p\cdot \bar x^i. \tag{$\ast$}$$ Adding up a strict and infinitely many weak inequalities gives a strict inequality between the sums only if the sums are finite numbers. If \(\sum_i p\cdot\bar x^i=+\infty\), then both sides are \(+\infty\), and \((\ast)\) would say "\(\infty>\infty\)", which is false N2 p.25.
  3. Feasibility: holds, but can't produce a contradiction. Both allocations exhaust the same aggregate endowment, so \(p\cdot\sum_i x^i = p\cdot\sum_i e^i = p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i\). With finite values this contradicts \((\ast)\). With infinite values it is the harmless statement \(\infty=\infty\).
The 1WT with infinitely many agents and goods N2 p.21

Let \((p,\{\bar x^i\})\) be a CE and suppose all preferences satisfy LNS. If, in addition, the value of the aggregate consumption is finite, $$p\cdot\sum_{i\in I}\bar x^i\lt \infty,$$ then \(\{\bar x^i\}\) is Pareto optimal.

Sufficient, not necessary

Finite value is a sufficient condition for efficiency. When it fails, the proof fails, but the CE need not be inefficient. Case 2 (\(\alpha=\beta\), \(\bar r=0\)) has infinite value, yet the CE coincides with the best symmetric allocation and is in fact Pareto optimal (Exercise 12.7). The sharp characterization is due to Cass (1972) and Balasko–Shell (1980). Roughly, a CE in this kind of economy is efficient iff \(\sum_t 1/p_t=\infty\). This goes beyond the slides; know that it exists.

3. Compute the value of the aggregate endowment

At each date \(t\ge1\) aggregate consumption is \(\bar x^{t-1}_t+\bar x^t_t = \bar e_t = 1\), so \(\sum_{i=0}^\infty\bar x^i = (1,1,1,\dots)\) N2 p.22. Its value is a geometric series: $$p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i=\lim_{T\to\infty}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Big(\frac{1}{1+\bar r}\Big)^{t-1} =\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1+\bar r}{\bar r}\Big[1-\Big(\frac{1}{1+\bar r}\Big)^{T}\Big]\quad(\bar r\neq0),$$ and the partial sum is just \(T\) when \(\bar r=0\) N2 p.23.

4. The Pareto improvement, priced at CE prices

It helps to see the failure in numbers. In Case 1, compare the improving allocation \(x^*\) (the best symmetric one) with the CE \(\bar x=e\) at CE prices. Write \(q\equiv 1/(1+\bar r) = p_{t+1}/p_t\), so \(q>1\) when \(\bar r<0\).

  • Initial old: \(p\cdot x^{*0}-p\cdot\bar x^0 = p_1(\beta-\alpha)>0\).
  • Generation \(t\ge1\): \(p\cdot x^{*t}-p\cdot\bar x^t = p_t\big[(1-\beta)-(1-\alpha)\big]+p_{t+1}(\beta-\alpha) = p_t(\beta-\alpha)(q-1)>0\).

Every agent's new bundle is strictly more expensive at CE prices. That is exactly what steps 1–2 of the proof predict. Yet both allocations are feasible and use up the same aggregate endowment \((1,1,\dots)\). With finitely many agents this would be impossible: a finite sum of strictly larger numbers is strictly larger. With infinitely many agents and an infinite total value it is perfectly consistent.

The transfer chain (Hilbert's hotel). Moving from \((1-\alpha,\alpha)\) to \((1-\beta,\beta)\) means every young person hands \(\varepsilon=\beta-\alpha\) to the current old person (arrows). The initial old receive \(\varepsilon\) and give nothing. Every later generation gives \(\varepsilon\) when young and receives \(\varepsilon\) when old. When \(\beta>\alpha\), each generation wants that trade, because its MRS at the endowment exceeds 1. With finitely many generations, someone, the last young, would give without receiving. With infinitely many generations there is no last one, so the chain never has to be "closed". No market price can replicate it: each young person would be lending to someone who will be dead when the loan comes due.

5. It takes a double infinity

Why didn't this come up in Lesson 4? Because there both the set of agents and the set of goods were finite, so every sum was finite. The two slide questions below show that relaxing one of the two finiteness assumptions is not enough to break the theorem N2 pp.26–27. Shell (1971) calls this the double infinity of traders and dated commodities.

Intuition: what the infinite value means

An infinite value of the aggregate endowment says the economy's total wealth at date-1 prices is unbounded. When \(\bar r<0\), goods in the far future are more valuable than goods today, and there is no end to the future. A pay-as-you-go scheme (Lesson 11) or fiat money is a way of tapping that wealth. Each generation is paid back by the next, at the implicit return \(0>\bar r\). With growth, that return becomes \((1+g)(1+n)-1\) (Lesson 13).

6. Check your understanding

1. Which step of the 1WT proof fails in the OLG example with \(\bar r<0\)?
  • Showing each dominating bundle costs at least as much
  • Summing the cost inequalities into a strict aggregate one
  • Using local non-satiation to rule out cheaper bundles
  • Checking that the dominating allocation is feasible
The agent-by-agent inequalities all still hold (Section 4 even computes them). What fails is aggregating: when \(\sum_i p\cdot\bar x^i=\infty\), adding a strict inequality to infinitely many weak ones does not give a strict inequality between the totals.
2. In Case 2 (\(\alpha=\beta\)), the value of the aggregate endowment is infinite. What follows?
  • The CE is Pareto dominated by the best symmetric one
  • No competitive equilibrium exists for these parameters
  • The 1WT is silent, yet the CE happens to be optimal
  • The 1WT applies because the interest rate is zero
Finite value is sufficient, not necessary. With \(\alpha=\beta\) the CE is the best symmetric allocation, and it is Pareto optimal (Exercise 12.7), even though the 1WT cannot be used to show it.
3. Which economy can have a CE with infinite aggregate value?
  • Infinitely many agents and infinitely many dated goods
  • Infinitely many agents but only finitely many goods
  • Finitely many agents with infinitely many dated goods
  • Finitely many agents and finitely many distinct goods
This is the double infinity. With finitely many goods, the value is \(\sum_\ell p_\ell\bar e_\ell\), a finite sum. With finitely many agents and monotone preferences, each \(p\cdot\bar x^i\le p\cdot e^i\lt \infty\), and a finite sum of finite numbers is finite.
4. In Case 1, at CE prices, how does generation \(t\)'s best-symmetric bundle compare in value with its endowment?
  • It costs exactly the same, since both are feasible
  • It costs strictly less, which is why they prefer it
  • The comparison is undefined since prices are infinite
  • It costs strictly more, as the 1WT proof's step 2 says
The difference is \(p_t(\beta-\alpha)(q-1)>0\) with \(q=1/(1+\bar r)>1\). Each price \(p_t\) is finite; only the infinite sum over agents is a problem. The bundle is strictly preferred, so by utility maximization it must be unaffordable, i.e. strictly more expensive.

Exercise 12.1 True or false? Justify. Past exams Exam-inspired

Answer each in one to four lines. Say which theorem you are using and which hypothesis does or does not hold.

  1. "Consider an equilibrium in the OLG model. If the value of aggregate consumption at market prices is finite, then the equilibrium allocation is Pareto optimal." Fall 2005 Midterm, Ex. 1 Q4
    Show answer

    True. This is the 1WT for infinite economies. LNS gives \(p\cdot x^i\ge p\cdot\bar x^i\) for every generation, with strict inequality for one. Because the value is finite, the agent-by-agent inequalities can be summed and regrouped by date, which contradicts feasibility. Exercise 12.2 asks for the full proof.

  2. "In an OLG model where agents live \(k\ge2\) periods, there is only one commodity per period, and the aggregate endowment is finite, if interest rates are strictly negative in all periods, the competitive equilibrium is NOT efficient." Fall 2025 Midterm 1, I.3
    Show answer

    True. With \(r_t<0\), prices \(p_t\) are increasing. A feasible chain of transfers from the young to the old (Section 4 for \(k=2\); Lesson 16 for \(k\) periods) takes goods away at low prices and gives them back at high prices. Every generation, including those alive at date 1, gains to first order. Note the wording: "NOT efficient" is a claim about the CE, not just about the 1WT failing to apply.

  3. "In the OLG endowment economy, the competitive equilibrium is Pareto optimal if and only if the value of the aggregate endowment is finite." Exam-inspired
    Show answer

    False. The "if" direction is item 1. The "only if" direction fails: Case 2 (\(\alpha=\beta\), \(\bar r=0\)) has \(\sum_tp_t=\infty\), yet the CE is Pareto optimal (Exercise 12.7). Finite value is sufficient, not necessary.

  4. "The First Welfare Theorem only needs local non-satiation. It holds whether the numbers of agents and goods are finite or infinite." Trap: a claim in a 2025 student study guide
    Show answer

    False. The proof also needs \(p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i\lt \infty\). This holds automatically if either the goods or the agents are finite (Exercises 12.3 and 12.4). With infinitely many of both (OLG) it can fail, and so can the theorem: Case 1 has LNS and an inefficient CE.

  5. "If the First Welfare Theorem fails in an economy, some agent must violate local non-satiation." Exam-inspired
    Show answer

    False. In Case 1, log preferences satisfy LNS. The theorem fails because the value of aggregate consumption is infinite (the double infinity), not because of satiation. Externalities and missing markets are other ways it can fail (Lesson 5).

  6. "In the OLG endowment economy with \(\bar r<0\), some generation has infinite wealth at equilibrium prices." Exam-inspired
    Show answer

    False. Generation \(t\)'s wealth \(p_te^t_t+p_{t+1}e^t_{t+1}\) is finite. Only the sum over infinitely many generations diverges. (If one agent had infinite wealth, it would have no utility-maximizing bundle and there would be no CE.)

  7. "The inefficiency of the endowment OLG equilibrium when \(\bar r<0\) is due to over-accumulation of capital." Trap: wording in a 2025 study guide
    Show answer

    False. The endowment economy has no capital, so nothing can be over-accumulated. The cause is that the young want to save while the only possible counterparties, the old, will not be around to repay. Intergenerational markets are missing. Formally, the value of the aggregate endowment is infinite. Over-accumulation is the right language only in the Diamond model (Lesson 15).

  8. "In the neoclassical growth model with one infinitely lived representative agent, if \(k_0\) is high enough that \(r(0)<0\), the competitive equilibrium is not Pareto optimal." Fall 2025 Final, Q4
    Show answer

    False. A negative \(r(0)\) is possible: \(r(0)=f'(k_0)-\delta<0\) once \(f'(k_0)<\delta\). It is harmless, because with one (finitely many) agent the value of aggregate consumption is finite and the 1WT applies (Exercise 12.5). Negative rates signal OLG inefficiency only because OLG has infinitely many agents.

  9. "A positive interest rate guarantees that the OLG equilibrium is Pareto optimal." Exam-inspired
    Show answer

    False in general. In the stationary economy with no growth, a constant \(\bar r>0\) gives a finite geometric sum, so the CE is PO. With growth, the condition is \(1+\bar r>(1+g)(1+n)\), so a positive but low rate can come with an inefficient CE (Lesson 13). On an exam, state your assumptions.

Exercise 12.2 Past exam: Fall 2005 Midterm, Ex. 1 Q4 Proof

The exam asked only for true/false on the statement in 12.1 (item 1). Here you prove it. Two-period OLG endowment economy with one good per date. Generation \(t\ge1\) has strictly increasing utility \(v^t(x^t_t,x^t_{t+1})\), and the initial old have \(u^0=x^0_1\). Let \((p,\bar x)\) be a CE with \(\sum_{t\ge1}p_t\bar e_t\lt \infty\), where \(\bar e_t=e^{t-1}_t+e^t_t\).

  1. (5 pts) Suppose a feasible \(\hat x\) Pareto-dominates \(\bar x\). Show that \(p_t\hat x^t_t+p_{t+1}\hat x^t_{t+1}\ge p_t\bar x^t_t+p_{t+1}\bar x^t_{t+1}\) for every \(t\), and \(p_1\hat x^0_1\ge p_1\bar x^0_1\), with at least one strict inequality. Which assumption do you use? One line.
  2. (10 pts) Show that \(\sum_{t\ge0}p\cdot\bar x^t\) and \(\sum_{t\ge0}p\cdot\hat x^t\) are finite and can be rewritten as sums over dates, \(\sum_{t\ge1}p_t(x^{t-1}_t+x^t_t)\). Hint: all terms are non-negative, and feasibility bounds each date's total.
  3. (5 pts) Derive the contradiction.
  4. (5 pts) Point to the exact step that is illegitimate when \(\sum_tp_t\bar e_t=\infty\).
Show solution
  1. (a) Strict monotonicity implies LNS. As in Lesson 4, a weakly preferred bundle cannot be cheaper (else a nearby, still affordable bundle would be strictly better), and a strictly preferred bundle must cost strictly more (else it was affordable). This applies to each generation and to the initial old.
  2. (b) Prices are positive (strict monotonicity) and consumptions are non-negative, so every term \(p_tx^s_t\ge0\). For non-negative double series the order of summation doesn't matter (Tonelli), so \(\sum_{s}p\cdot x^s=\sum_{t\ge1}p_t(x^{t-1}_t+x^t_t)\). Feasibility gives \(x^{t-1}_t+x^t_t\le\bar e_t\) for both allocations, so both sums are at most \(\sum_tp_t\bar e_t\lt \infty\). For \(\bar x\), feasibility holds with equality (LNS, so nothing is wasted in a CE), and its sum equals \(\sum_tp_t\bar e_t\).
  3. (c) Sum the inequalities from (a). All sums are finite, so a strict inequality in one term survives: \(\sum_sp\cdot\hat x^s>\sum_sp\cdot\bar x^s=\sum_tp_t\bar e_t\). But by (b), \(\sum_sp\cdot\hat x^s=\sum_tp_t(\hat x^{t-1}_t+\hat x^t_t)\le\sum_tp_t\bar e_t\). Contradiction. ∎
  4. (d) Step (c). With infinite value, both sides of the summed inequality are \(+\infty\), and "\(\infty>\infty\)" is not a contradiction. Equivalently, you cannot subtract \(\sum_sp\cdot\bar x^s\) from both sides. The agent-by-agent inequalities remain true; there is just no finite total to compare them with.

Exam tip: when a T/F mentions OLG and "finite value", the answer is almost always this argument in two lines: LNS gives the inequalities, finite value makes them summable, and the sum contradicts feasibility.

Exercise 12.3 Slides N2 p.26 Proof

Pure exchange, finitely many goods \(L=\mathbb{R}^m\), infinitely many agents, consumption sets \(X^i=\mathbb{R}^m_+\), and aggregate endowment bounded in each component: \(\bar e_\ell\equiv\sum_{i\in I}e^i_\ell\lt \infty\) for \(\ell=1,\dots,m\). Show that in any CE \((p,\{\bar x^i\})\), \(p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i\lt \infty\). Then explain why the 1WT goes through.

Hint

Follow the slide hint: \(p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i = p_1\sum_i\bar x^i_1+\dots+p_m\sum_i\bar x^i_m\). What does feasibility say about each \(\sum_i\bar x^i_\ell\)? Why can you swap the two sums?

Show solution
  1. Prices are non-negative. If \(p_\ell<0\) for some \(\ell\), any agent with monotone preferences would demand unboundedly much of good \(\ell\), since buying it relaxes the budget. That contradicts feasibility with \(\bar e_\ell\lt \infty\). So \(p\ge0\). (Under the strict monotonicity used in class, \(p\gg0\).)
  2. Swap the sums. All terms \(p_\ell\bar x^i_\ell\) are non-negative, so by Tonelli's theorem for series, \(\sum_i\sum_\ell p_\ell\bar x^i_\ell=\sum_\ell p_\ell\sum_i\bar x^i_\ell\), whether finite or not.
  3. Use feasibility good by good. \(\sum_i\bar x^i_\ell=\bar e_\ell\lt \infty\). Hence \(p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i=\sum_{\ell=1}^m p_\ell\bar e_\ell\), a finite sum of finite numbers, so it is finite.
  4. 1WT. Any dominating feasible \(\{x^i\}\) also has \(\sum_i p\cdot x^i\le\sum_\ell p_\ell\bar e_\ell\lt \infty\). Summing \(p\cdot x^i\ge p\cdot\bar x^i\) (strict for \(i'\)) over agents is now legitimate and gives \(\sum_i p\cdot x^i>\sum_i p\cdot\bar x^i=\sum_\ell p_\ell\bar e_\ell\). Contradiction.

Why this matters: an infinite number of agents alone is harmless, because there are only \(m\) prices and \(m\) finite aggregate quantities to multiply.

Exercise 12.4 Slides N2 p.27 Proof

Finitely many agents (\(I\) finite), infinitely many goods \(L=\mathbb{R}^\infty\), all \(u^i\) strictly increasing. Show that in any CE, \(p\cdot\sum_{i\in I}\bar x^i\lt \infty\).

Hint

The slide asks two questions. Could \(p\cdot\bar x^i\) be infinite for some \(i\)? And could \(p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i\) be infinite if every \(p\cdot\bar x^i\) is finite?

Show solution
  1. Each agent's wealth is finite. Suppose \(p\cdot e^i=\infty\). Then the budget constraint \(p\cdot x\le p\cdot e^i\) constrains nothing. In particular, \(\bar x^i\) plus one more unit of any good is affordable and, by strict monotonicity, strictly better. So no utility maximum exists, contradicting the CE. Hence \(p\cdot e^i\lt \infty\).
  2. Each agent's consumption has finite value. \(p\cdot\bar x^i\le p\cdot e^i\lt \infty\) by the budget constraint.
  3. Finite sum. All terms are non-negative (\(p\ge0\) by monotonicity), so \(p\cdot\sum_{i\in I}\bar x^i=\sum_{i\in I}p\cdot\bar x^i\). This is a sum of \(|I|\lt \infty\) finite numbers, hence finite.

Contrast with OLG: there too each \(p\cdot\bar x^i\) is finite (two-period lives), but there are infinitely many of them. When \(\bar r\le0\) their sum diverges.

Exercise 12.5 Past exam: Fall 2025 Final, Q4 (20 pts)

Continuous-time neoclassical growth model with fixed labour supply and one infinitely lived representative agent. Utility and production are strictly increasing, strictly concave and satisfy Inada conditions. Capital depreciates at rate \(\delta>0\), and the rental rate is \(v=r+\delta\).

  1. (10 pts) Can the initial capital stock be so high that the real interest rate at \(t=0\), \(r(0)\), is negative? Yes or no, with a one-line reason.
  2. (10 pts) Can there be an initial condition with capital so high that the equilibrium is NOT Pareto optimal? Yes or no. (Then, for practice: justify in three lines using the results of this lesson, and contrast with Case 1 of the OLG economy.)
Show solution

(a) Yes. \(r(0)=f'(k_0)-\delta\), and \(f'(k)\to0\) as \(k\to\infty\) (Inada). So for \(k_0\) large enough, \(f'(k_0)\lt \delta\) and \(r(0)<0\).
(b) No. There is one agent, so by Exercise 12.4 (finitely many agents, strictly increasing utility) the value of aggregate consumption is finite, and the 1WT applies whatever \(k_0\) is. More directly, with a single agent the CE allocation solves the planner's problem. A high \(k_0\) just makes the agent rich: she consumes a lot early and runs capital down, and \(r(t)\) rises toward its steady-state value \(\rho>0\). In OLG, a negative \(r\) is a symptom of inefficiency only because the double infinity can make the value infinite. In the representative-agent model, \(r<0\) for a while is harmless.

Grading note from the 2025 review: the points are for the reasoning ("which 1WT hypothesis is relevant?"), not for algebra.

Exercise 12.6 Past exam: 2017/18 Midterm 1, "OLG and trees"

Time \(t=1,2,\dots\), and a unit mass is born each period and lives two periods. Generation \(t\) is endowed with \(e^t_t>0\) when young and nothing when old. Utility \(u^t(c^t_t,c^t_{t+1})\) is strictly increasing and concave. There is one tree (a stock) that pays dividends \(d_t\ge0\), owned at \(t=1\) by the initial old. The tree's price, ex-dividend, is \(q_t\). Agents can also borrow and lend at rate \(r_t\). The young choose saving in bonds \(s_t\) and trees \(a_t\): $$c^t_t+a_tq_t+s_t=e^t_t,\qquad c^t_{t+1}=a_t(q_{t+1}+d_{t+1})+s_t(1+r_t).$$

  1. (5 pts) What are the equilibrium values of \(a_t\) and \(s_t\)? Why? Two short reasons.
  2. (5 pts) What relationship between \(1+r_t\) and \((q_{t+1}+d_{t+1})/q_t\) is needed for an interior solution to the young's problem? One line.
  3. (10 pts) Let \(\sigma_t(R)\equiv\arg\max_xu^t(e^t_t-x,xR)\). Write the difference equation the equilibrium \(\{q_t\}\) must solve. Then, with constant \(e^t_t=\bar e\), \(u^t=\bar u\) and \(d_t=\bar d\), write the equation for a constant price \(\bar q\). Hint: one side is \(\bar q\); the other is a function of \(\bar d/\bar q\).
  4. (10 pts) Assume Inada conditions and \(\bar d>0\). Is the implied \(\bar r\) strictly positive? Is the allocation Pareto optimal? Justify by citing the right theorem and checking its hypothesis.
  5. (5 pts) With \(\bar u=(1-\beta)\log c_y+\beta\log c_o\), solve for \(\bar q\) and \(\bar r\).
  6. (10 pts) (Extension, not on the exam.) Let \(\bar d=0\), so the tree is a "bubble" with no fundamental value. Find the two constant-price equilibria, and say which one is Pareto optimal under log utility.
Show solution
  1. (a) \(a_t=1\). There is one tree, and the old (who don't value anything after \(t\)) sell it, so the young must hold it all. \(s_t=0\). Members of a generation are identical, so net lending within the cohort is zero, and the old cannot borrow (they won't be alive to repay). Formally, by induction: the initial old eat \(d_1+q_1\); market clearing gives \(c^1_1=e^1_1-q_1\), so \(s_1=0\); and so on.
  2. (b) No arbitrage: \(1+r_t=(q_{t+1}+d_{t+1})/q_t\). If the tree paid more, the young would borrow without limit to buy trees. If it paid less, nobody would hold it. Either way the tree market could not clear at \(a_t=1\).
  3. (c) The young's total saving is \(a_tq_t+s_t=q_t\), and optimal saving is \(\sigma_t(1+r_t)\). So \(\sigma_t\big((q_{t+1}+d_{t+1})/q_t\big)=q_t\). In a steady state, \(\bar q=\bar\sigma(1+\bar d/\bar q)\) and \(\bar r=\bar d/\bar q\).
  4. (d) Inada conditions imply \(\bar\sigma>0\), so \(\bar q>0\), and \(\bar r=\bar d/\bar q>0\). The equilibrium is PO: prices are \(p_t=(1+\bar r)^{-(t-1)}\), and the value of aggregate consumption is \(\sum_tp_t(\bar e+\bar d)=(\bar e+\bar d)(1+\bar r)/\bar r\lt \infty\). The OLG 1WT (Exercise 12.2) applies. Common mistake (in a circulating set of answers): writing "\(\bar r>0\), so by the 1WT it is PO" without checking finite value. The standard 1WT does not apply to OLG without that hypothesis.
  5. (e) Log utility saves a fixed share: \(\bar\sigma=\beta\bar e\), independent of \(R\). Hence \(\bar q=\beta\bar e\) and \(\bar r=\bar d/(\beta\bar e)\). The rate rises with \(\bar d\), falls with \(\beta\) (more saving) and falls with \(\bar e\).
  6. (f) With \(\bar d=0\), no arbitrage reads \(1+r=q_{t+1}/q_t\). One steady state is \(\bar q=0\): the tree is worthless, there is autarky, and generation \(t\) consumes \((\bar e,0)\). The other is \(\bar q=\bar\sigma(1)>0\) with \(\bar r=0\). Under log utility this is \(\bar q=\beta\bar e\), and each generation consumes \(((1-\beta)\bar e,\beta\bar e)\). That is the best symmetric allocation, so the bubble equilibrium is PO even though its value is infinite (compare Exercise 12.7). Autarky is Pareto-dominated by it, and the initial old gain \(\beta\bar e\). The valued bubble does exactly what PAYG does in Lesson 11 and rolled-over debt does in Lesson 14 (Exercise 14.7).

Intuition for (d): an asset paying a positive dividend forever cannot be priced at \(r\le g\), since its price would be infinite. So a positive-dividend asset rules out dynamic inefficiency.

Exercise 12.7 Proof (beyond the slides: Case 2 is efficient)

Let \(\alpha=\beta\), so the CE is \(\bar c=(1-\beta,\beta)\) for every generation, with \(\bar r=0\). Show that no feasible allocation Pareto-dominates it.

Hint

Let \(d_t\) be the extra amount the old get at date \(t\) relative to \(\bar c\). Feasibility then forces the young at \(t\) to get \(d_t\) less. Use strict concavity of \(v\) and the fact that its indifference curve at \(\bar c\) is tangent to the line \(c_y+c_o=1\).

Show solution
  1. WLOG feasibility holds with equality (throwing goods away cannot help). Describe any allocation by \(d_t\equiv x^{t-1}_t-\beta\), so \(x^t_t=1-\beta-d_t\). The initial old are weakly better off iff \(d_1\ge0\).
  2. Generation \(t\) gets \((1-\beta-d_t,\ \beta+d_{t+1})\). At \(\bar c\) the MRS is 1, so the indifference curve is tangent to a line of slope \(-1\). By strict quasi-concavity it lies strictly above that line away from \(\bar c\). Hence weakly preferring requires \(d_{t+1}\ge d_t\), strictly if \(d_t\ne0\).
  3. For a strict improvement, some \(d_k>0\): either the initial old gain (\(d_1>0\)), or some generation strictly gains, which by step 2 forces some later \(d>0\). From then on \(d\) is strictly increasing. Strict concavity gives more. Log has curvature bounded away from zero on compact sets, so \(d_{t+1}-d_t\ge\kappa d_t^2\) for some \(\kappa>0\). An increasing sequence satisfying this exceeds any bound in finitely many steps.
  4. But \(d_t\le 1-\beta\) (the young cannot consume negative amounts). Contradiction. ∎

Compare with Case 1: there the MRS at the CE exceeds 1. Generation \(t\) accepts \(d_{t+1}=d_t=\varepsilon\), a constant transfer, so no explosion is needed.

Exercise 12.8 Exam-inspired (the transfer chain at CE prices, and truncation)

Take the log economy of Lesson 10 with \(\beta>\alpha\) (Case 1). Normalize \(p_1=1\), and let \(q\equiv p_{t+1}/p_t=1/(1+\bar r)\).

  1. (5 pts) Show that \(q=\frac{\beta(1-\alpha)}{(1-\beta)\alpha}>1\).
  2. (10 pts) Let \(x^*\) be the best symmetric allocation. Show that \(p\cdot x^{*t}-p\cdot\bar x^t=p_t(\beta-\alpha)(q-1)>0\) for every generation \(t\ge1\), and that this gap grows without bound. Why does this not contradict feasibility of \(x^*\)? Three lines.
  3. (10 pts) Now let the world end at date \(T\). Generation \(T\) is born, consumes \(c^T_T\) and has no old age (utility \(\log c^T_T\)). Show that the CE is still autarky, that the transfer chain is no longer a Pareto improvement, and that the autarky CE is now Pareto optimal whatever the sign of \(\bar r\).
Show solution

(a) \(q=p_{t+1}/p_t=v_2/v_1\) at the endowment \(=\frac{\beta/\alpha}{(1-\beta)/(1-\alpha)}\). It exceeds 1 iff \(\beta(1-\alpha)>\alpha(1-\beta)\iff\beta>\alpha\).
(b) \(p\cdot x^{*t}-p\cdot\bar x^t=p_t[(1-\beta)-(1-\alpha)]+p_{t+1}[\beta-\alpha]=(\beta-\alpha)(p_{t+1}-p_t)=p_t(\beta-\alpha)(q-1)\). This is positive, and since \(p_t=q^{t-1}\to\infty\), the gap explodes. No contradiction: both allocations have total value \(\sum_tp_t=\infty\), so summing the agent-level strict inequalities gives "\(\infty>\infty\)". Economically, generation \(t\)'s extra cost is paid by generation \(t+1\)'s transfer, forever.
(c) The forward induction of Lesson 8 works as before, and at \(T\) the last generation eats what is left, \(e^T_T\). Moving to \((1-\beta,\beta)\) now requires generation \(T\) to give \(\beta-\alpha>0\) to generation \(T-1\) and get nothing back, so it loses. With \(T+1\) agents and \(T\) goods, the value of the aggregate endowment is finite, and the 1WT says autarky is PO. It is the absence of a last generation that makes the improvement possible.

Key takeaways

  • The 1WT proof needs \(p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i\lt \infty\) to add up the agent-level inequalities. With finitely many agents or finitely many goods this is automatic.
  • In the OLG log example, the value is \(\sum_t(1+\bar r)^{-(t-1)}\), which is finite iff \(\bar r>0\).
  • Case 1 (\(\bar r<0\)) is a genuine failure: every agent's improved bundle costs more at CE prices, financed by the next generation, forever.
  • Finite value is sufficient, not necessary: Case 2 (\(\bar r=0\)) is efficient anyway.
  • The culprit is the double infinity (Shell 1971): no last generation means the transfer chain never has to close.
