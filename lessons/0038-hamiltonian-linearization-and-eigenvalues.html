The Hamiltonian Matrix and Eigenvalue Pairing

Covers Hamiltonian slides 25–52 · About 55 min · Prerequisites: Lesson 37, Lesson 33 (discrete-time stability), linear algebra (eigenvalues, diagonalization)
By the end you can:
  1. write the linearization of the Hamiltonian system around the steady state as \(\dot{\hat z}=\mathcal{H}\hat z\), where \(\hat z=(\hat\lambda,\hat x)'\) and \(\mathcal H\) is the \(2m\times2m\) Hamiltonian matrix;
  2. compute the four blocks of \(\mathcal H\) from the second-order partials of \(H^*\) evaluated at the steady state;
  3. prove the eigenvalue-pairing theorem: if \(\mu\) is an eigenvalue of \(\mathcal H\) then \(\rho-\mu\) is also an eigenvalue, with matching algebraic multiplicity;
  4. use the saddle-path proposition to find the optimal policy rule \(\hat\lambda=S\hat x\) and characterize \(S\) as a solution to the matrix Riccati equation;
  5. derive the continuous-time analogue of the almost-reciprocal pairs from Lesson 33, and explain the role of \(\rho\) versus \(\ln\beta\) in the two frameworks.
Primary source Alvarez, Hamiltonian slides 25–52. The Hamiltonian-matrix pairing result is treated in Acemoglu (2009), §7.6–7.7; in Anderson et al. (1996), §2–3; and in Stengel (1994), Ch. 4. The Riccati equation derivation follows the slides closely.

1. Linearizing the Hamiltonian system

After substituting the optimal control \(u^*(x,\lambda)\) from \(H_u=0\), the first-order conditions reduce to a system of \(2m\) ODEs in the state-costate vector \(z=(x',\lambda')'\in\mathbb R^{2m}\) Ham p.25:

$$\dot x=H^*_\lambda(x,\lambda),\qquad\dot\lambda=\rho\lambda-H^*_x(x,\lambda).$$

Let \((\bar x,\bar\lambda)\) be the steady state. Define deviations \(\hat x=x-\bar x\) and \(\hat\lambda=\lambda-\bar\lambda\). Linearizing around \((\bar x,\bar\lambda)\):

Definition: the Hamiltonian matrix
$$\dot{\hat z}=\mathcal{H}\hat z,\quad\text{where }\hat z=\begin{pmatrix}\hat\lambda\\\hat x\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathcal{H}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho I_m-\bar H^*_{x\lambda} & -\bar H^*_{xx}\\ \bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda} & \bar H^*_{\lambda x}\end{pmatrix}.$$

Equivalently, writing the second partials of \(H^*\) evaluated at \((\bar x,\bar\lambda)\):

$$\mathcal{H}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{11} & A_{12}\\A_{21} & A_{22}\end{pmatrix}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\rho I_m-\bar H^*_{x\lambda} & -\bar H^*_{xx}\\ \bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda} & \bar H^*_{\lambda x}\end{pmatrix}.$$

The four blocks in terms of the primitives of the problem:

  • \(A_{11}=\rho I_m-\bar H^*_{x\lambda}\): combines the discount rate with the cross-partial of \(H^*\) in \((x,\lambda)\). At the modified golden rule, \(\bar H^*_{x\lambda}=\rho I_m\), so \(A_{11}=0\) for the neoclassical model.
  • \(A_{12}=-\bar H^*_{xx}\): negative Hessian of \(H^*\) in the state; positive semidefinite in well-behaved problems since \(H^*_{xx}\preceq0\).
  • \(A_{21}=\bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda}\gt0\): Hessian of \(H^*\) in \(\lambda\); positive because \(H^*\) is convex in \(\lambda\) (it is a pointwise max over linear functions of \(\lambda\)).
  • \(A_{22}=\bar H^*_{\lambda x}=(A_{11}+\rho I_m)'\): the cross-partial; equals \(\rho I_m\) at the neoclassical steady state.

Neoclassical growth, blocks computed. With \(H^*(k,\lambda)=\ln(c^*(\lambda))+\lambda(f(k)-\delta k-c^*(\lambda))\), where \(c^*(\lambda)=1/\lambda\) (from \(U'(c)=\lambda\) for log utility):

  • \(A_{11}=\rho-\bar H^*_{k\lambda}=\rho-(f'(\bar k)-\delta)=\rho-\rho=0\). The discount rate and the net return on capital exactly cancel at the modified golden rule.
  • \(A_{12}=-\bar H^*_{kk}=-\bar\lambda f''(\bar k)>0\). Positive because \(f''<0\) and \(\bar\lambda>0\).
  • \(A_{21}=\bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda}=1/\bar\lambda^2=\bar c^2>0\). Positive because \(H^*\) is convex in \(\lambda\).
  • \(A_{22}=\bar H^*_{\lambda k}=f'(\bar k)-\delta=\rho\). Equals \(\rho\) by the modified golden rule.

The neoclassical Hamiltonian matrix: \(\mathcal H=\begin{pmatrix}0 & -\bar\lambda f''(\bar k)\\ \bar c^2 & \rho\end{pmatrix}\). Note \(\operatorname{tr}\mathcal H=0+\rho=\rho\) and \(\det\mathcal H=\bar\lambda f''(\bar k)\cdot\bar c^2\lt0\) (saddle), consistent with the eigenvalue-pairing theorem below.

2. Eigenvalue pairing theorem

The critical structural result for continuous-time optimal control is Ham p.30:

Theorem: continuous-time eigenvalue pairing

If \(\mu\) is an eigenvalue of \(\mathcal H\) with algebraic multiplicity \(k\), then \(\rho-\mu\) is also an eigenvalue with algebraic multiplicity \(k\).

Proof sketch (via block determinant)

Examine the characteristic polynomial \(\det(\mathcal H-\mu I_{2m})=0\). Write:

$$\det(\mathcal H-\mu I)=\det\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}-\mu I & A_{12}\\A_{21} & A_{22}-\mu I\end{pmatrix}.$$

Now evaluate \(\det(\mathcal H-(\rho-\mu)I)\). The key is to show that \(\det(\mathcal H-(\rho-\mu)I)=\pm\det(\mathcal H-\mu I)\), so the two have the same roots (up to a sign that is a nonzero constant). This follows from the symplectic structure of \(\mathcal H\): multiplying the matrix \(\mathcal H-(\rho-\mu)I\) by the symplectic matrix \(J=\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\-I&0\end{pmatrix}\) on the left and \(J'\) on the right, and using the symmetry of the second-order partials (\(H^*_{x\lambda}=H^*_{\lambda x}{}'\), \(H^*_{xx}\) symmetric, \(H^*_{\lambda\lambda}\) symmetric), one obtains \(J(\mathcal H-(\rho-\mu)I)J'=-(\mathcal H-\mu I)'\). Taking determinants: \(\det(\mathcal H-(\rho-\mu)I)=(-1)^{2m}\det(\mathcal H-\mu I)=\det(\mathcal H-\mu I)\). So the characteristic polynomials are equal, hence they share the same roots. \(\square\)

Consequence: saddle-point structure. If \(\mathcal H\) has \(m\) eigenvalues with \(\text{Re}(\mu)\lt\rho/2\) and \(m\) with \(\text{Re}(\mu)\gt\rho/2\) (no eigenvalue exactly at \(\rho/2\)), then exactly \(m\) eigenvalues have \(\text{Re}(\mu)\lt0\) after discounting, giving a saddle: \(m\) stable directions and \(m\) unstable. Since the state \(x\) has dimension \(m\), the saddle path is exactly determined — there is a unique stable trajectory from each initial condition \(x_0\).

Pairing in discrete vs. continuous time

In discrete time (Lesson 33), eigenvalues come in "almost-reciprocal pairs" \((\lambda_i, 1/(\beta\lambda_i))\) with product \(1/\beta\). In continuous time, eigenvalues come in pairs \((\mu_i, \rho-\mu_i)\) with sum \(\rho\). The analogy: discrete-time eigenvalues multiply to \(1/\beta>1\); continuous-time eigenvalues sum to \(\rho>0\). The same principle holds: the unstable directions are determined by the \(m\) eigenvalues with the wrong sign. The correspondence under \(\lambda=e^{\mu}\) sends a continuous-time eigenvalue \(\mu\) to the discrete-time eigenvalue \(e^\mu\), and \(\rho-\mu\to e^{\rho-\mu}=e^\rho/e^\mu=1/(\beta e^\mu)\), recovering the discrete-time pairing.

Figure 38.1 — Characteristic polynomial \(p(\mu)=\mu^2-\rho\mu+\det\mathcal H\) of the Hamiltonian matrix. The two roots \(\mu_\pm\) always sum to \(\rho\) (eigenvalue pairing) and are real when \(\det\mathcal H\lt0\) (saddle case). Increasing \(\rho\) shifts both roots right while keeping their sum fixed; more negative \(\det\mathcal H\) spreads the roots further apart.

3. The saddle path and the decision rule

Under the eigenvalue-pairing condition, exactly \(m\) eigenvalues of \(\mathcal H\) have negative real part. Denote them \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_m\) with associated eigenvectors \(v_i=\begin{pmatrix}p_i\\q_i\end{pmatrix}\), where \(p_i,q_i\in\mathbb C^m\) Ham p.38.

The general solution starting near the steady state is:

$$\hat z(t)=\sum_{i=1}^m a_i e^{\mu_i t}v_i+\sum_{i=m+1}^{2m}a_i e^{\mu_i t}v_i.$$

For stability (and TC satisfaction), set \(a_i=0\) for \(i=m+1,\ldots,2m\) (the unstable eigenvalues). The stable saddle path is spanned by the \(m\) stable eigenvectors \(\{v_1,\ldots,v_m\}\). Along the saddle path, the state-costate relationship is linear:

Proposition: linear decision rule on the saddle path

Suppose \(\mathcal H\) has \(m\) eigenvalues with negative real part, with associated stable eigenvectors forming the matrix \(P=\begin{pmatrix}P_{11}\\P_{12}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{2m\times m}\). The optimal costate satisfies:

$$\hat\lambda=S\hat x,\quad\text{where }S=P_{12}P_{11}^{-1}\in\mathbb R^{m\times m}.$$

Substituting into \(\dot{\hat x}=A_{11}\hat x+A_{12}\hat\lambda=(A_{11}+A_{12}S)\hat x\), the state evolves as \(\dot{\hat x}=\Omega\hat x\) where \(\Omega=A_{11}+A_{12}S\) has eigenvalues \(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_m\) (the stable ones).

Once \(S\) is known, the optimal control follows from \(H_u=0\) and \(\lambda=\bar\lambda+S\hat x\): \(\hat u=(H_{uu})^{-1}(H_{ux}+H_{u\lambda}S)^{-1}... \) (details vary by problem). The practical steps are (1) compute \(\mathcal H\), (2) find the \(m\) stable eigenvectors, (3) form \(S=P_{12}P_{11}^{-1}\), (4) derive the optimal control from \(H_u=0\).

4. The Riccati equation

The matrix \(S\) satisfies a nonlinear matrix equation called the (continuous-time algebraic) Riccati equation. This can be derived by differentiating \(\hat\lambda=S\hat x\) along the optimal path and matching with the costate equation Ham p.44.

The continuous-time Riccati equation
$$S A_{12} S + S A_{11} + A_{22}S + A_{21} = 0,$$

or in terms of \(H^*\) partials:

$$S \bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda} S + S \bar H^*_{\lambda x} + \bar H^*_{x\lambda}S + (\rho I - \bar H^*_{xx}) = 0.$$

(\(S\) is \(m\times m\), symmetric if \(H^*\) has symmetric second partials.)

Derivation

Differentiate \(\hat\lambda=S\hat x\) along the optimal path: \(\dot{\hat\lambda}=S\dot{\hat x}\). The linearized system (with \(\hat z=(\hat\lambda,\hat x)'\)) gives:

$$\dot{\hat\lambda}=A_{11}\hat\lambda+A_{12}\hat x=(A_{11}S+A_{12})\hat x,$$ $$S\dot{\hat x}=S(A_{21}\hat\lambda+A_{22}\hat x)=S(A_{21}S+A_{22})\hat x.$$

Setting equal for all \(\hat x\): \(A_{11}S+A_{12}=SA_{21}S+SA_{22}\). Rearranging:

$$SA_{21}S+SA_{22}-A_{11}S-A_{12}=0.$$

Substituting \(A_{11}=\rho I-\bar H^*_{x\lambda}\), \(A_{12}=-\bar H^*_{xx}\), \(A_{21}=\bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda}\), \(A_{22}=\bar H^*_{\lambda x}\):

$$S\bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda}S+S\bar H^*_{\lambda x}-(\rho I-\bar H^*_{x\lambda})S-(-\bar H^*_{xx})=0.$$ $$\boxed{S\bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda}S+S\bar H^*_{\lambda x}+\bar H^*_{x\lambda}S+\bar H^*_{xx}-\rho S=0.}$$

This is the algebraic Riccati equation (ARE). \(\square\)

Scalar case. With \(m=1\), \(S\) is a scalar. Writing \(a=\bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda}\gt0\), \(b=\bar H^*_{\lambda x}=\bar H^*_{x\lambda}\), \(c=\bar H^*_{xx}\lt0\): the Riccati equation is \(aS^2+2bS+(c-\rho S)=0\), i.e. \(aS^2+(2b-\rho)S+c=0\). Two solutions; the stable one has the right sign for a saddle path. Ham p.50

5. Connecting to the saddle path

The saddle path in state-costate space is the linear manifold \(\{\hat\lambda=S\hat x\}\). In terms of the original control \(u\), the optimal decision rule is:

$$u^*(x) = \bar u + (du^*/dx)|_{\bar x,\bar\lambda}\hat x + (du^*/d\lambda)|_{\bar x,\bar\lambda}S\hat x + O(\hat x^2),$$

a linear function of the state deviation \(\hat x\) near the steady state Ham p.48.

Comparison with discrete time. In Lesson 35, the optimal decision rule was \(\hat y=g'(\bar x)\hat x\) where \(g'(\bar x)=-P_{11}^{-1}P_{12}\) used the stable eigenvectors of the discrete-time transition matrix \(A\). The same logic applies here: form the matrix of stable eigenvectors of \(\mathcal H\), extract the \(x\) and \(\lambda\) blocks, and form their ratio. The continuous-time Riccati equation plays the role of the discrete-time Riccati equation from Lesson 35.

6. Quiz

The Hamiltonian matrix \(\mathcal H\) is obtained by:

  • Taking the second partials of the Hamiltonian \(H\) with respect to \((x,u)\)
  • Linearizing the state-costate system \((\dot x,\dot\lambda)\) around the steady state
  • Computing the inverse of the Hessian of \(H^*\) at the steady state
  • Applying the eigenvalue-pairing theorem to the control problem
The Hamiltonian matrix arises from linearizing \(\dot x=H^*_\lambda\) and \(\dot\lambda=\rho\lambda-H^*_x\) around \((\bar x,\bar\lambda)\). The coefficients of this linearization are second-order partials of \(H^*$ evaluated at the steady state, which form the four blocks of \(\mathcal H\). It is not the Hessian of \(H\) or \(H^*\) directly, and does not require the pairing result to construct.

If \(\mu=2\) is an eigenvalue of \(\mathcal H\) when \(\rho=3\), what must also be an eigenvalue?

  • \(-2\) must be an eigenvalue of the system
  • \(1/2\) must be an eigenvalue of the system
  • \(1\) must be an eigenvalue of the system
  • \(-1\) must be an eigenvalue of the system
The pairing theorem says if \(\mu\) is an eigenvalue then \(\rho-\mu\) is also one. With \(\mu=2\) and \(\rho=3\): the paired eigenvalue is \(3-2=1\). Note \(\mu=2>0\) (unstable) and \(\rho-\mu=1>0\) (also unstable in this case). For saddle-path stability we need some eigenvalues with \(\text{Re}(\mu)\lt0\); this pair does not provide them, so there must be other eigenvalues in the system.

The matrix \(S\) in the optimal rule \(\hat\lambda=S\hat x\) satisfies:

  • The continuous-time algebraic Riccati equation
  • The continuous-time Bellman-Hamilton-Jacobi equation
  • The discrete-time Euler equation applied at the steady state
  • The first-order condition \(H^*_{x\lambda}=0\) at the steady state
Differentiating \(\hat\lambda=S\hat x\) along the optimal path and substituting the linearized equations gives a quadratic matrix equation in \(S\): \(S\bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda}S+S\bar H^*_{\lambda x}+\bar H^*_{x\lambda}S+\bar H^*_{xx}-\rho S=0\). This is the (continuous-time) algebraic Riccati equation (ARE). The solution with the correct stability properties gives the saddle-path matrix.

Why does the saddle path have exactly \(m\) dimensions in a problem with \(m\) states?

  • Because the Hamiltonian is \(m\)-times differentiable near steady state
  • Because the control variable has the same dimension as the state
  • Because the Riccati equation always has a unique symmetric solution
  • Because the pairing gives exactly \(m\) stable and \(m\) unstable eigenvalues
The \(2m\times2m\) Hamiltonian matrix has eigenvalues in paired groups summing to \(\rho\). Under the assumption that no eigenvalue lies at \(\rho/2\), there are exactly \(m\) with \(\text{Re}(\mu)\lt\rho/2\) (stable after discounting) and \(m\) with \(\text{Re}(\mu)\gt\rho/2\) (unstable). The saddle path is the \(m\)-dimensional stable manifold spanned by the \(m\) stable eigenvectors. Since the state \(x_0\) provides \(m\) initial conditions, the optimal path is uniquely determined.

7. True or False

True or False — justify in one or two lines
  1. If \(\mathcal H\) has an eigenvalue \(\mu=\rho/2\), the saddle-path proposition fails: there is no saddle path. Ham p.30
    Answer

    True. The pairing theorem pairs \(\mu\) with \(\rho-\mu=\rho/2\) — itself. So \(\mu=\rho/2\) is paired with itself, and there is no longer a clean split into \(m\) stable and \(m\) unstable eigenvalues. This "center manifold" case requires additional analysis. The slides and Acemoglu §7.6 note this as a degenerate case requiring separate treatment.

  2. The Riccati equation \(SA_{12}S+SA_{11}-A_{22}S-A_{21}=0\) is linear in \(S\). Ham p.44
    Answer

    False. It contains the term \(SA_{12}S\), which is quadratic in \(S\). That is why the equation is called the "Riccati equation" (a generalization of the scalar Riccati ODE). For a scalar \(S\) this is a quadratic equation in \(S\) with two solutions; we select the one consistent with the saddle path.

  3. In the scalar case (\(m=1\)), the saddle path is a line through the steady state in \((x,\lambda)\) space with slope \(S\). Ham p.50
    Answer

    True. The relationship \(\hat\lambda=S\hat x\) is linear, so near the steady state the saddle path is approximated by the line through \((\bar x,\bar\lambda)\) with slope \(S\) in the \((x,\lambda)\) plane. Exactly on the saddle path the trajectory stays on this line (linearization is exact only locally; the true saddle path may be curved farther from the steady state).

  4. The eigenvalue-pairing theorem requires the Hamiltonian \(H\) to be separable in \((x,u)\). Ham p.30
    Answer

    False. The pairing result follows from the symplectic structure of the Hamiltonian matrix, which requires only that \(H^*\) have symmetric second partials (i.e. \(H^*_{x\lambda}=H^*_{\lambda x}{}'\)) — a consequence of Clairaut's theorem. No separability in \((x,u)\) is required. The result holds for any smooth problem with an interior optimum where \(H_{uu}\) is invertible.

  5. The optimal decision rule \(\hat u = G\hat x\) (for some matrix \(G\)) is linear in the state deviation only near the steady state. Ham p.48
    Answer

    True. The linear rule \(\hat u=G\hat x\) is an approximation obtained by linearizing \(u^*(x)\) around \(\bar x\). It is exact (globally linear) only in the special case where \(H^*\) is globally quadratic, i.e. in the LQR problem of Lesson 39. For general nonlinear problems, the true decision rule is nonlinear far from the steady state.

8. Exercises

Exercise 38.1 Ham p.25–32 (Hamiltonian matrix for neoclassical growth)

Specialize to \(U(c)=\ln c\), \(f(k)=Ak^\alpha\), \(\delta>0\), \(\rho>0\), \(0\lt\alpha\lt1\).

  1. Compute \(\bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda}\), \(\bar H^*_{\lambda k}\), \(\bar H^*_{kk}\) at the steady state \((\bar k,\bar\lambda)\) from Exercise 37.1. (10 pts)
  2. Write out the \(2\times2\) Hamiltonian matrix \(\mathcal H\). Compute \(\operatorname{tr}\mathcal H\) and \(\det\mathcal H\). (10 pts)
  3. Verify the eigenvalue-pairing theorem: show that if \(\mu_1,\mu_2\) are the eigenvalues, then \(\mu_1+\mu_2=\rho\). (5 pts)
  4. Find the eigenvalues in terms of \(\rho\), \(\alpha\), \(A\), \(\delta\). Show that one is positive and one is negative. (10 pts)
Hint

For (a): \(H^*_{\lambda\lambda}=d^2H^*/d\lambda^2=1/\bar\lambda^2=\bar c^2\) (since \(\bar\lambda=1/\bar c\)); equivalently \(-1/U''(\bar c)=\bar c^2>0\) for \(U=\ln\). For \(H^*_{kk}=\bar\lambda f''(\bar k)\lt0\). For (b): with \(\hat z=(\hat\lambda,\hat k)'\), use \(A_{11}=\rho-\bar H^*_{k\lambda}\) and \(A_{22}=\bar H^*_{\lambda k}=\rho\). The \(\rho I\) in \(A_{11}\) cancels \(H^*_{k\lambda}=\rho\) to give \(A_{11}=0\); then \(\operatorname{tr}\mathcal H=A_{11}+A_{22}=0+\rho=\rho\).

Show solution

(a) From Ex 37.1: \(\bar\lambda=U'(\bar c)=1/\bar c\). Computing the second partials of \(H^*\) at \((\bar k,\bar\lambda)\):

  • \(\bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda}=\frac{d^2H^*}{d\lambda^2}=\frac{d}{d\lambda}(f(\bar k)-\delta\bar k-c^*(\lambda))=-\frac{dc^*}{d\lambda}=\frac{1}{\lambda^2}\Big|_{\bar\lambda}=\bar c^2\gt0\).
  • \(\bar H^*_{\lambda k}=\frac{d}{dk}H^*_\lambda\big|_{\bar k}=\frac{d}{dk}(f(k)-\delta k)\big|_{\bar k}=f'(\bar k)-\delta=\rho\) (modified golden rule).
  • \(\bar H^*_{kk}=\bar\lambda f''(\bar k)=\frac{1}{\bar c}\cdot A\alpha(\alpha-1)\bar k^{\alpha-2}\lt0\) (since \(f''<0\) and \(\bar\lambda>0\)).

(b) Using \(\hat z=(\hat\lambda,\hat k)'\) ordering (as in the slides):

$$\mathcal H=\begin{pmatrix}\rho-\bar H^*_{k\lambda} & -\bar H^*_{kk}\\ \bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda} & \bar H^*_{\lambda k}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho-\rho & -\bar\lambda f''(\bar k)\\ \bar c^2 & \rho\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0 & -\bar\lambda f''(\bar k)\\ \bar c^2 & \rho\end{pmatrix}.$$

\(\operatorname{tr}\mathcal H=0+\rho=\rho\). \(\det\mathcal H=0\cdot\rho-(-\bar\lambda f''(\bar k))\cdot\bar c^2=\bar\lambda f''(\bar k)\bar c^2\lt0\) (saddle).

(c) \(\mu_1+\mu_2=\operatorname{tr}\mathcal H=\rho\). By the eigenvalue-pairing theorem, if \(\mu\) is an eigenvalue then \(\rho-\mu\) is also one, so eigenvalues must sum to \(\rho\). \(\operatorname{tr}=\rho\) confirms this. \(\square\)

(d) \(\mu_{1,2}=\frac{\rho}{2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{\rho^2}{4}-\det\mathcal H}=\frac{\rho}{2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{\rho^2}{4}+|\bar\lambda f''(\bar k)|\bar c^2}\). Since \(\det\mathcal H\lt0\), the discriminant exceeds \(\rho^2/4\), so \(\mu_1=\rho/2+\sqrt{...}\gt\rho\gt0\) and \(\mu_2=\rho/2-\sqrt{...}\lt0\). One positive, one negative: genuine saddle structure. The optimal path lies along the stable eigenvector (negative eigenvalue direction).

Exercise 38.2 Ham p.44 (Riccati equation, scalar case)

In the scalar case (\(m=1\)), write the Riccati equation as \(aS^2+bS+c=0\) where \(a=\bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda}\), \(b=2\bar H^*_{\lambda x}-\rho\), \(c=\bar H^*_{xx}\).

  1. Use the quadratic formula to find the two solutions \(S_\pm\). (5 pts)
  2. Show that one solution \(S_-\) corresponds to the stable saddle path (negative \(\lambda\)-eigenvalue) and the other \(S_+\) to the unstable arm. (10 pts)
  3. Apply to the neoclassical growth model with \(\ln c\) utility and \(Ak^\alpha\) production, using the values from Exercise 38.1. Write out \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) explicitly. (10 pts)
Hint

For (b): once you have \(S\), the state equation becomes \(\dot{\hat k}=(A_{11}+A_{12}S)\hat k\). The sign of \(A_{11}+A_{12}S\) determines stability. The stable \(S\) is the one that makes \(A_{11}+A_{12}S\lt0\).

Show solution

(a) \(S_\pm=(-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac})/(2a)=(-(2\bar H^*_{\lambda x}-\rho)\pm\sqrt{(2\bar H^*_{\lambda x}-\rho)^2-4\bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda}\bar H^*_{xx}})/(2\bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda})\).

(b) The state evolves as \(\dot{\hat x}=(A_{11}+A_{12}S)\hat x\). Stability requires \(A_{11}+A_{12}S\lt0\), i.e. \(\bar H^*_{\lambda x}+\bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda}S\lt0\). Since \(A_{12}=\bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda}>0\), we need \(S\lt-A_{11}/A_{12}=-\bar H^*_{\lambda x}/\bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda}\). Of the two solutions, the smaller one \(S_-\) achieves this. \(S_+\) gives \(A_{11}+A_{12}S_+>0\) (unstable).

(c) For neoclassical growth with \(\ln c\) and \(Ak^\alpha\): \(a=\bar c^2\) (from \(\bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda}=\bar c^2\)), \(b=2\rho-\rho=\rho\) (using \(\bar H^*_{\lambda k}=\rho\)), \(c=\bar H^*_{kk}=\bar\lambda A\alpha(\alpha-1)\bar k^{\alpha-2}\lt0\). Riccati: \(\bar c^2\cdot S^2+\rho S+\bar\lambda A\alpha(\alpha-1)\bar k^{\alpha-2}=0\). \(S_-=(-\rho-\sqrt{\rho^2-4\bar c^2\cdot\bar\lambda A\alpha(\alpha-1)\bar k^{\alpha-2}})/(2\bar c^2)\), which is negative because the numerator is \(-\rho\) minus a positive square root (noting that \(\bar H^*_{kk}\lt0\) so \(4ac\lt0\) and the discriminant \(>0\)).

Key takeaways

  • Linearizing the Hamiltonian system \((\dot x,\dot\lambda)=(H^*_\lambda,\rho\lambda-H^*_x)\) around the steady state gives \(\dot{\hat z}=\mathcal H\hat z\) where \(\mathcal H\) is the \(2m\times2m\) Hamiltonian matrix with blocks of second-order partials of \(H^*\).
  • Eigenvalue pairing: if \(\mu\) is an eigenvalue of \(\mathcal H\), so is \(\rho-\mu\). Pairs sum to \(\rho\). In the undiscounted case (\(\rho=0\)), pairs are \(\pm\mu\). The pairing follows from the symplectic structure of \(\mathcal H\).
  • Saddle-path proposition: if no eigenvalue is at \(\rho/2\), there are exactly \(m\) stable directions. The optimal path lies on the \(m\)-dimensional saddle path, which locally satisfies \(\hat\lambda=S\hat x\).
  • The matrix \(S\) solves the algebraic Riccati equation (ARE): \(S\bar H^*_{\lambda\lambda}S+S\bar H^*_{\lambda x}+\bar H^*_{x\lambda}S+\bar H^*_{xx}-\rho S=0\). In the LQR case (Lesson 39), the ARE has a closed-form characterization and unique positive definite solution.
