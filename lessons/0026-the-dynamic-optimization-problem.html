Notation for Part VI (read this once)
  • \(x\) and \(y\) are states: \(x\) is today's state and \(y\) is tomorrow's. In Part I, \(x^i\) was a consumption bundle and \(y^j\) a production plan. Here they are dates of one state variable.
  • \(F\) is the period return function (utility as a function of today's and tomorrow's state), not a production function. Production is \(G(k,n)\). The slides write \(f(k)\equiv G(k,1)+(1-\delta)k\) for output plus undepreciated capital, i.e. total resources available at the end of the period. Careful: the Fall 2025 MT1 writes \(f\) for production \(G(k,1)\) itself and adds \((1-\delta)k\) separately.
  • \(\Gamma\) is the feasibility correspondence. It is not the planner multiplier \(\gamma_l\) of Lessons 22–23. Later in this deck the letter \(\gamma\) gets three other jobs (a policy root, CRRA curvature, a growth rate). Each lesson will flag which one is in use.
  • \(\beta\in(0,1)\) is the discount factor. In Class Note II (Lessons 8–11), \(\beta\) was the preference weight on old-age consumption.
  • \(\alpha\) is the Cobb–Douglas capital share, \(G(k,1)=Ak^\alpha\). In Note II it was the old-age endowment, and in Blanchard–Yaari the rate at which labor income declines.
  • The slides use \(U\) for both the utility function and the set of feasible controls. Here the control set is written \(\mathcal U\). Slide 9 also uses \(h\) for human capital, while slide 5 uses \(h\) for the return function in control–state notation. Context tells them apart.

1. Why a dynamic planner, and why now

Part V solved the planner's problem in a static economy: choose an allocation to maximize \(\sum_i\lambda_iu^i(x^i)\) subject to resource constraints, and read prices off the multipliers (Lesson 22, Lesson 23). Part VI does the same for an economy that lasts forever. The commodities are dated (consumption at \(t=0,1,2,\dots\)), as in the date-event economies of Lesson 1, and the planner chooses an entire path.

Why study a planner rather than markets? Take the neoclassical growth model: one infinitely lived representative household (a dynasty), competitive firms with a constant-returns technology, and complete markets for dated goods. There are infinitely many goods but finitely many agents. As Lesson 12 showed, that is enough for the value of the aggregate endowment to be finite, so the proof of the 1WT (Lesson 4) goes through. The competitive equilibrium allocation is Pareto optimal. With one household it is the solution of the planner's problem. Conversely, the 2WT (Lesson 6) makes the planner's solution a competitive equilibrium. Prices are recovered from the planner's marginal utilities, and interest rates from marginal products. So to find the equilibrium, solve one maximization problem. This is the problem whose optimality conditions (the Euler equation and the transversality condition) occupy the rest of this part.

Contrast with OLG

In OLG (Parts II–III) there are infinitely many goods and infinitely many agents, the value of the aggregate endowment can be infinite, and equilibria can be inefficient (Shell's double infinity, Lesson 12). With one dynasty, only one of the two infinities is present, so equilibria are efficient. The capital stocks of Lesson 15 (Diamond) and Lesson 19 (Blanchard–Yaari) can exceed the golden rule. You will see in Lesson 27 that the planner's steady state \(\beta f'(\bar k)=1\) never does.

2. The four primitives \([X,\Gamma,F,\beta]\)

Definition (a dynamic programming problem) EE p.3
  • \(X\) is the state space, the set of possible values of the state \(x\). Usually \(X\subseteq\mathbb R^m\).
  • \(\Gamma:X\rightrightarrows X\) is the feasibility correspondence: \(\Gamma(x)\) is the set of states that can be reached tomorrow from state \(x\) today. Its graph is $$\mathrm{Gr}(\Gamma)\equiv\{(x,y):x\in X,\ y\in\Gamma(x)\}.$$
  • \(F:\mathrm{Gr}(\Gamma)\to\mathbb R\) is the period return function: \(F(x,y)\) is the utility earned this period when today's state is \(x\) and tomorrow's is \(y\).
  • \(\beta\in(0,1)\) is the discount factor.

The sequence problem is $$V^*(x_0)=\max_{\{x_{t+1}\}_{t=0}^\infty}\ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\beta^tF(x_t,x_{t+1})\qquad\text{s.t.}\quad x_{t+1}\in\Gamma(x_t)\ \text{ for all }t\ge0,\quad x_0\text{ given.}$$

Some things to notice:

  • The choice at date \(t\) is tomorrow's state. Everything decided today (consumption, investment, hours) is either implied by the pair \((x_t,x_{t+1})\) or folded into \(F\). The unknown is a sequence of states \(x_1,x_2,\dots\). A sequence satisfying the constraints is a feasible plan. SLP call the set of feasible plans from \(x_0\) \(\Pi(x_0)\).
  • Why \(F\) lives on the graph. Returns are needed only at pairs that can actually occur. Often \(F\) cannot be defined anywhere else. In the growth model with log utility, \(F(k,k')=\log(f(k)-k')\) is not a real number when \(k'\ge f(k)\). Defining \(F\) on \(\mathrm{Gr}(\Gamma)\) keeps the primitives honest: feasibility is in \(\Gamma\), preferences are in \(F\).
  • Stationarity. \(\Gamma\), \(F\) and \(\beta\) do not depend on calendar time. The problem that starts at date \(t\) from state \(x_t\) is the same problem as the one that starts at 0 from \(x_0=x_t\). That is why the value depends only on \(x_0\), written \(V^*(x_0)\). It is also why the optimal choice will turn out to be a time-invariant policy \(x_{t+1}=g(x_t)\).
  • "max" is shorthand. Strictly, \(V^*\) is a supremum, and the infinite sum must be well defined. SLP assume \(\Gamma(x)\neq\emptyset\) (Assumption 4.1) and that \(\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{t=0}^n\beta^tF(x_t,x_{t+1})\) exists for every feasible plan, possibly as \(\pm\infty\) (Assumption 4.2). Bounded \(F\) with \(\beta\lt1\) is enough. The slides take these regularity conditions for granted, and so will we.

3. The neoclassical growth model in this notation

The planner maximizes \(\sum_t\beta^tU(c_t)\) subject to the resource constraint and the law of motion of capital EE p.4: $$c_t+i_t=G(k_t,1),\qquad k_{t+1}=i_t+(1-\delta)k_t,\qquad c_t\ge0,\ k_{t+1}\ge0,$$ with labor supplied inelastically and normalized to one, \(G\) neoclassical (constant returns, increasing and concave in \(k\)), and \(\delta\in[0,1]\) the depreciation rate.

  1. Eliminate investment. From the law of motion, \(i_t=k_{t+1}-(1-\delta)k_t\). Substituting into the resource constraint gives $$c_t=G(k_t,1)+(1-\delta)k_t-k_{t+1}\equiv f(k_t)-k_{t+1}.$$ Here \(f(k)\) is everything the economy has at the end of the period: output plus the capital left after depreciation.
  2. Eliminate consumption. Given \((k_t,k_{t+1})\), consumption is pinned down: \(c_t=f(k_t)-k_{t+1}\). So utility at \(t\) is a function of today's and tomorrow's capital only: $$F(x,y)=U\big(f(x)-y\big).$$
  3. Translate the inequality constraints. \(c_t\ge0\) becomes \(k_{t+1}\le f(k_t)\), and \(k_{t+1}\ge0\) is the lower bound. So $$\Gamma(x)=[0,f(x)],\qquad X=\mathbb R_+.$$

Why write the problem in states only? Because it leaves a single type of unknown, the capital path, with no constraints linking dates beyond \(k_{t+1}\in\Gamma(k_t)\). Each \(k_{t+1}\) appears in exactly two period returns: \(F(k_t,k_{t+1})\), where it is "spent", and \(F(k_{t+1},k_{t+2})\), where it "produces". Setting the derivative with respect to \(k_{t+1}\) to zero balances these two effects. That is the Euler equation of Lesson 27.

Several properties of \(F\) and \(\Gamma\) follow from those of \(U\) and \(G\). They are exactly the hypotheses the next lessons need.

  • Signs. \(F_x=U'(c)f'(x)>0\): more capital today means more to consume. \(F_y=-U'(c)\lt0\): more capital tomorrow means less consumption today. Capital is a "good" (\(F_x\ge0\)), which Lesson 27's sufficiency theorem uses.
  • Concavity. \(f(x)-y\) is concave in \((x,y)\) because \(f\) is concave. \(U\) is increasing and concave, and an increasing concave function of a concave function is concave, so \(F\) is concave. With \(U'\gt0\), \(U''\lt0\) and \(f''\lt0\), the Hessian $$F_{xx}=U''f'^2+U'f'',\quad F_{yy}=U'',\quad F_{xy}=-U''f',\qquad F_{xx}F_{yy}-F_{xy}^2=U'U''f''\gt0$$ is negative definite, so \(F\) is strictly concave.
  • Complementarity. \(F_{xy}=-U''f'\gt0\). A higher \(k_t\) raises the marginal utility cost of \(k_{t+1}\) less than it raises resources. This sign will make the Euler equation solvable for \(k_{t+2}\) (Lesson 28).
  • Convex graph. \(\mathrm{Gr}(\Gamma)=\{(x,y):x\ge0,\ 0\le y\le f(x)\}\) is the region under a concave function, a convex set.
  • A bounded state space. Let \(k_{\max}\) solve \(f(k)=k\), i.e. \(G(k,1)=\delta k\). For Cobb–Douglas \(G=Ak^\alpha\), \(k_{\max}=(A/\delta)^{1/(1-\alpha)}\). Above \(k_{\max}\), even consuming nothing cannot keep capital constant. So every feasible path stays in \([0,\max\{k_0,k_{\max}\}]\), and one can take \(X\) to be that compact interval.
Investment can be negative here

\(\Gamma(k)=[0,f(k)]\) allows \(k'\lt(1-\delta)k\), i.e. \(i\lt0\). The planner can "eat" installed capital, turning it back into consumption one for one. If investment is irreversible (\(i\ge0\)), the lower bound moves up: \(\Gamma(k)=[(1-\delta)k,f(k)]\). This is still a convex graph, but the constraint can now bind, for instance when \(k_0\) is far above the steady state. Toggle it in the figure.

A feasible plan is a chain of points in this region: \((k_0,k_1)\), then \((k_1,k_2)\), and so on, where each point's \(y\)-coordinate becomes the next point's \(x\)-coordinate. The 45° line performs that hand-off. The sequence problem asks for the best plan among all feasible chains. Already within this one-parameter family there is a trade-off: too little saving starves future production, too much starves current consumption.

4. States and controls: \([X,\mathcal U,h,g,\beta]\)

A second notation, closer to how models are usually written, separates the variables that carry the past into the future (states) from the variables chosen within the period (controls) EE p.5: $$V^*(x_0)=\max_{\{u_t\}_{t=0}^\infty}\ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\beta^th(x_t,u_t)\qquad\text{s.t.}\quad x_{t+1}=g(x_t,u_t),\quad u_t\in\mathcal U,\quad x_0\text{ given}.$$ \(\mathcal U\) is the set of controls, \(h\) the period return, and \(g\) the law of motion. This is the notation of the Hamiltonian (folder 7), where \(\dot x=g(x,u)\), and of many dynamic programming problems.

From controls to states EE p.5

Define $$F(x,y)=\max_u\{h(x,u):u\in\mathcal U,\ y=g(x,u)\},\qquad \Gamma(x)=\{y:\text{there is }u\in\mathcal U\text{ with }y=g(x,u)\}.$$ If the maximum defining \(F\) is attained for every \((x,y)\in\mathrm{Gr}(\Gamma)\), then the two sequence problems have the same value \(V^*(x_0)\). An optimal plan in one notation gives an optimal plan in the other.

Proof
  1. Every control plan is dominated by a state plan. Take any feasible \(\{u_t\}\) and the states it generates, \(x_{t+1}=g(x_t,u_t)\). By the definition of \(\Gamma\), \(x_{t+1}\in\Gamma(x_t)\), so \(\{x_{t+1}\}\) is a feasible state plan. By the definition of \(F\), \(h(x_t,u_t)\le F(x_t,x_{t+1})\), because \(u_t\) is one of the controls that lead from \(x_t\) to \(x_{t+1}\). Discount and sum: the control plan's value is at most the state plan's value, which is at most \(V^*_{\text{states}}(x_0)\).
  2. Every state plan is matched by a control plan. Take any feasible \(\{x_{t+1}\}\). Since \(x_{t+1}\in\Gamma(x_t)\), the set \(\{u\in\mathcal U:g(x_t,u)=x_{t+1}\}\) is non-empty. Pick \(u_t\) attaining the maximum in \(F(x_t,x_{t+1})\). The controls \(\{u_t\}\) are feasible, generate exactly \(\{x_{t+1}\}\), and earn \(h(x_t,u_t)=F(x_t,x_{t+1})\) every period. So the state plan's value is at most \(V^*_{\text{controls}}(x_0)\).
  3. Combine. Taking suprema in steps 1 and 2 gives \(V^*_{\text{controls}}(x_0)\le V^*_{\text{states}}(x_0)\le V^*_{\text{controls}}(x_0)\). If \(\{x^*_{t+1}\}\) is an optimal state plan, step 2's controls are an optimal control plan. If \(\{u^*_t\}\) is an optimal control plan, step 1's states are an optimal state plan, and the inequalities in step 1 hold with equality. ∎
What the inner maximization does

A control affects the future only through tomorrow's state. Once \(y\) is fixed, choosing among the controls that reach \(y\) is a static problem. Examples are splitting resources between consumption and leisure, or choosing hours given the human capital you want tomorrow. \(F\) solves that static problem once and for all. What remains in the sequence problem is purely intertemporal: how much state to carry forward. Exams use this split all the time: "intratemporal" conditions live inside \(F\), and the Euler equation is the intertemporal one.

5. Mapping models: the slide exercises, worked

Try each exercise before opening the solution. These are the exact tasks of slides 6–10. They are also the opening parts of several past exam questions (Section 6).

Slide exercise A (baseline): the growth model with controls EE p.6

Write the neoclassical growth model in the notation \([X,\mathcal U,h,g,\beta]\). What is the state and what is the control? The candidate variables are capital, consumption and investment.

Show solution

The state is capital, \(x=k\). It is predetermined at \(t\) and carries the past into the future. Either flow variable can be the control EE p.7:

  • Control = consumption, \(u=c\): \(g(x,u)=G(x,1)+(1-\delta)x-u\) and \(h(x,u)=U(u)\). The return depends only on the control.
  • Control = investment, \(u=i\): \(g(x,u)=(1-\delta)x+u\) and \(h(x,u)=U(G(x,1)-u)\). The law of motion is now linear and the return depends on both.

Both representations give the same \(F\): \(F(x,y)=U(f(x)-y)\). In each case only one control reaches a given \(y\) (\(c=f(x)-y\) or \(i=y-(1-\delta)x\)), so the inner max in the theorem is trivial. The feasibility constraints \(c\ge0\), \(k'\ge0\) make the control set depend on the state: \(c\in[0,f(x)]\), or \(i\in[-(1-\delta)x,\,G(x,1)]\). The fixed-\(\mathcal U\) notation hides this, and \(\Gamma\) captures it exactly: \(\Gamma(x)=[0,f(x)]\).

Slide exercise B (labor supply): two controls, one state EE p.6

Now utility is \(U(c,\ell)\) over consumption and leisure, with one unit of time, \(\ell+n=1\), and production \(G(k,n)\). Write the problem in \([X,\mathcal U,h,g,\beta]\) and in \([X,\Gamma,F,\beta]\). The candidate variables are capital, consumption, labor and investment.

Show solution

Controls. The state is \(x=k\). The controls are \(u=(u_1,u_2)=(c,\ell)\). (The slide says "let \(c\) and \(\ell\) be the state". That is a typo: they are the controls EE p.8.) The law of motion and the return are $$g(x,u)=(1-\delta)x+G(x,1-u_2)-u_1,\qquad h(x,u)=U(u_1,u_2).$$

States only. Now many controls lead to the same \(k'\): work more and consume more, or work less and consume less. So the inner maximization is a real problem: $$F(x,y)=\max_{c\ge0,\ \ell\in[0,1]}\ U(c,\ell)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad y=(1-\delta)x+G(x,1-\ell)-c.$$ At an interior solution, substitute \(c\) from the constraint and differentiate with respect to \(\ell\). The intratemporal condition is $$U_\ell(c,\ell)=U_c(c,\ell)\,G_n(x,1-\ell):$$ the marginal utility of an hour of leisure equals the utility value of what that hour would produce. The largest feasible \(y\) comes from working all day and consuming nothing: $$\Gamma(x)=\big[0,\ (1-\delta)x+G(x,1)\big].$$

Exercise 26.1 shows that this \(F\) is concave when \(U\) and \(G\) are.

Slide exercise C (Lucas–Uzawa): a two-dimensional state EE pp.9–10

Physical capital follows \(k_{t+1}=(1-\delta_k)k_t+i_t\). Human capital follows \(h_{t+1}=(1-\delta_h)h_t+h_t\varphi(s_t)\), where \(s_t\) is time spent learning and \(\varphi\) is increasing. Time is split as \(1=n_t+s_t\), and output is \(c_t+i_t=G(k_t,h_tn_t)\), with \(G\) constant returns in capital and effective labor \(hn\). Utility is \(\sum_t\beta^tU(c_t)\). Using \((k,h)\) as the state, write \(F(x,y)\) and \(\Gamma(x)\).

Show solution

Let \(x=(k,h)\) and \(y=(k',h')\). The slide's answer EE p.10 writes \(F\) in two equivalent ways: $$F(k,h,k',h')=\max_{c\ge0,\ n\in[0,1]}U(c)\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ h'=h(1-\delta_h)+h\varphi(1-n),\ \ c=G(k,hn)+(1-\delta_k)k-k',$$ or, substituting \(c\) out, $$F(k,h,k',h')=\max_{n\in[0,1]}U\big(G(k,hn)+(1-\delta_k)k-k'\big)\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ h'=h(1-\delta_h)+h\varphi(1-n).$$ With \(\varphi\) strictly increasing, the human capital constraint pins down hours, so the max is over a single point: $$n(h,h')=1-\varphi^{-1}\!\Big(\frac{h'}{h}-1+\delta_h\Big),\qquad F=U\big(G(k,h\,n(h,h'))+(1-\delta_k)k-k'\big).$$ The feasible set is $$\Gamma(k,h)=\big\{(k',h')\ge0:\ \exists\,n\in[0,1]\text{ with }h'=h(1-\delta_h)+h\varphi(1-n)\text{ and }G(k,hn)+(1-\delta_k)k-k'\ge0\big\}.$$ Equivalently, \(h'\in\big[h(1-\delta_h+\varphi(0)),\,h(1-\delta_h+\varphi(1))\big]\) and \(0\le k'\le G(k,h\,n(h,h'))+(1-\delta_k)k\).

Reading it. \(\Gamma(k,h)\) is now a two-dimensional set with a downward-sloping frontier. More human capital tomorrow requires more time learning today, hence less output and less room for physical capital tomorrow. The state is two-dimensional, but the return still depends only on \((x,y)\). (The slide's "time to lean" means "learn".)

6. What the exams ask: map the model, then check the signs

Mapping a model into \(F\) and \(\Gamma\) is the opening part of several past Alvarez questions. It appears in the Fall 2005 midterm, where human capital is worth 20 of 170 points, and in the Fall 2025 MT1, where two parts are worth 30 points. A later question then needs properties of \(F\): concavity for sufficiency (Lesson 27), and the sign of \(F_{xy}\) for the dynamics (Lesson 28, and folder 5 on stability). Graders want the full checklist:

The checklist for a mapped problem
  1. Substitute. Use the law of motion to eliminate investment, and the budget or resource constraint to eliminate consumption. Write \(F(x,y)\).
  2. Feasibility. Turn \(c\ge0\) (and \(k'\ge0\), or \(i\ge0\) if investment is irreversible) into bounds on \(y\). That is \(\Gamma(x)\).
  3. First derivatives. \(F_x>0\) (the state is a good) and \(F_y\lt0\) (accumulating is costly today).
  4. Concavity. \(F_{xx}\lt0\), \(F_{yy}\lt0\) and \(F_{xx}F_{yy}-F_{xy}^2>0\). The determinant is the step most often skipped, and it is needed: two negative diagonal entries do not make a matrix negative definite.
  5. Cross partial. The sign of \(F_{xy}\), usually positive.

For the growth model, written the Fall 2025 way as \(F(k,k')=v\big(f(k)+(1-\delta)k-k'\big)\) with production \(f\): $$F_1=v'\,(f'+1-\delta)>0,\quad F_2=-v'\lt0,\quad F_{11}=v''(f'+1-\delta)^2+v'f''\lt0,\quad F_{22}=v''\lt0,\quad F_{12}=-v''(f'+1-\delta)>0,$$ $$F_{11}F_{22}-F_{12}^2=v'v''f''>0.$$ The determinant is positive because it is a product of one positive and two negative numbers. A commonly circulated walkthrough of this exam checks only \(F_1,F_2,F_{11},F_{12}\) and declares strict concavity. Do not lose points that way. Exercises 26.3–26.5 are the past-exam versions.

7. Check your understanding

1. In the sequence problem \(\max\sum_t\beta^tF(x_t,x_{t+1})\), what is chosen at date \(t\)?
  • The state next period, \(x_{t+1}\)
  • The state this period, \(x_t\)
  • Consumption, always a separate control
  • The discount factor each period
\(x_t\) is inherited from yesterday's choice (or given, at \(t=0\)). Consumption has been substituted out: it is implied by \((x_t,x_{t+1})\). \(\beta\) is a parameter. The unknown is the sequence \(x_1,x_2,\dots\).
2. With \(f(k)=G(k,1)+(1-\delta)k\) and \(\delta\lt1\), which pair \((F(x,y),\,\Gamma(x))\) describes the growth model?
  • \(U(f(x)-y)\) and \([0,f(x)]\)
  • \(U(f(x))-y\) and \([0,f(x)]\)
  • \(U(f(x)-y)\) and \([0,x]\)
  • \(U(G(x,1)-y)\) and \([0,G(x,1)]\)
Consumption is \(c=f(x)-y\), and \(c\ge0\) gives \(y\le f(x)\). Utility is applied to consumption, not to resources alone. The last option drops the undepreciated capital \((1-\delta)x\), so it is right only when \(\delta=1\).
3. Why is \(F\) defined only on the graph of \(\Gamma\)?
  • The graph is always closed and bounded
  • Infeasible pairs always give returns of zero
  • Returns are needed only at feasible pairs
  • Concavity requires a bounded domain for F
The planner never visits an infeasible pair, and often \(F\) is not even defined there: \(\log(f(k)-k')\) has no value when \(k'\ge f(k)\). The graph need not be compact (\(X=\mathbb R_+\)), and concavity needs a convex domain, not a bounded one.
4. In the growth model with labor supply (slides 6–8), which variables are states and which are controls?
  • States \(c\) and \(\ell\); control \(k\)
  • State \(k\); controls \(c\) and \(\ell\)
  • States \(k\) and \(\ell\); control \(c\)
  • State \(\ell\); controls \(k\) and \(c\)
Capital is the only variable that links periods. Consumption and leisure are chosen within the period and enter \(F\) through an inner maximization. Slide 8's "let \(c\) and \(\ell\) be the state" is a typo for "controls".
5. In Lucas–Uzawa with \(\varphi\) strictly increasing, what pins down hours worked \(n\) inside \(F(k,h,k',h')\)?
  • The physical-capital choice \(k'\) given \(k\)
  • The marginal utility of leisure alone
  • Nothing: hours are fixed at one
  • The human-capital choice \(h'\) given \(h\)
\(h'=h(1-\delta_h)+h\varphi(1-n)\) can be inverted: \(n=1-\varphi^{-1}(h'/h-1+\delta_h)\). Choosing tomorrow's human capital is choosing today's time allocation. Leisure is not in utility here.
6. A drill writes the constraint \(c_t+k_{t+1}=Ak_t^\alpha\). What is the depreciation rate?
  • \(\delta=0\): capital never depreciates
  • \(\delta=1\): capital fully depreciates
  • \(\delta=\alpha\): depreciation equals share
  • Undetermined by this constraint
With depreciation \(\delta\), the constraint is \(c_t+k_{t+1}=Ak_t^\alpha+(1-\delta)k_t\). Here no undepreciated capital is left over, so \(\delta=1\). A commonly circulated study guide labels this "no depreciation", which is backwards.

8. Exercises

On past Alvarez exams this material is always the first step of a longer question: write the state, \(F\) and \(\Gamma\), then verify the hypotheses (signs, concavity, \(F_{xy}\)) that later parts use. The exercises below are in that format, with general functions.

True or false? Justify. Past exam Exam-inspired

Answer True or False and justify in at most four lines.

  1. In the sequence problem, the period return \(F(x,y)\) must be defined, and finite, for every pair \((x,y)\in X\times X\). exam-inspired (slide 3)
    Answer

    False. \(F\) is defined on \(\mathrm{Gr}(\Gamma)\) only EE p.3. In the growth model with log utility, \(F(k,k')=\log(f(k)-k')\) is not defined for \(k'\ge f(k)\). Those pairs are infeasible, so no value is needed there.

  2. Writing the growth model as \(F(k,k')=U(f(k)-k')\) loses information: the optimal consumption path cannot be recovered from the optimal capital path. exam-inspired
    Answer

    False. \(c_t=f(k_t)-k_{t+1}\) for all \(t\), and investment is \(i_t=k_{t+1}-(1-\delta)k_t\). Every quantity is a function of consecutive states. That is exactly why consumption could be substituted out.

  3. The growth model has a unique representation in the control–state notation \([X,\mathcal U,h,g,\beta]\). slide 7
    Answer

    False. Either consumption or investment can be the control: \((h,g)=(U(u),\,G(x,1)+(1-\delta)x-u)\) or \((U(G(x,1)-u),\,(1-\delta)x+u)\) EE p.7. Both collapse to the same \(F\) and \(\Gamma\). The state-only notation is the canonical one.

  4. To show that \(F(k,k')=v\big(f(k)+(1-\delta)k-k'\big)\) is strictly concave, with \(v\) and \(f\) strictly increasing and strictly concave, it suffices to check \(F_{11}\lt0\) and \(F_{22}\lt0\). Fall 2025 MT1 QII.10 (a circulating answer's shortcut)
    Answer

    False as a method, although the conclusion is true. Negative diagonal entries do not make a \(2\times2\) Hessian negative definite. Here the determinant is \(F_{11}F_{22}-F_{12}^2=v'v''f''>0\), and that step must be shown. (Alternative: \(f(k)+(1-\delta)k-k'\) is concave in \((k,k')\), and an increasing concave function of a concave function is concave.)

  5. In the growth model with endogenous labor supply, consumption and leisure are state variables. slide 8
    Answer

    False. They are controls, chosen within the period. Only \(k\) links periods. Slide 8 says "state", which is a typo. In the state-only notation they are maximized out inside \(F\), subject to the intratemporal condition \(U_\ell=U_cG_n\).

  6. With \(\Gamma(k)=[0,f(k)]\), the planner may choose negative gross investment. exam-inspired
    Answer

    True. Any \(k'\in[0,(1-\delta)k)\) means \(i=k'-(1-\delta)k\lt0\): installed capital is converted back into consumption. Irreversible investment would be \(\Gamma(k)=[(1-\delta)k,f(k)]\).

  7. With \(G(k,1)=Ak^\alpha\), \(\alpha\in(0,1)\), \(\delta\in(0,1]\), every feasible capital path satisfies \(k_t\le\max\{k_0,(A/\delta)^{1/(1-\alpha)}\}\) for all \(t\). exam-inspired
    Answer

    True. Let \(\hat k=\max\{k_0,k_{\max}\}\), where \(f(k_{\max})=k_{\max}\). Since \(f(k)-k=Ak^\alpha-\delta k\) is negative for \(k>k_{\max}\), \(f(\hat k)\le\hat k\). By induction, if \(k_t\le\hat k\) then \(k_{t+1}\le f(k_t)\le f(\hat k)\le\hat k\), using that \(f\) is increasing. So \(X\) can be taken compact.

  8. The growth model has one infinitely lived household and infinitely many dated goods. Its competitive equilibrium allocation therefore need not solve the planner's problem, for the same reason as in OLG. exam-inspired (Lessons 4 and 12)
    Answer

    False. The OLG failure needs infinitely many goods and infinitely many agents, which is what allows the aggregate endowment to have infinite value. With finitely many agents, each with finite wealth in equilibrium, the value is finite and the 1WT proof goes through (Lesson 12). With LNS, the CE allocation is PO, and with one household that means it solves the planner's problem.

  9. In the human capital problem with \(g(h_t)+m=c_t+p\,i_t\) and \(h_{t+1}=i_t+(1-\delta)h_t\), the feasible correspondence is \(\Gamma(h)=\big[0,\ (g(h)+m)/p+(1-\delta)h\big]\). Fall 2005 Midterm, Ex. 2 Q5
    Answer

    True. \(c=g(h)+m-p\big(h'-(1-\delta)h\big)\ge0\) gives the upper bound, and \(h'\ge0\) is the lower bound (the state space \(X=\mathbb R_+\)). Investment \(i=h'-(1-\delta)h\) may be negative.

  10. If \(F(x,y)\) is concave, then \(\tilde F=U\circ F\) is also concave for any concave \(U\). exam-inspired (Fall 2025 MT1 QII, version 2)
    Answer

    False without monotonicity. A decreasing concave \(U\) can destroy concavity. For example, \(F(x)=-x^2\) and \(U(z)=-z\) give \(\tilde F=x^2\), which is convex. With \(U\) increasing and concave, \(\tilde F\) is concave: \(\tilde F(\theta a+(1-\theta)b)\ge U(\theta F(a)+(1-\theta)F(b))\ge\theta\tilde F(a)+(1-\theta)\tilde F(b)\). This is the case in the exam.

Exercise 26.1 Proof Slides pp.6–8 (concavity survives the inner maximization)

Take the labor-supply model of slide exercise B. \(U(c,\ell)\) is jointly concave and strictly increasing in \(c\), and \(G(k,n)\) is concave.

  1. Show that \(F(x,y)\) does not change if the constraint \(y=(1-\delta)x+G(x,1-\ell)-c\) is replaced by \(c\le(1-\delta)x+G(x,1-\ell)-y\). Why is the inequality version more convenient? (5 pts)
  2. Prove that \(F\) is concave in \((x,y)\). (15 pts)
  3. Prove that \(\mathrm{Gr}(\Gamma)\) is convex. (5 pts)
  4. At an interior solution, compute \(F_x\) and \(F_y\). (Use the envelope theorem.) (10 pts)
Hint

For (b), take optimal \((c,\ell)\) at \((x,y)\) and \((\hat c,\hat\ell)\) at \((\hat x,\hat y)\). Show that the convex combination of the controls is feasible at the convex combination of the states. For (d), the Lagrangian of the inner problem is \(U(c,\ell)+\mu[(1-\delta)x+G(x,1-\ell)-c-y]\).

Show solution

(a) Since \(U\) is strictly increasing in \(c\), any solution of the inequality-constrained problem has the constraint binding: otherwise raise \(c\). So both problems have the same maximizers and the same value. The inequality version defines a convex feasible set when \(G\) is concave. The equality version does not, because the graph of a nonlinear function is not convex.

(b) Let \(\theta\in(0,1)\), with \(x_\theta=\theta x+(1-\theta)\hat x\) and similarly \(y_\theta,c_\theta,\ell_\theta\). Then $$c_\theta\le\theta[(1-\delta)x+G(x,1-\ell)-y]+(1-\theta)[(1-\delta)\hat x+G(\hat x,1-\hat\ell)-\hat y]\le(1-\delta)x_\theta+G(x_\theta,1-\ell_\theta)-y_\theta,$$ by concavity of \(G\). Also \(c_\theta\ge0\) and \(\ell_\theta\in[0,1]\). So \((c_\theta,\ell_\theta)\) is feasible at \((x_\theta,y_\theta)\), and $$F(x_\theta,y_\theta)\ge U(c_\theta,\ell_\theta)\ge\theta U(c,\ell)+(1-\theta)U(\hat c,\hat\ell)=\theta F(x,y)+(1-\theta)F(\hat x,\hat y).$$ The general lesson: maximizing a jointly concave function over a jointly convex set yields a concave value function of the parameters.

(c) \(\mathrm{Gr}(\Gamma)=\{(x,y):x\ge0,\ 0\le y\le(1-\delta)x+G(x,1)\}\) is the region under a concave function of \(x\), hence convex.

(d) Envelope theorem: \(F_x=\mu[(1-\delta)+G_k(x,1-\ell)]\) and \(F_y=-\mu\). The FOC for \(c\) gives \(\mu=U_c\). So \(F_x=U_c\,[1-\delta+G_k]\gt0\) and \(F_y=-U_c\lt0\). These are the same formulas as with fixed labor, evaluated at the optimal hours. That is why the Euler equation of Lesson 27 keeps its familiar form.

Exercise 26.2 Proof Slides pp.9–10 (is the Lucas–Uzawa return concave?)

Use the Lucas–Uzawa \(F\) from slide exercise C, with \(U\) and \(G\) increasing and concave and \(\varphi\) strictly increasing. Effective labor is \(E(h,h')\equiv h\,n(h,h')\).

  1. Let \(\varphi(s)=Bs\), with \(B>0\). Show that \(E(h,h')\) is linear in \((h,h')\), and conclude that \(F\) is concave in \((k,h,k',h')\). (10 pts)
  2. Now let \(\varphi\) be strictly increasing and concave. Show that \(\psi(z)\equiv1-\varphi^{-1}(z-1+\delta_h)\) is concave. Show that \(E(h,h')=h\,\psi(h'/h)\) is concave for \(h>0\). (15 pts)
  3. Conclude that \(F\) is concave. Which property of \(G\) beyond concavity did you use? (5 pts)
  4. In two lines, interpret \(\partial E/\partial h'\lt0\). (5 pts)
Hint

The inverse of an increasing concave function is increasing and convex. \(h\psi(h'/h)\) is the perspective of \(\psi\). Write \(\frac{\theta h'+(1-\theta)\hat h'}{\theta h+(1-\theta)\hat h}\) as a weighted average of \(h'/h\) and \(\hat h'/\hat h\).

Show solution

(a) \(h'=h(1-\delta_h)+hB(1-n)\) gives \(hn=h-\frac{h'-(1-\delta_h)h}{B}=h\Big(1+\frac{1-\delta_h}{B}\Big)-\frac{h'}{B}\), which is linear. Then \(G(k,E)\) is concave in \((k,h,h')\), because a concave function composed with a linear map is concave. Adding the linear term \((1-\delta_k)k-k'\) keeps it concave, and applying increasing concave \(U\) gives concave \(F\).

(b) \(\varphi^{-1}\) is increasing and convex, so \(z\mapsto-\varphi^{-1}(z-1+\delta_h)\) is concave, and so is \(\psi\). For the perspective, take \(h,\hat h>0\), \(\theta\in(0,1)\) and \(H=\theta h+(1-\theta)\hat h\). Let \(\omega=\theta h/H\in(0,1)\). Then \(\frac{\theta h'+(1-\theta)\hat h'}{H}=\omega\frac{h'}{h}+(1-\omega)\frac{\hat h'}{\hat h}\), so $$E(\text{mix})=H\psi\Big(\omega\tfrac{h'}{h}+(1-\omega)\tfrac{\hat h'}{\hat h}\Big)\ge H\big[\omega\psi(\tfrac{h'}{h})+(1-\omega)\psi(\tfrac{\hat h'}{\hat h})\big]=\theta E(h,h')+(1-\theta)E(\hat h,\hat h').$$

(c) \(G\) is concave and nondecreasing in its second argument, and \(E\) is concave. So \(G(k,E(h,h'))\) is concave in \((k,h,h')\): a concave, nondecreasing function of a concave function is concave. Then proceed as in (a). Monotonicity of \(G\) in labor is the extra property used.

(d) \(\partial E/\partial h'=-1/\varphi'(\cdot)\lt0\). More human capital tomorrow costs hours of work today. This is the intertemporal trade-off that makes \(\Gamma(k,h)\) downward sloping in the \((k',h')\) plane.

Exercise 26.3 Past exam: Fall 2005 MT, Ex. 2 Q1 and Q5 (human capital in \([X,\Gamma,F,\beta]\))

A household maximizes \(\sum_t\beta^tu(c_t)\). It produces \(g(h_t)\) goods from human capital \(h_t\), receives a constant endowment \(m\) of goods each period, and buys human capital investment \(i_t\) at the constant price \(p\) (in goods). Human capital follows \(h_{t+1}=i_t+(1-\delta)h_t\), with \(h_0>0\) given. \(u\) and \(g\) are strictly increasing, strictly concave and satisfy Inada conditions.

  1. Write the period budget constraint. (10 pts)
  2. Write the state \(x_t\), the return \(F(x_t,x_{t+1})\) and the correspondence \(\Gamma(x_t)\). (Use that consumption is non-negative.) (20 pts)
  3. Compute \(F_x,F_y,F_{xy}\) and show that \(F\) is concave. (10 pts)
  4. In one line: what role does \(m\gt0\) play when \(h\) is small? (5 pts)
Show solution

(a) \(g(h_t)+m=c_t+p\,i_t\).

(b) \(x_t=h_t\). Substituting \(i_t=h_{t+1}-(1-\delta)h_t\) gives \(c_t=g(h_t)+m-p\big(h_{t+1}-(1-\delta)h_t\big)\). So $$F(h,h')=u\big(g(h)+m-p(h'-(1-\delta)h)\big),\qquad\Gamma(h)=\Big[0,\ \frac{g(h)+m}{p}+(1-\delta)h\Big].$$

(c) \(F_x=u'(c)\,[g'(h)+p(1-\delta)]>0\), \(F_y=-p\,u'(c)\lt0\), and \(F_{xy}=-p\,u''(c)[g'(h)+p(1-\delta)]>0\). Consumption \(c(h,h')\) is concave in \((h,h')\), as \(g\) is concave and the rest is linear. \(u\) is increasing and concave, so \(F\) is concave, and strictly so because the determinant is \(F_{xx}F_{yy}-F_{xy}^2=p^2u'u''g''>0\).

(d) With \(m>0\), consumption can be positive even at \(h=0\), so \(\Gamma(h)\) is non-degenerate everywhere and the household can start accumulating from nothing. The exam's next questions derive the Euler equation \(u'(c_t)=\beta u'(c_{t+1})[1-\delta+g'(h_{t+1})/p]\) twice, from a Lagrangian and from this \(F\). Lesson 27 does the same.

Exercise 26.4 Past exam: Fall 2025 MT1 QII.10 (the growth model, two utility functions)

Version 1: \(\max\sum_t\beta^tv(c_t)\) s.t. \(c_t=f(k_t)-i_t\), \(k_{t+1}=i_t+(1-\delta)k_t\), with \(v\) and \(f\) strictly increasing, strictly concave and satisfying Inada conditions. Here \(f\) is production. Version 2 replaces \(v\) by \(U\circ v\), with \(U\) strictly increasing and concave.

  1. Map version 1 into \(F(k,k')\). (5 pts)
  2. Show that \(F\) is strictly increasing in \(k\), strictly decreasing in \(k'\), strictly concave, and has \(F_{kk'}>0\). Show every step of the concavity check. (10 pts)
  3. Let \(\tilde F=U\circ F\). Show that \(\tilde F_x>0\), \(\tilde F_y\lt0\), \(\tilde F_{xy}>0\) and that \(\tilde F\) is concave. (10 pts)
Show solution

(a) \(F(k,k')=v\big(f(k)+(1-\delta)k-k'\big)\).

(b) Write \(c=f(k)+(1-\delta)k-k'\) and \(R\equiv f'(k)+1-\delta>0\). Then \(F_1=v'R>0\), \(F_2=-v'\lt0\), \(F_{11}=v''R^2+v'f''\lt0\), \(F_{22}=v''\lt0\), \(F_{12}=-v''R>0\), and $$F_{11}F_{22}-F_{12}^2=v''^2R^2+v'v''f''-v''^2R^2=v'v''f''>0.$$ So the Hessian is negative definite and \(F\) is strictly concave. (A commonly circulated answer stops after \(F_{11}\lt0\) and \(F_{12}>0\). The determinant is the step that actually proves concavity.)

(c) \(\tilde F_x=U'F_x>0\), \(\tilde F_y=U'F_y\lt0\), and $$\tilde F_{xy}=U''F_xF_y+U'F_{xy}>0,$$ since \(U''\le0\) and \(F_xF_y\lt0\) make the first term non-negative. Concavity follows from "increasing concave of concave". (The exam then uses these signs to compare speeds of convergence of the two versions. That analysis is folder 5.)

Exercise 26.5 Past exam: Fall 2025 MT1 QII.11 (adjustment costs)

\(\max\sum_t\beta^tc_t\) s.t. \(c_t=f(k_t)-i_t-\Phi(k_{t+1}-k_t)\) and \(k_{t+1}=i_t+(1-\delta)k_t\). Here \(f\) is strictly concave production satisfying Inada conditions, and \(\Phi\) is the cost of changing the capital stock, with \(\Phi(0)=\Phi'(0)=0\) and \(\Phi''>0\).

  1. Map the problem into \(F(k,k')\). Using \(c\ge0\), write \(\Gamma(k)\) and show that its graph is convex. (10 pts)
  2. Compute the first and second derivatives of \(F\) and the Hessian determinant. Where are the signs \(F_1>0\), \(F_2\lt0\) guaranteed? (10 pts)
  3. The exam also states "\(\Phi'\ge0\)". Is that consistent with \(\Phi'(0)=0\) and \(\Phi''>0\)? (5 pts)
  4. In one line: which feature of \(F\) do adjustment costs create that linear utility alone would not? (5 pts)
Show solution

(a) \(F(k,k')=f(k)+(1-\delta)k-k'-\Phi(k'-k)\) and \(\Gamma(k)=\{k'\ge0:\ k'+\Phi(k'-k)\le f(k)+(1-\delta)k\}\). The function \((k,k')\mapsto k'+\Phi(k'-k)-f(k)-(1-\delta)k\) is convex, because \(\Phi\) composed with a linear map is convex and \(-f\) is convex. The graph is its lower level set intersected with \(k'\ge0\), hence convex. Each \(\Gamma(k)\) is an interval.

(b) \(F_1=f'+1-\delta+\Phi'(k'-k)\), \(F_2=-1-\Phi'(k'-k)\), \(F_{11}=f''-\Phi''\lt0\), \(F_{22}=-\Phi''\lt0\), \(F_{12}=\Phi''>0\), and $$F_{11}F_{22}-F_{12}^2=(f''-\Phi'')(-\Phi'')-\Phi''^2=-f''\Phi''>0.$$ Near a steady state, \(k'-k\approx0\) and \(\Phi'\approx0\), so \(F_1\approx f'+1-\delta>0\) and \(F_2\approx-1\lt0\). Far from it, the signs need \(\Phi'\gt-(f'+1-\delta)\) and \(\Phi'\gt-1\).

(c) No, not globally. \(\Phi'(0)=0\) and \(\Phi''>0\) force \(\Phi'(z)\lt0\) for \(z\lt0\), so "\(\Phi'\ge0\)" can hold only for \(k'\ge k\). The local results the exam needs are evaluated at the steady state, where \(\Phi'=0\), so nothing breaks. On an exam, say this in one line rather than silently using a false global sign.

(d) \(F_{12}=\Phi''>0\). With linear utility and no adjustment cost, \(F_{12}=0\): the return is separable in \(k\) and \(k'\), and the optimal path jumps to the steady state in one period (Lesson 29). Adjustment costs create the complementarity that makes adjustment gradual (Lesson 32).

Exercise 26.6 Exam-inspired (four more mappings)

For each problem, give the state, \(F(x,y)\), \(\Gamma(x)\) and \(X\). (5 pts each)

  1. Cake eating. A cake of size \(x_0\) is consumed over time: \(c_t+x_{t+1}=x_t\), \(c_t\ge0\).
  2. Saving at a constant interest rate. Assets \(a_t\) earn the gross return \(1+r\). Income \(w\) is constant, \(c_t+a_{t+1}=w+(1+r)a_t\), and borrowing is limited by \(a_{t+1}\ge-b\). Why might you want to shift the state to \(\tilde a=a+b\)?
  3. Irreversible investment. The growth model with \(i_t\ge0\).
  4. Habit formation. The growth model with utility \(U(c_t-\eta c_{t-1})\), \(\eta\in(0,1)\), \(c_{-1}\) given.
Show solution

(a) \(x\) is the cake, \(F(x,y)=U(x-y)\), \(\Gamma(x)=[0,x]\) and \(X=\mathbb R_+\). (This is SLP §5.2.)

(b) \(x=a\), \(F(x,y)=U\big(w+(1+r)x-y\big)\), \(\Gamma(x)=[-b,\ w+(1+r)x]\) and \(X=[-b,\infty)\). Shifting to \(\tilde a=a+b\) gives \(X=\mathbb R_+\), the lower bound assumed in the sufficiency theorem of Lesson 27. In shifted units, \(F(\tilde x,\tilde y)=U\big(w-rb+(1+r)\tilde x-\tilde y\big)\). Lesson 29 studies this problem with \(\beta(1+r)=1\).

(c) \(x=k\), \(F(x,y)=U(f(x)-y)\), \(\Gamma(x)=[(1-\delta)x,f(x)]\) and \(X=\mathbb R_+\).

(d) Utility depends on last period's consumption, so \(k\) alone is not enough to summarize the past: enlarge the state. Let \(x_t=(k_t,c_{t-1})\) and \(y=(k',c)\), where \(c\) is today's consumption, which becomes tomorrow's lagged consumption. Then \(F\big((k,c_-),(k',c)\big)=U(c-\eta c_-)\), and $$\Gamma(k,c_-)=\{(k',c):\ c=f(k)-k',\ 0\le k'\le f(k)\}.$$ The general rule: the state must contain everything from the past that affects current returns or feasibility.

Key takeaways

  • A dynamic problem is \([X,\Gamma,F,\beta]\). The sequence problem chooses tomorrow's state every period: \(V^*(x_0)=\max\sum_t\beta^tF(x_t,x_{t+1})\) s.t. \(x_{t+1}\in\Gamma(x_t)\). \(F\) is defined only on \(\mathrm{Gr}(\Gamma)\).
  • Growth model: \(c=f(k)-k'\) with \(f(k)=G(k,1)+(1-\delta)k\), so \(F(x,y)=U(f(x)-y)\) and \(\Gamma(x)=[0,f(x)]\). Every quantity is a function of consecutive states.
  • Control–state notation \([X,\mathcal U,h,g,\beta]\) maps to \(F(x,y)=\max\{h(x,u):y=g(x,u)\}\) and \(\Gamma(x)=g(x,\mathcal U)\), with the same value. Intratemporal choices (leisure, hours) are maximized out inside \(F\).
  • Exam checklist after mapping: \(F_x>0\), \(F_y\lt0\), \(F_{xx}\lt0\), \(F_{yy}\lt0\), the determinant \(F_{xx}F_{yy}-F_{xy}^2>0\), and the sign of \(F_{xy}\). For the growth model, \(\det=U'U''f''>0\) and \(F_{xy}=-U''f'>0\).
  • With one dynasty, the competitive equilibrium is the planner's solution: finitely many agents make the 1WT work, unlike in OLG. So solving \(V^*\) solves the model.
