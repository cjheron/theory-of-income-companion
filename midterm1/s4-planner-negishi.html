Session 4: The Planner, the Representative Agent and Negishi Weights

Lessons 21–23 · About 75 minutes · 16 true/false items + 4 guided derivations · Study-guide hub · Aggregation cheatsheet (sections up to Negishi)
What this session drills. The Spring 2023 midterm was built on this material: an 11-part planner problem, then an OLG question that reuses its answer. The Fall 2025 and Fall 2005 midterms each had a T/F item on \(\lambda_i=1/\mu_i\). By the end you should be able to:
  1. write the planner's FOCs \(\lambda_iu^i_l=\gamma_l\), solve for \(\gamma\) using feasibility, and say how allocations move with \(\lambda_i\);
  2. match them to the household FOCs \(u^i_l=\mu_ip_l\): \(\lambda_i\propto1/\mu_i\), \(p\propto\gamma\);
  3. use Negishi's method: guess weights, solve the planner, check budgets at \(p=\gamma\), update.
Not on the midterm: Gorman aggregation and its limits (Lessons 24–25). Where a past question touched them, the guide gives you the in-scope route.
Primary source Alvarez, Class Note 3 (Aggregation, Social Planner), slides 3–25. Textbook backup: Mas-Colell, Whinston & Green §16.E–16.F (Pareto optima as welfare-maximizers, and their FOCs). Original: Negishi (1960), "Welfare Economics and Existence of an Equilibrium for a Competitive Economy", Metroeconomica 12, 92–97.

1. Warm-up: blank-page recall (6 min)

Spaced repetition first (2 min): the Diamond law of motion with log utility and Cobb–Douglas, and the BY \(\dot C\) equation. Then:

  1. State the planner-weight theorem (both directions, with their hypotheses).
    (1) If \(\lambda\gg0\) and the allocation solves \(\max\sum_i\lambda_iu^i\) s.t. feasibility, it is PO. (2) Under HH, FF and CC (concave \(u^i\)), so that \(U\) is convex, every PO allocation solves the problem for some \(\lambda\ge0\), \(\lambda\ne0\). Zero weights can be needed for (2), but they break (1). L21 §4
  2. Exchange economy: the planner's FOCs and what each part says.
    \(\lambda_i\,\partial u^i(x^i)/\partial x_l=\gamma_l\) for all \(i,l\) (interior). Ratio across goods: every MRS equals \(\gamma_l/\gamma_k\) (efficiency). Ratio across agents: \(u^i_l/u^j_l=\lambda_j/\lambda_i\), so a higher weight means lower marginal utility, i.e. more consumption (distribution). L22 §5
  3. What is \(\gamma_l\)?
    The marginal value to the planner of one more unit of the aggregate endowment of good \(l\): \(\gamma_l=\partial U(\bar e;\lambda)/\partial\bar e_l\) (envelope theorem). L22 §7
  4. The dictionary between CE and planner.
    Household: \(u^i_l=\mu_ip_l\), with \(\mu_i=\partial V^i/\partial w\) the marginal utility of income. Planner: \(\lambda_iu^i_l=\gamma_l\). They match with \(\lambda_i=\kappa/\mu_i\), \(\gamma=\kappa p\). Richer agents (lower \(\mu_i\)) get higher weights. L23 §2
  5. Negishi's algorithm in one line.
    Guess \(\lambda\); solve the planner; set \(p=\gamma(\lambda)\); compute each agent's budget gap \(p\cdot(x^i(\lambda)-e^i)\); lower the weights of agents who consume more than their endowment is worth and raise the others; repeat until every budget balances. L23 §6

3. True or false? Justify in 1–4 lines

Past exam · Fall 2025 MT1 I.4
"Assume all agents have strictly concave, strictly increasing, differentiable utility functions satisfying Inada conditions. In a pure endowment economy, equilibrium prices of each commodity are proportional to a weighted average of the marginal utility of that commodity across all agents, with the same weights across agents for all commodities."

True. Inada gives interior demands, so each household's FOC is \(u^i_l=\mu_ip_l\) for every good, i.e. \(p_l=\frac{1}{\mu_i}u^i_l\) for every \(i\). Averaging over agents, \(p_l=\frac1I\sum_i\frac{1}{\mu_i}u^i_l\propto\sum_i\frac{1}{\mu_i}u^i_l\). Agent \(i\)'s weight \(1/\mu_i\) does not depend on \(l\). These are exactly the Negishi weights.

Past exam · Fall 2005 Midterm, Ex. 1 Q5
"Let \(\mu_i\) be the marginal utility of income of agent \(i\) and \(p_s\) the price of the consumption good in state \(s\). Let \(\lambda_i\) be the weight of agent \(i\) in the planner's problem and \(\gamma_s\) the marginal social value of good \(s\). Then setting \(\mu_i=\lambda_i\) and \(p_s=\gamma_s\) implies that the allocations of the competitive equilibrium and of the planner's problem coincide."

False. The household FOC is \(u_i'(c^i_s)=\mu_ip_s\) and the planner's is \(\lambda_iu_i'(c^i_s)=\gamma_s\). They coincide when \(\lambda_i=1/\mu_i\) (and \(\gamma=p\), up to a common scale), not \(\lambda_i=\mu_i\). With \(\lambda_i=\mu_i\) they coincide only if all \(\mu_i\) are equal. Rich agents have low \(\mu_i\) and get high weights.

Past exam · Fall 2025 MT1 I.6
"Pure endowment economy, \(I\) agents, \(m\) goods. A feasible allocation is envy-free if \(u^i(x^i)\ge u^i(x^j)\) for all \(i,j\). Consider the competitive equilibrium with endowments \(e^i=\frac1I\bar e\). Its allocation is envy-free."

True. Everyone's wealth is \(p\cdot\bar e/I\), so all budget sets are identical. Agent \(j\)'s bundle \(\bar x^j\) is affordable to \(i\), and \(i\) chose \(\bar x^i\), so \(u^i(\bar x^i)\ge u^i(\bar x^j)\). No LNS, convexity or identical preferences needed. Adding LNS, the 1WT makes it PO as well: a market route to "fair and efficient".

Traps: equal endowments are sufficient, not necessary; with production you also need equal shares.
Past exam · Fall 2005 Midterm, Ex. 1 Q2
"Consider an economy with one good, with commodities indexed by the state of nature. If all agents have expected utility and are risk averse but their subjective probabilities differ, then their equilibrium consumption is only a function of the aggregate endowment."

False. The CE is PO (with respect to each agent's own beliefs), so it solves the planner's problem with FOC \(\lambda_i\pi^i_sv_i'(c^i_s)=\gamma_s\). With common beliefs \(\pi_s\) cancels across agents, and \(c^i_s\) depends on \(s\) only through \(\bar e_s\). With different beliefs, \(c^i_s\) also depends on \(\pi^i_s\) relative to others' beliefs: agents bet, consuming more in the states they think likelier. Two states with the same \(\bar e\) can give \(i\) different consumption.

Exam-inspired
"Any allocation that solves the planner's problem \(\max\sum_i\lambda_iu^i(x^i)\) subject to feasibility, with all \(\lambda_i>0\), is Pareto optimal. No convexity is needed."

True. If a feasible allocation Pareto-dominated it, then \(u^i\ge\bar u^i\) for all \(i\), strictly for one, and with \(\lambda\gg0\) the weighted sum would be strictly larger, contradicting optimality. Convexity is needed only for the converse.

Exam-inspired common wrong statement
"An allocation that solves the planner's problem for some weights \(\lambda\ge0\), \(\lambda\ne0\), is Pareto optimal."

False. With a zero weight, the planner does not care whether agent \(i\) gets more. Example: one good, \(u^1=u^2=x\), \(X^1=[0,a]\) with \(a<\bar e\), \(\lambda=(1,0)\). Every allocation with \(x^1=a\) and \(x^2\in[0,\bar e-a]\) solves \(W\), but only \(x^2=\bar e-a\) is PO. (If the solution is unique, it is PO even with zero weights.)

Exam-inspired
"Every Pareto optimal allocation maximizes \(\sum_i\lambda_iu^i\) for some weights \(\lambda\ge0\), \(\lambda\ne0\), even if utility functions are only quasi-concave."

False. Necessity needs the utility possibility set \(U\) to be convex, which needs concave utility representations (CC) plus HH and FF. With quasi-concave but not concave \(u^i\), \(U\) can be non-convex, and a PO point in a "dent" of the frontier is not the maximizer of any linear function \(\sum\lambda_iu^i\). Concavity is cardinal: the representation matters.

Exam-inspired common wrong statement
"At an interior solution of the planner's problem, the common MRS between goods \(l\) and \(k\) does not depend on the Pareto weights \(\lambda\)."

False in general. The condition "all MRSs are equal to \(\gamma_l/\gamma_k\)" does not contain \(\lambda\), but its common value does. Changing \(\lambda\) moves the planner along the contract curve, and the MRS changes along it unless preferences are identical and homothetic (or, more generally, Gorman-aggregable). That is why equilibrium prices depend on the distribution of wealth.

Exam-inspired
"In a CE, the multiplier \(\mu_i\) on agent \(i\)'s budget constraint equals \(\partial V^i(p,w^i)/\partial w\). Doubling \(u^i\) doubles \(\mu_i\) and halves agent \(i\)'s Negishi weight, without changing any allocation."

True. By the envelope theorem (or directly: \(V_w=\sum_lu^i_lx_{l,w}=\mu_i\sum_lp_lx_{l,w}=\mu_i\), using the binding budget), \(\mu_i\) is the marginal utility of income. It is cardinal: scaling \(u^i\) by 2 scales \(\mu_i\) by 2 and \(\lambda_i=1/\mu_i\) by \(1/2\), so \(\lambda_iu^i\) and the planner's solution are unchanged.

Exam-inspired
"If two agents have the same strictly concave utility function, the richer one gets the smaller Negishi weight, because the planner favours those with high marginal utility."

False. With a common concave \(u\), \(V\) is concave in wealth, so the richer agent has the lower \(\mu_i\) and the higher weight \(\lambda_i=1/\mu_i\). The planner must put a high weight on an agent to justify giving it a large bundle (low marginal utility at the optimum).

Exam-inspired
"In a pure exchange economy, equilibrium relative prices depend only on the aggregate endowment \(\bar e\), not on how it is distributed across agents."

False in general. Redistributing endowments changes the Negishi weights \(\lambda_i=1/\mu_i\), which moves the planner along the contract curve, which changes \(\gamma_l/\gamma_k=p_l/p_k\). Example: two agents with Cobb–Douglas weights \(a_1\neq a_2\) on good 1. Moving wealth to the agent who likes good 1 more raises \(p_1/p_2\). It is true only in special (Gorman) cases, such as identical homothetic preferences.

Exam-inspired
"The multiplier \(\gamma_l\) on the resource constraint for good \(l\) in the planner's problem equals the marginal increase in \(U(\bar e;\lambda)=\max\sum_i\lambda_iu^i\) from one more unit of the aggregate endowment of good \(l\)."

True. Envelope theorem: \(\partial U(\bar e;\lambda)/\partial\bar e_l=\gamma_l\). With Negishi weights \(\gamma=p\), so the representative agent's marginal utilities at \(\bar e\) are proportional to equilibrium prices. This is how the representative agent "prices" goods.

cf. PSET 3 P3
"With expected utility \(\sum_s\pi_s\frac{x_s^{1-\sigma}}{1-\sigma}\), the same \(\sigma\) and the same beliefs for all agents, every Pareto optimal allocation gives each agent a constant share of the aggregate endowment across states."

True. The planner's FOC \(\lambda_i\pi_s(x^i_s)^{-\sigma}=\gamma_s\) gives \(x^i_s=(\lambda_i\pi_s/\gamma_s)^{1/\sigma}\). Summing over \(i\) and using feasibility: \(x^i_s=\frac{\lambda_i^{1/\sigma}}{\sum_j\lambda_j^{1/\sigma}}\,\bar e_s\). The share depends on the weights only, not on \(s\). (Different \(\sigma\)s or different beliefs break this.)

cf. PSET 3 P1 Ex. 11
"With strictly concave expected utility, common beliefs, and an aggregate endowment that is the same in every state, any Pareto optimal allocation gives each agent the same consumption in every state. So if markets are so incomplete that agents end up consuming their own risky endowments, the equilibrium is not Pareto optimal."

True. The planner's FOC \(\lambda_iv_i'(x^i_s)=\gamma_s/\pi_s\equiv\tilde\gamma_s\). Feasibility \(\sum_i(v_i')^{-1}(\tilde\gamma_s/\lambda_i)=\bar e_s\) pins \(\tilde\gamma_s\) as a function of \(\bar e_s\), so with \(\bar e_s\) constant, \(\tilde\gamma_s\) and every \(x^i_s\) are constant across states. Consuming one's own endowment, which varies across states, is therefore not PO. (Without interiority, Jensen gives the same: replacing each agent's consumption by its expected value is feasible when \(\bar e\) is constant and makes every risk-averse agent strictly better off.)

Exam-inspired
"The proof of the 1WT by matching household and planner first-order conditions uses the same assumptions as the general proof of Lesson 4."

False. It needs much more: differentiability, interior solutions (Inada), and concave utilities, so that the planner's FOCs are sufficient for a maximum. The general proof uses only LNS. (The smooth proof is useful because it delivers \(\lambda_i=1/\mu_i\) and \(p=\gamma\), not because it is more general.)

Exam-inspired
"In Negishi's method, after solving the planner's problem for arbitrary weights \(\lambda\gg0\), every agent's budget constraint holds with equality at prices \(p=\gamma(\lambda)\)."

False. For arbitrary weights the planner's allocation is PO but generally not affordable: \(p\cdot x^i(\lambda)\neq p\cdot e^i\) for some agents, which is a CE with transfers, not a CE. The method adjusts the weights (down for agents who consume more than their endowment is worth, up for the others) until all budgets balance. Only then is the planner's allocation the CE allocation. (By Walras' law the budget gaps sum to zero at any \(\lambda\).)

4. Guided derivations

Past exam · Spring 2023 Midterm 1, Q1 parts 1–8 each part "1 line" unless stated

Pure endowment economy, \(m\) goods, \(I\) consumers with $$u^i(x^i)=-\sum_{l=1}^m\pi_l\frac{(\theta^i_l-x^i_l)^{1+\sigma}}{1+\sigma},\qquad\pi_l>0,\ \sigma>0,\ \theta^i_l>0,$$ on \(X^i=\{x:x_l\le\theta^i_l\}\). The planner maximizes \(\sum_i\lambda_iu^i(x^i)\), \(\lambda_i>0\), s.t. \(\sum_ix^i_l=\bar e_l\). Let \(\bar\theta_l=\sum_i\theta^i_l\), and assume \(\bar e_l<\bar\theta_l\).

  1. (max 4 lines) Show \(u^i\) is strictly increasing and strictly concave on \(X^i\).
  2. Which \(\sigma\) makes \(u^i\) quadratic?
  3. Economic interpretation of \(\theta^i_l\)?
  4. The planner's FOC for \(x^i_l\), with \(\gamma_l\) the multiplier on good \(l\).
  5. Solve for \(x^i_l\) in terms of \(\theta^i_l,\lambda_i,\gamma_l/\pi_l,\sigma\).
  6. Use feasibility to find \(\gamma_l\) in terms of \(\pi_l,\bar\theta_l-\bar e_l,\sigma,\{\lambda_j\}\).
  7. Substitute: \(x^i_l-\theta^i_l\) in terms of \(\bar e_l-\bar\theta_l\), \(\sigma\) and the \(\lambda\)'s.
  8. (2 lines) Write \(x^i_l-\theta^i_l=\delta^i(\bar e_l-\bar\theta_l)\). Give \(\delta^i>0\), show \(\sum_i\delta^i=1\), and show \(x^i_l\) rises with \(\lambda_i\).

1. Each term is \(v(c)=-\pi(\theta-c)^{1+\sigma}/(1+\sigma)\) with \(v'=\pi(\theta-c)^\sigma>0\) and \(v''=-\pi\sigma(\theta-c)^{\sigma-1}<0\) for \(c<\theta\). (The official solution has a sign typo in \(v''\).) A separable sum of such terms is strictly increasing, and strictly concave since its Hessian is diagonal and negative.
2. \(\sigma=1\).
3. The satiation (bliss) point of consumer \(i\) for good \(l\). LNS fails exactly at \(x=\theta^i\).
4. \(\lambda_i\pi_l(\theta^i_l-x^i_l)^\sigma=\gamma_l\).
5. \(x^i_l-\theta^i_l=-(\gamma_l/\pi_l)^{1/\sigma}\lambda_i^{-1/\sigma}\).
6. Sum over \(i\): \(\bar\theta_l-\bar e_l=(\gamma_l/\pi_l)^{1/\sigma}\sum_j\lambda_j^{-1/\sigma}\), so \(\gamma_l=\pi_l\dfrac{(\bar\theta_l-\bar e_l)^\sigma}{\big(\sum_j\lambda_j^{-1/\sigma}\big)^\sigma}\).
7. \(x^i_l-\theta^i_l=(\bar e_l-\bar\theta_l)\dfrac{\lambda_i^{-1/\sigma}}{\sum_j\lambda_j^{-1/\sigma}}\).
8. \(\delta^i=\lambda_i^{-1/\sigma}/\sum_j\lambda_j^{-1/\sigma}\), positive and summing to one. Raising \(\lambda_i\) lowers \(\delta^i\), and since \(\bar e_l-\bar\theta_l<0\), it raises \(x^i_l\): a higher weight means more consumption (closer to satiation).

Past exam · Spring 2023 Midterm 1, Q2 5 parts

Two-period OLG. Each cohort has types \(i=1,\dots,I\) with $$u^{t,i}=-\pi_y\frac{(\theta^i_y-x^{i,t}_t)^{1+\sigma}}{1+\sigma}-\pi_o\frac{(\theta^i_o-x^{i,t}_{t+1})^{1+\sigma}}{1+\sigma},$$ endowments \(\alpha^i_y\) young and \(\alpha^i_o\) old; the initial old of type \(i\) have \(u=x\) and endowment \(\alpha^i_o\). Let \(\bar\alpha_y=\sum_i\alpha^i_y\), \(\bar\alpha_o=\sum_i\alpha^i_o\), \(\bar\theta_y=\sum_i\theta^i_y\), \(\bar\theta_o=\sum_i\theta^i_o\) (assume \(\bar\alpha<\bar\theta\)). Answer yes/no where asked.

  1. Is the equilibrium equal to autarky?
  2. Is the total consumption of each cohort when young equal to \(\bar\alpha_y\)?
  3. Is the interest rate constant?
  4. Find \(\frac{1}{1+r_t}=\frac{p_{t+1}}{p_t}\) in terms of \(\bar\theta_y,\bar\theta_o,\pi_y,\pi_o,\bar\alpha_y,\bar\alpha_o,\sigma\). (The exam's hint: treat the cohort as a representative agent. In-scope route: each cohort is a two-good exchange economy, use part 6 of the previous question.)
  5. Suppose \(\pi_o<\pi_y\), \(\bar\alpha_y=\bar\alpha_o\), \(\bar\theta_y=\bar\theta_o\). Is \(r\) positive or negative? Is the allocation PO? Justify.

1. No (generically): types differ, so they trade within the cohort.
2. Yes. The initial old (linear utility) consume their endowments, so date-1 feasibility gives cohort 1's young \(\bar\alpha_y\) in total. Summing cohort 1's budgets then gives \(\bar\alpha_o\) when old, and induction does the rest: no trade across cohorts. (The exam prints "\(I-\bar\alpha\)", a typo carried over from the problem-set version.)
3. Yes (see 4).
4. Each cohort's CE is a CE of a two-good exchange economy (goods: young-date and old-date consumption), so it is PO and solves a planner problem with Negishi weights, and relative prices equal \(\gamma_o/\gamma_y\). From part 6 of the previous question the \(\sum_j\lambda_j^{-1/\sigma}\) factor cancels in the ratio: \(\dfrac{p_{t+1}}{p_t}=\dfrac{\pi_o(\bar\theta_o-\bar\alpha_o)^\sigma}{\pi_y(\bar\theta_y-\bar\alpha_y)^\sigma}\), the same for every \(t\).
5. \(\frac{1}{1+r}=\pi_o/\pi_y<1\), so \(r>0\). PO, because \(p_t=(1+r)^{-(t-1)}\) falls geometrically, so the value of the aggregate endowment \(\sum_tp_t(\bar\alpha_y+\bar\alpha_o)\) is finite and the 1WT applies. "\(r>0\)" alone is not a full justification.

Exam-inspired 50 pts

Two goods, \(I\) agents, \(u^i=a_i\log x^i_1+(1-a_i)\log x^i_2\), \(a_i\in(0,1)\), endowments \(e^i\gg0\), aggregate \(\bar e\).

  1. (10 pts, 2 lines) For weights \(\lambda\gg0\), solve the planner: \(x^i_l\) and \(\gamma_l\) as functions of \(\lambda\).
  2. (10 pts, 1 line) Compute \(\gamma\cdot x^i(\lambda)\).
  3. (10 pts, 2 lines) Using \(p=\gamma(\lambda)\), write the condition on \(\lambda\) under which the planner's allocation is a CE (Negishi's fixed point).
  4. (10 pts, 2 lines) Show \(\lambda_i\) equals agent \(i\)'s wealth, and check this against \(\lambda_i=1/\mu_i\).
  5. (10 pts, 2 lines) Show \(p_1/p_2\) depends on the distribution of endowments unless all \(a_i\) are equal.

(a) FOC \(\lambda_ia_i/x^i_1=\gamma_1\Rightarrow x^i_1=\lambda_ia_i/\gamma_1\); feasibility gives \(\gamma_1=\sum_j\lambda_ja_j/\bar e_1\). Similarly \(x^i_2=\lambda_i(1-a_i)/\gamma_2\), \(\gamma_2=\sum_j\lambda_j(1-a_j)/\bar e_2\).
(b) \(\gamma\cdot x^i=\lambda_ia_i+\lambda_i(1-a_i)=\lambda_i\).
(c) Each budget must balance at \(p=\gamma(\lambda)\): \(\lambda_i=\gamma(\lambda)\cdot e^i\) for all \(i\). Summing gives \(\sum\lambda_i=\gamma\cdot\bar e=\sum\lambda_i\), so one equation is redundant (Walras): the weights are pinned down up to scale.
(d) By (c), \(\lambda_i=p\cdot e^i=w_i\). For log utility with weights summing to 1, \(V^i=\log w_i+\text{const}(p)\), so \(\mu_i=1/w_i\) and \(1/\mu_i=w_i=\lambda_i\). ✓
(e) \(\frac{p_1}{p_2}=\frac{\gamma_1}{\gamma_2}=\frac{\sum_j\lambda_ja_j}{\sum_j\lambda_j(1-a_j)}\cdot\frac{\bar e_2}{\bar e_1}\). If all \(a_j=a\), this is \(\frac{a}{1-a}\frac{\bar e_2}{\bar e_1}\), independent of \(\lambda\). Otherwise, shifting wealth (weight) toward agents with high \(a_j\) raises \(p_1/p_2\).

Past exam · Fall 2025 Final Q2 20 pts · optional if short on time

\(m\) goods, \(I\) agents, \(u^i(c)=H(c-\theta^i)\), with \(H\) homogeneous of degree one, strictly increasing and strictly quasi-concave. Take \(c^i\ge\theta^i\) and \(\bar e\ge\bar\theta\equiv\sum_i\theta^i\).

  1. (10 pts) Fix \(\lambda\) and \(\bar e\). Write the problem that defines \(U(\bar e;\lambda)\). Do not solve it.
  2. (10 pts) Write an expression for \(U(\bar e;\lambda)\) using \(H\) and a simple transformation of the \(\theta^i\). Start with equal weights \(\lambda_i=\lambda\).
  3. (check) Test your answer: with \(I=1\) it must give \(\lambda_1H(\bar e-\theta^1)\), and \(U(\bar e;k\lambda)=kU(\bar e;\lambda)\).
Hint for 2

Let \(z^i=c^i-\theta^i\). HD1 + quasi-concave + positive ⇒ concave, and concave + HD1 ⇒ superadditive: \(H(a+b)\ge H(a)+H(b)\).

1. \(U(\bar e;\lambda)=\max_{\{c^i\}}\sum_i\lambda_iH(c^i-\theta^i)\) s.t. \(\sum_ic^i=\bar e\) (good by good), \(c^i\ge\theta^i\).
2. With \(Z=\bar e-\bar\theta\): \(\lambda\sum_iH(z^i)\le\lambda H(\sum_iz^i)=\lambda H(Z)\), attained by any split along the ray \(z^i=s_iZ\). So \(U=\lambda H(\bar e-\bar\theta)\): the transformation is the unweighted sum \(\bar\theta=\sum_i\theta^i\). (With unequal weights, \(U=(\max_i\lambda_i)H(\bar e-\bar\theta)\), a corner.)
3. Both checks pass. The circulating answer \(H(\bar e-\sum_i\lambda_i\theta^i)\) fails both, and is right only when every \(\lambda_i=1\).
