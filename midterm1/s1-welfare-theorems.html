Session 1: The Welfare Theorems

Lessons 1–7 · About 75 minutes · 16 true/false items + 3 guided derivations · Study-guide hub · Cheatsheet
What this session drills. The most-tested idea on Alvarez's midterms is exactly which hypothesis each welfare theorem uses. Three of the five in-scope true/false items on the Fall 2025 midterm, and the first item of the Fall 2005 midterm, are of this kind. By the end you should be able to:
  1. prove the 1WT from memory and name the step each assumption feeds;
  2. say what breaks (and what does not) under IRS, externalities, taxes, incomplete markets and heterogeneous beliefs;
  3. state the 2WT with its real conclusion (a quasi-equilibrium) and the extra condition that turns it into a CE.
Primary source Alvarez, Slides 1, pp.15–35 (1WT statement and proof, failure questions, 2WT, Arrow's remark). Textbook backup: Mas-Colell, Whinston & Green, Microeconomic Theory, §16.C (1WT) and §16.D (2WT and quasi-equilibrium).
How each question works
  1. Commit first. For true/false, pick True or False and write a 1–4 line justification, as on the exam. For derivations, work on paper.
  2. Check. The T/F call is graded automatically. Then grade your justification against the model answer. Be strict: on the exam, a right call with the wrong reason earns little.
  3. Follow the link if you miss. Each miss shows the exact lesson section to reread, and goes on your review queue (saved in this browser). Monday's session sends you back to it.

1. Warm-up: blank-page recall (5 min)

Before anything else, write these on a blank sheet from memory. Then click Show to check. This is the highest-value five minutes of the session: retrieval, not rereading, is what builds memory that lasts until Wednesday.

  1. Define a competitive equilibrium \((p,\bar x,\bar y)\).
    (i) \(\bar y^j\in\arg\max_{y\in Y^j}p\cdot y\) for each firm; (ii) \(\bar x^i\in\arg\max\{u^i(x):x\in X^i,\ p\cdot x\le p\cdot e^i+\sum_j\theta^i_jp\cdot\bar y^j\}\) for each household; (iii) feasibility \(\sum_i\bar x^i=\sum_i e^i+\sum_j\bar y^j\). L2 §2
  2. Define local non-satiation.
    For every \(x\in X^i\) and every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is \(\hat x\in X^i\) with \(\|\hat x-x\|<\varepsilon\) and \(u^i(\hat x)>u^i(x)\). L3 §3
  3. State the 1WT and list the five moves of its proof.
    CE + LNS ⇒ the CE allocation is PO. Suppose a feasible \((x,y)\) Pareto-dominates. (1) \(u^i(x^i)\ge u^i(\bar x^i)\Rightarrow p\cdot x^i\ge p\cdot\bar x^i\) (LNS). (2) Strictly for the one who is strictly better off (utility maximization). (3) Sum: \(p\cdot\sum x^i>p\cdot\sum\bar x^i\) (needs a finite sum). (4) Profit max: \(p\cdot\sum\bar y^j\ge p\cdot\sum y^j\). (5) Feasibility of both allocations turns (3) and (4) into a contradiction. L4 §3
  4. State the 2WT: hypotheses and conclusion.
    HH (convex \(X^i\), continuous, convex upper contour sets), FF (aggregate \(Y\) convex), and some household not satiated at \(\bar x\). Then for a PO allocation there is \(p\ne0\) such that firms maximize profit and each household minimizes expenditure on its upper contour set: a quasi-equilibrium. L6 §3
  5. What turns a quasi-equilibrium into a CE?
    Arrow's remark: if \(u^i\) is continuous and there is a cheaper point \(\tilde x^i\in X^i\) with \(p\cdot\tilde x^i\lt p\cdot\bar x^i\), expenditure minimization implies utility maximization. L7 §2

2. Read first: only the boxes you could not reproduce

If a recall item above was shaky, read just its box (about 5 minutes each), then come back. Skip what you got right.

3. True or false? Justify in 1–4 lines

About 3 minutes each. On the exam these are worth 10–20 points apiece, and the justification carries most of the points.

Past exam · Fall 2025 MT1 I.1
"If there are finitely many commodities, and preferences satisfy local non-satiation, the allocation of a competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient."

True. These are exactly the hypotheses of the 1WT. LNS gives \(p\cdot x^i\ge p\cdot\bar x^i\) for any bundle at least as good, and utility maximization makes it strict for a strictly better one. With finitely many goods, \(p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i=p\cdot(\sum_ie^i+\sum_j\bar y^j)\) is a finite number, so the inequalities can be summed. Profit maximization and feasibility then give a contradiction. Nothing about convexity, continuity or the number of agents is needed.

Full marks: "all hypotheses of the 1WT hold" plus the role of LNS. Bonus insight: "finitely many goods" is what rules out the OLG failure.
Past exam · Fall 2025 MT1 I.2
"If there are increasing returns to scale, a competitive equilibrium can NOT be efficient."

False. IRS makes the production set non-convex. The 1WT never uses convexity: move (4) only uses that \(\bar y^j\) maximizes \(p\cdot y\) on \(Y^j\), whatever the shape of \(Y^j\). So if a CE exists, it is PO. What IRS threatens is existence of a price-taking CE (profit \(n^\gamma-wn\) is unbounded for \(\gamma>1\)), and the 2WT, which needs convex \(Y\).

Full marks: "the 1WT needs only LNS; convexity is a 2WT hypothesis." Saying "IRS ⇒ monopoly ⇒ inefficient" earns nothing: a monopoly outcome is not a CE.
Past exam · Fall 2005 Midterm, Ex. 1 Q1
"The First Welfare Theorem requires as an assumption that the utility functions be all quasi-concave."

False. The 1WT uses only LNS (together with the definition of a CE). Quasi-concavity, i.e. convex upper contour sets, is part of Assumption HH, which the Second Welfare Theorem needs to make the set \(A\) convex so it can be separated from \(B\).

Exam-inspired
"The 1WT requires preferences to be continuous and strictly increasing."

False. It requires only LNS. Strictly increasing preferences on \(\mathbb R^m_+\) imply LNS (move a little along any coordinate), so they are sufficient but not necessary: \(u(x)=x_1-x_2\) is not increasing, yet it is locally non-satiated (raise \(x_1\) a little). Continuity is never used in the 1WT proof (it matters for the 2WT and for Arrow's remark).

Exam-inspired
"If one household has a 'thick' indifference curve (a region with non-empty interior on which its utility is constant), a competitive equilibrium allocation can fail to be Pareto optimal."

True. LNS fails on the thick region, so move (1) breaks: a bundle that is just as good can be strictly cheaper. If the CE bundle sits inside the thick region, the household could give up some goods at no utility cost and those goods could make another household strictly better off. The CE is then Pareto-dominated.

"Can fail" is the key word: the theorem's conclusion is no longer guaranteed, though a particular CE may still be PO.
Past exam · Fall 2005 Midterm, Ex. 1 Q3
"In the security market economy, if the number of securities is smaller than the number of states, the resulting equilibrium allocation is necessarily inefficient."

False, because of "necessarily". With missing markets, some marginal rates of substitution are never equalized, so the CE can fail to be (unconstrained) PO. But it need not: if endowments are already Pareto optimal (e.g. identical agents, or endowments proportional to the aggregate with identical homothetic preferences), or if the available securities span the trades agents actually want, the equilibrium is efficient.

Exam-inspired cf. Fall 2005 Ex. 1 Q2
"With commodities indexed by states of nature, if households hold different subjective probabilities over the states, the competitive equilibrium allocation is not Pareto optimal."

False. Beliefs live inside \(u^i(x)=\sum_s\pi^i_sv^i(x_s)\), and the 1WT proof never looks inside \(u^i\). With complete markets and LNS, the CE is PO with respect to each household's own beliefs (ex-ante efficiency). Agents bet against each other, and at most one belief can be right. A planner using the "true" probabilities would apply a different welfare criterion.

Exam-inspired
"With a consumption externality (household 1's utility depends on household 2's consumption), the step of the 1WT proof that fails is 'every bundle at least as good as \(\bar x^i\) costs at least as much as \(\bar x^i\)'."

True. With an externality, \(u^1(x^1,x^2)\). Household 1's optimality says \(\bar x^1\) is best given \(\bar x^2\). A Pareto-improving allocation changes \(x^2\) too, so "\(u^1(x^1,x^2)\ge u^1(\bar x^1,\bar x^2)\)" no longer implies \(p\cdot x^1\ge p\cdot\bar x^1\). Moves (1)–(2) break. (A production externality breaks move (4) instead.)

Exam-inspired
"In a finite economy with production and LNS, a proportional tax on households' purchases of good 1 (producers receive the pre-tax price), with the revenue rebated lump sum to households, leaves the competitive equilibrium allocation Pareto optimal."

False (in general). Households pay \(p_1(1+t)\) while firms receive \(p_1\). Households set their MRS equal to the after-tax price ratio, firms set their MRT equal to the pre-tax ratio, so MRS ≠ MRT. In the proof, households and firms no longer face the same price vector, so moves (1)–(2) (household prices) and (4) (firm prices) cannot be combined. Contrast: a lump-sum tax only changes endowment values, and the 1WT still holds.

Exam-inspired
"Suppose all budget constraints bind, and at prices \(p\) markets \(1,\dots,m-1\) clear. If \(p_m=0\), market \(m\) need not clear."

True. Walras' law gives \(p\cdot z(p)=0\). If the other \(m-1\) markets clear, it reads \(p_mz_m=0\), which forces \(z_m=0\) only if \(p_m\ne0\). A free good (\(p_m=0\)) can be in excess supply. So "\(m-1\) markets clear ⇒ the last clears" needs \(p_m\neq0\).

Exam-inspired
"Second Welfare Theorem: if preferences satisfy LNS, every Pareto optimal allocation is a competitive equilibrium allocation for a suitable redistribution of endowments."

False. Three things are missing. (i) Convexity: HH (convex upper contour sets, continuity) and FF (convex aggregate \(Y\)) are needed for the separating hyperplane. (ii) Some household must be non-satiated at \(\bar x\). (iii) Even then, the theorem only delivers a quasi-equilibrium (expenditure minimization). A cheaper-point condition (Arrow's remark) is needed to get utility maximization, i.e. a CE.

Exam-inspired
"At a quasi-equilibrium, each household's bundle maximizes its utility on its budget set."

False. At a quasi-equilibrium each household minimizes expenditure among bundles at least as good as \(\bar x^i\). The two coincide only with a cheaper point. Counterexample: \(u=\sqrt{x_1}+x_2\), \(\bar x=e=(0,1)\), \(p=(1,0)\). The bundle \(e\) minimizes expenditure (it costs 0), but at \(p_2=0\) good 2 is free, so demand for it is unbounded: \(e\) is not utility-maximizing. No other price vector works either.

Exam-inspired
"Let \(X^i=\mathbb R^m_+\), utilities continuous and strictly increasing, and suppose the Pareto optimal allocation gives every household \(\bar x^i\gg0\). Then any quasi-equilibrium price vector \(p\ne0\) supporting it also supports it as a competitive equilibrium (with transfers)."

True. Monotonicity gives \(p\ge0\) (a negative price would make expenditure minimization unbounded below). With \(p\ge0\), \(p\ne0\) and \(\bar x^i\gg0\), we have \(p\cdot\bar x^i>0=p\cdot0\), so \(0\in X^i\) is a cheaper point. By continuity, Arrow's remark turns expenditure minimization into utility maximization.

Full marks: name the three steps: \(p\ge0\), cheaper point \(0\), Arrow's remark (continuity).
Exam-inspired
"The endowments and ownership shares that decentralize a given Pareto optimal allocation as a competitive equilibrium are unique."

False. All that matters is each household's wealth at the supporting prices: \(p\cdot e^i+\sum_j\theta^i_j\pi^j+T^i=p\cdot\bar x^i\). Any combination of endowments and shares that delivers those wealth levels works, e.g. \(e^i=\bar x^i-\sum_j\theta^i_j\bar y^j\) for any shares θ. (With endowments and shares fixed, the transfers \(T^i\) needed are pinned down once \(p\) is, up to scale, at least when preferences are smooth; but they can be paid in any bundle worth \(T^i\).)

Exam-inspired
"With a fixed cost in production, a Pareto optimal allocation may be impossible to decentralize as a competitive equilibrium, whatever the transfers."

True. A fixed cost makes \(Y\) non-convex, so FF fails and the separating hyperplane argument breaks. In the Crusoe example \(c=(n-n_0)^\gamma\), at the price that supports the optimal tangency, the firm makes negative profits and would rather shut down when \(n_0>\frac{1-\gamma}{1+b}\). No price makes the optimum profit-maximizing.

Exam-inspired
"The 1WT requires many firms in each industry."

False. Price-taking is built into the CE definition. The proof never uses the number of firms: move (4) holds firm by firm. A single price-taking firm is fine. A firm that exercises market power is not in a CE at all, so the theorem says nothing about it.

4. Guided derivations

Alvarez's long questions are chains of short parts, each "1 line" or "max 3 lines". Answer each part on paper before revealing.

Exam-inspired Proof 50 pts

Prove the First Welfare Theorem. Let \((p,\bar x,\bar y)\) be a CE in which all households satisfy LNS. Suppose a feasible \((x,y)\) Pareto-dominates \((\bar x,\bar y)\), with household \(i'\) strictly better off.

  1. (10 pts, max 3 lines) Show \(u^i(x^i)\ge u^i(\bar x^i)\Rightarrow p\cdot x^i\ge p\cdot\bar x^i\).
  2. (10 pts, 1 line) Show \(p\cdot x^{i'}>p\cdot\bar x^{i'}\).
  3. (10 pts, 1 line) Show \(p\cdot\sum_j\bar y^j\ge p\cdot\sum_jy^j\).
  4. (10 pts, max 3 lines) Derive the contradiction.
  5. (10 pts, 1 line) Which step requires the value of the aggregate endowment to be finite, and why?
Hint for (a)

Argue by contradiction: if \(x^i\) were strictly cheaper, use LNS in a small ball around \(x^i\) that stays inside the budget set.

(a) Suppose \(p\cdot x^i\lt p\cdot\bar x^i\le w^i\). The budget set contains a ball around \(x^i\) (intersected with \(X^i\)). By LNS there is \(\hat x\) in that ball with \(u^i(\hat x)>u^i(x^i)\ge u^i(\bar x^i)\), and \(\hat x\) is affordable. This contradicts the optimality of \(\bar x^i\).
(b) If \(p\cdot x^{i'}\le p\cdot\bar x^{i'}\), then \(x^{i'}\) is affordable and strictly preferred, contradicting optimality.
(c) Each \(\bar y^j\) maximizes \(p\cdot y\) on \(Y^j\) and \(y^j\in Y^j\); sum over \(j\).
(d) Summing (a) and (b): \(p\cdot\sum_ix^i>p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i\). Feasibility of both allocations: \(\sum_ix^i-\sum_i\bar x^i=\sum_jy^j-\sum_j\bar y^j\). Take values: LHS \(>0\), while (c) says RHS \(\le0\). Contradiction.
(e) The summation in (d). If \(p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i=\infty\), the strict inequality reads "\(\infty>\infty\)" and gives nothing. This is how the 1WT fails in OLG.

Exam-inspired 40 pts

The cheapest point. One household, \(X=\mathbb R^2_+\), \(u(x)=\sqrt{x_1}+x_2\), endowment \(e=(0,1)\), no firms.

  1. (5 pts, 1 line) Why is \(\bar x=e\) Pareto optimal?
  2. (10 pts, max 2 lines) Show that \(p=(1,0)\) supports \(\bar x\) as a quasi-equilibrium.
  3. (15 pts, max 4 lines) Show that no price vector \(p\ge0\), \(p\ne0\) supports \(\bar x\) as a CE.
  4. (10 pts, 2 lines) Which hypothesis of Arrow's remark fails, and why does that matter?

(a) With one household, PO means maximizing \(u\) on the feasible set \(\{x\in\mathbb R^2_+:x\le e\}\). Since \(u\) is increasing, the maximum is \(e\).
(b) Expenditure minimization: \(\min\{x_1: u(x)\ge u(e)=1\}\). Every bundle costs \(x_1\ge0\), and \(\bar x\) costs \(0\), so it is a minimizer.
(c) If \(p_1>0,p_2>0\): demand has \(x_1^d>0\ne\bar x_1\) (the tangency \(x_1=(p_2/2p_1)^2\), or the corner \(x_1=p_2/p_1\) when wealth is too small). If \(p_2=0\) (so \(p_1>0\)): good 2 is free and \(u\) increases in \(x_2\), so demand is unbounded. If \(p_1=0\) (so \(p_2>0\)): good 1 is free and demand for it is unbounded. In no case is \(e\) demanded.
(d) There is no cheaper point: at \(p=(1,0)\) wealth is \(p\cdot e=0\), and no bundle in \(X\) costs less than 0. Arrow's argument mixes the better bundle with a strictly cheaper one; without one, expenditure minimization need not imply utility maximization.

Exam-inspired 50 pts

Audit the proof. For each modification, say whether the 1WT still holds and, if not, which move of the proof (1–5 in the recall box) breaks. Max 2 lines each.

  1. (10 pts) Household 1 is satiated on a whole region, \(u^1=\min\{x_1+x_2,\,1\}\), and its CE bundle lies in the interior of that region. (Bonus: what if instead it had a unique bliss point and consumed exactly it?)
  2. (10 pts) Firm 2's production set depends on firm 1's output (a production externality).
  3. (10 pts) Some production sets are non-convex.
  4. (10 pts) Infinitely many households and infinitely many dated goods (OLG).
  5. (10 pts) Some markets are missing: good 3 cannot be traded.

(a) LNS fails on the satiation region, so move (1) breaks: household 1 is just as happy with a strictly cheaper bundle in the region. The 1WT can fail: in the L5 §2 example, shrinking household 1's bundle toward \(x_1+x_2=1\) leaves it indifferent and frees goods that make household 2 strictly better off. Bonus: with a unique bliss point that household 1 consumes, the proof survives. Any allocation that household 1 weakly prefers must give it the same bundle, so move (1) holds trivially for it, and the others satisfy LNS.
(b) Move (4) breaks: \(\bar y^2\) maximizes profit on \(Y^2(\bar y^1)\), but the dominating allocation uses \(Y^2(y^1)\), a different set. The 1WT can fail.
(c) Holds. No move uses convexity. (Existence and the 2WT are at risk, not the 1WT.)
(d) Move (3) can break: if \(p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i=\infty\), summing gives "\(\infty>\infty\)". The 1WT can fail (Samuelson's OLG with \(r<0\)).
(e) Fails relative to unconstrained PO, at move (5): good 3 has no price, so feasibility cannot be valued and MRSs involving good 3 are never equalized. It holds relative to constrained PO (a planner who also cannot move good 3).
