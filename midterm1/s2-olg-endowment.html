Session 2: The OLG Endowment Economy

Lessons 8–13 · About 80 minutes · 15 true/false items + 4 guided derivations · Study-guide hub · OLG cheatsheet
What this session drills. "Is the OLG equilibrium efficient?" is the single most frequent in-scope question on past exams. It came up on the Fall 2005, 2017/18, Spring 2023 and Fall 2025 midterms and on the Fall 2025 final. The examiner wants one specific argument, so practise saying it:
  1. the CE is no trade, and the autarky interest rate comes from the MRS at the endowment;
  2. efficiency depends on whether the value of the aggregate endowment at CE prices is finite. With \(p_t=(1+\bar r)^{-(t-1)}\) and no growth, that means the sign of \(\bar r\); with growth it means \(1+\bar r\) versus \((1+g)(1+n)\);
  3. "\(r>0\) so the 1WT applies" is not a full answer. Say why the value is finite.
Primary source Alvarez, Class Note II, pp.4–33, and Class Note III, pp.25–27 (population growth). The classic readings: Samuelson (1958), JPE 66(6), and Shell (1971), "Notes on the Economics of Infinity".

1. Warm-up: blank-page recall (5 min)

Start with a 2-minute spaced repetition from Session 1: write the five moves of the 1WT proof. Then:

  1. Why is the CE of the Note II economy no trade? Give the induction.
    The initial old value only date-1 goods and own \(e^0_1\). Their budget is \(p_1x\le p_1e^0_1\), so \(x^0_1=e^0_1\). Date-1 feasibility then forces generation 1's young to consume \(e^1_1\). Generation 1's budget, with \(x_1=e^1_1\), forces \(x_2=e^1_2\) (strictly increasing \(u\)). Then date-2 feasibility pins generation 2's young consumption, and so on. L8 §4
  2. Log example, endowments \((1-\alpha,\alpha)\), \(v=(1-\beta)\log c_y+\beta\log c_o\): write \(1+\bar r\) and \(s(r)\).
    \(1+\bar r=\dfrac{(1-\beta)\alpha}{\beta(1-\alpha)}\), so \(\bar r=\dfrac{\alpha-\beta}{\beta(1-\alpha)}\). \(s(r)=\beta(1-\alpha)-\dfrac{(1-\beta)\alpha}{1+r}\). L9 §2
  3. The three cases.
    \(\beta>\alpha\) (\(\bar r<0\)): the best symmetric allocation \((1-\beta,\beta)\) Pareto-dominates the CE. \(\beta=\alpha\) (\(\bar r=0\)): CE = best symmetric; PO even though the value is infinite. \(\beta<\alpha\) (\(\bar r>0\)): the value is finite, so the CE is PO; generations \(t\ge1\) would prefer \((1-\beta,\beta)\) but the initial old would lose. L10 §4
  4. State the 1WT for infinitely many agents and goods.
    If preferences satisfy LNS and \(p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i<\infty\) (finite value of aggregate consumption = endowment), the CE allocation is PO. Sufficient, not necessary. L12 §2
  5. Growing economy: the inefficiency condition.
    \(\bar r\) is unchanged by \(g,n\). The best symmetric allocation dominates the CE iff \(1+\bar r<(1+g)(1+n)\), which is (up to the knife edge) when the value of the aggregate endowment is infinite. L13 §5

3. True or false? Justify in 1–4 lines

Past exam · Fall 2005 Midterm, Ex. 1 Q4
"Consider an equilibrium in the OLG model. If the value of aggregate consumption at market prices is finite, then the equilibrium allocation is Pareto optimal."

True (given LNS, which holds with increasing utility). This is the 1WT for infinitely many agents and goods. The only step that can fail in OLG is summing \(p\cdot x^i\ge p\cdot\bar x^i\) (strict for one \(i\)) over infinitely many agents. With \(p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i<\infty\), the sum is a legitimate strict inequality, and feasibility gives the contradiction exactly as in the finite case.

Exam-inspired
"In the OLG endowment economy, if the value of the aggregate endowment at equilibrium prices is infinite, the competitive equilibrium allocation is not Pareto optimal."

False. Finite value is sufficient for PO, not necessary. In Case 2 of the log example (\(\alpha=\beta\), \(\bar r=0\)), \(p_t=1\) for all \(t\), so the value is \(\sum_t1=\infty\). Yet the CE equals the best symmetric allocation \((1-\beta,\beta)\) and is PO.

Exam-inspired common wrong answer
"The OLG competitive equilibrium can be inefficient because markets are incomplete: the unborn cannot trade with the current old."

False as a diagnosis. The OLG CE is an Arrow–Debreu equilibrium with a price for every dated good, and every agent can trade in every market at which it is alive. Nothing in the 1WT proof requires agents to meet. The proof breaks at one step: with infinitely many agents and goods, the value of the aggregate endowment can be infinite, and summing the inequalities gives "\(\infty>\infty\)" (Shell's double infinity).

Some circulating review notes give the "missing market" answer. Alvarez's slides give the infinite-value one; use it.
Exam-inspired Note II pp.26–27
"In an economy with finitely many households and infinitely many dated goods, a competitive equilibrium in which all utilities are strictly increasing is Pareto optimal."

True. Strictly increasing utility gives LNS, so every budget binds: \(p\cdot\bar x^i=p\cdot e^i\). In a CE each household's wealth must be finite, or it would have no optimal choice. A finite sum of finite numbers is finite, so \(p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i<\infty\) and the 1WT applies. A failure needs a double infinity: infinitely many goods and infinitely many agents.

Exam-inspired
"The result that the OLG endowment economy has a unique, no-trade equilibrium relies on log utility."

False. The induction uses only that (i) the initial old value only date-1 consumption, (ii) each generation is a single (representative) household with strictly increasing utility, and (iii) feasibility at each date. Log utility only gives closed forms for \(\bar r\). What the result does need is one type per cohort: with heterogeneous households, they trade within the cohort (Lesson 16, and the Spring 2023 midterm).

Exam-inspired
"In the log example with endowments \((1-\alpha,\alpha)\) and weights \((1-\beta,\beta)\), the equilibrium interest rate is negative if and only if \(\beta>\alpha\)."

True. Prices support autarky: \(\frac{1}{1+\bar r}=\frac{v_2}{v_1}\big|_{e}=\frac{\beta/\alpha}{(1-\beta)/(1-\alpha)}\). So \(\bar r=\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\beta(1-\alpha)}\), which is negative iff \(\beta>\alpha\). Intuition: households want more old-age consumption (\(\beta\)) than their old-age endowment share (\(\alpha\)), but there is nobody to save with, so the interest rate falls until they are happy not to trade.

Exam-inspired
"In the log example, an increase in the old-age endowment share \(\alpha\) lowers the autarky interest rate."

False: it raises it. \(1+\bar r=\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\) is increasing in \(\alpha\). A higher \(\alpha\) means the young are poorer now and richer later, so they want to borrow at the old rate. Since nobody can lend, the interest rate rises until they no longer want to.

Exam-inspired
"In the log example with \(\beta>\alpha\), the allocation giving every generation \((1-\beta,\beta)\) and the initial old \(\beta\) Pareto-dominates the competitive equilibrium."

True. It is feasible (\(c_y+c_o=1\) at each date). Every generation \(t\ge1\) strictly prefers \((1-\beta,\beta)\), the maximizer of \(v\) on \(c_y+c_o=1\), to \((1-\alpha,\alpha)\) since \(\alpha\ne\beta\). The initial old get \(\beta>\alpha\). Everyone is strictly better off. The implementation is a never-ending chain: each young gives \(\beta-\alpha\) to the current old.

Exam-inspired
"In the log example with \(\beta<\alpha\), every generation \(t\ge1\) prefers \((1-\beta,\beta)\) to its CE bundle, so the CE is not Pareto optimal."

False. The premise is true but the conclusion does not follow. Moving to \((1-\beta,\beta)\) requires the old to consume less (\(\beta<\alpha\)), so the initial old lose. In fact the CE is PO: \(\bar r>0\), so \(p_t=(1+\bar r)^{-(t-1)}\) and the value of the aggregate endowment is \(\sum_tp_t=(1+\bar r)/\bar r<\infty\). The 1WT applies.

Exam-inspired
"If \(\beta<\alpha\), introducing a small pay-as-you-go social security tax \(\tau>0\) makes every agent in the economy worse off."

False: the initial old receive \(\tau\) without ever paying, so they gain. Every generation \(t\ge1\) loses: at \(\tau=0\), \(\frac{d}{d\tau}v(1-\alpha-\tau,\alpha+\tau)=-v_1+v_2\), which is negative when \(\bar r>0\) (the MRS \(v_1/v_2=1+\bar r>1\)). PAYG pays a return of 0 (population growth) while the market pays \(\bar r>0\).

Exam-inspired
"If \(\beta>\alpha\), every PAYG tax \(\tau\in(0,\beta-\alpha]\) is a Pareto improvement over the competitive equilibrium."

True. After-tax endowments are \((1-\alpha-\tau,\alpha+\tau)\) and the CE is still no trade. \(W(\tau)=v(1-\alpha-\tau,\alpha+\tau)\) is strictly concave, increasing at \(0\) (since \(\bar r<0\)), and maximized at \(\tau=\beta-\alpha\). So \(W(\tau)>W(0)\) on \((0,\beta-\alpha]\), and in fact up to some \(\bar\tau>\beta-\alpha\). The initial old gain \(\tau\).

Exam-inspired Note II p.30
"In the growing OLG endowment economy (endowments \((1+g)^t(1-\alpha)\) young and \((1+g)^t\alpha\) old, cohort size \((1+n)^t\)), faster productivity growth \(g\) raises the equilibrium interest rate."

False. The CE is still no trade, and \(\bar r\) is the MRS at each generation's own endowment. Scaling both endowments by \((1+g)^t\) leaves the log MRS unchanged (homotheticity), so \(\bar r=\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\beta(1-\alpha)}\) as before. Growth changes the benchmark \((1+g)(1+n)\) against which \(\bar r\) is compared, not \(\bar r\) itself.

Exam-inspired
"With population growth \(n>0\), the OLG endowment equilibrium can be Pareto inefficient even though \(\bar r>0\)."

True. The best symmetric allocation dominates the CE iff \(1+\bar r<(1+g)(1+n)\). With \(0<\bar r\lt n\) (and \(g=0\)), the CE is inefficient. Equivalently, the aggregate endowment grows at rate \((1+g)(1+n)\) while prices fall at \(1+\bar r\), so its value \(\sum_t\big(\frac{(1+g)(1+n)}{1+\bar r}\big)^{t}\) is infinite. "\(r>0\) ⇒ efficient" holds only without growth.

Exam-inspired Note III pp.25–27
"In the OLG endowment economy with a fixed PAYG tax \(\tau\) per young person, a permanent fall in the population growth rate \(n\) hurts the current old but benefits future generations."

False. Each old person receives \((1+n)\tau\). After the fall, the current old receive less, so they lose. Every future generation still pays \(\tau\) when young and receives \((1+n')\tau<(1+n)\tau\) when old: a lower return on the same contribution. Its budget line rotates around autarky toward it, so it loses too. Nobody gains.

Exam-inspired
"In the log example with endowments in both periods, desired savings \(s(r)\) are strictly increasing in \(r\); with log utility and no old-age endowment they would not depend on \(r\) at all."

True. \(s(r)=\beta(1-\alpha)-\frac{(1-\beta)\alpha}{1+r}\). With log utility, income and substitution effects cancel on the young's own wages, so only the old-age endowment term moves with \(r\). A higher \(r\) lowers its present value \(\alpha/(1+r)\), the human-wealth effect, and so raises saving. With no old-age endowment, \(s=\beta\times\)(young endowment), which is flat in \(r\). That is the Diamond-model case of Session 3.

4. Guided derivations

Past exam · 2017/18 Midterm 1, "OLG and trees", parts 1–6 60 pts

Two-period lives, one household per cohort. Generation \(t\) has endowment \(e_t>0\) when young and nothing when old, and utility \(u^t(c^t_t,c^t_{t+1})\) strictly increasing and concave. There is one tree, paying dividends \(d_t\ge0\); the initial old own it. Feasibility: \(c^{t-1}_t+c^t_t=d_t+e_t\). The young buy \(a_t\) trees at ex-dividend price \(q_t\) and bonds \(s_t\) paying \(1+r_t\): \(c^t_t+a_tq_t+s_t=e_t\), \(c^t_{t+1}=a_t(q_{t+1}+d_{t+1})+s_t(1+r_t)\).

  1. (10 pts) What is the equilibrium \(a_t\)? Why?
  2. (10 pts) What is the equilibrium \(s_t\)? Why?
  3. (10 pts, 1 line) For an interior solution, relate \(1+r_t\) to \((q_{t+1}+d_{t+1})/q_t\).
  4. (10 pts, 1 equation) Let \(\sigma_t(R)\equiv\arg\max_xu^t(e_t-x,xR)\). Write a difference equation for \(q_t\).
  5. (10 pts) With constant \(\bar e,\bar u,\bar d\), write the equation for a steady state \(\bar q\).
  6. (10 pts) Assume Inada conditions and \(\bar d>0\). Is \(\bar r>0\)? Is the steady-state allocation Pareto optimal?

1. \(a_t=1\): the old will not be alive next period, so they sell; the supply of trees is 1 and only the young hold them.
2. \(s_t=0\): bonds are in zero net supply and all young are identical, and the old cannot borrow because they will not be there to repay.
3. \(1+r_t=\dfrac{q_{t+1}+d_{t+1}}{q_t}\) (no arbitrage between trees and bonds).
4. Total saving is \(a_tq_t+s_t=q_t\), so \(\sigma_t\!\left(\dfrac{q_{t+1}+d_{t+1}}{q_t}\right)=q_t\).
5. \(\bar\sigma\!\left(1+\dfrac{\bar d}{\bar q}\right)=\bar q\).
6. Inada makes saving strictly positive at any finite return, so \(\bar q=\bar\sigma(\cdot)>0\) and \(\bar r=\bar d/\bar q>0\). Yes, PO, but justify it with the value argument: \(p_t=(1+\bar r)^{-(t-1)}\), so the value of the aggregate endowment is \((\bar e+\bar d)(1+\bar r)/\bar r<\infty\), and the 1WT proof goes through. "\(r>0\) so the 1WT applies" alone is incomplete.

Past exam · 2017/18 Midterm 1, "OLG and trees", parts 7–12 60 pts

Continue with the steady state \(\bar\sigma(1+\bar d/\bar q)=\bar q\). Answer each in 1–3 lines.

  1. (10 pts) If \(\bar\sigma\) is strictly increasing, is the steady state unique?
  2. (10 pts) \(\bar d\) rises. What happens to \(\bar q\) and \(\bar r\)?
  3. (10 pts) Preferences shift so that the young save more at every interest rate. \(\bar q\)? \(\bar r\)?
  4. (10 pts) Both goods are normal and \(\bar e\) rises. \(\bar q\)? \(\bar r\)?
  5. (10 pts) \(\bar u\) is homothetic and \(\bar e,\bar d\) are both multiplied by \(\lambda\). \(\bar q\)? \(\bar r\)?
  6. (10 pts) \(\bar u=(1-\beta)\log c_y+\beta\log c_o\). Solve for \(\bar q\) and \(\bar r\).

7. Yes. The left side \(\bar\sigma(1+\bar d/q)\) is decreasing in \(q\) (the return falls as \(q\) rises) and the right side \(q\) is increasing, so they cross at most once. They do cross: near \(q=0\) the left side is positive, and at \(q=\bar e\) it is below \(\bar e\) (Inada keeps \(c_y>0\)).
8. The left-side curve shifts up, so \(\bar q\) rises. And \(\bar\sigma(1+\bar r')=\bar q'>\bar q=\bar\sigma(1+\bar r)\) with \(\bar\sigma\) increasing, so \(\bar r\) rises too. (With log utility \(\bar q\) does not move at all, and \(\bar r\) still rises.)
9. The new function satisfies \(\tilde\sigma(R)\ge\bar\sigma(R)\). The curve shifts up, so \(\bar q\) rises and \(\bar r=\bar d/\bar q\) falls. (A circulating answer key writes this inequality backwards.)
10. Old-age consumption \(c_o=xR\) is normal, so at any \(R\) the saving \(x\) rises with \(\bar e\). As in 9: \(\bar q\) rises and \(\bar r\) falls.
11. Homotheticity gives \(\bar\sigma(R;\lambda\bar e)=\lambda\bar\sigma(R;\bar e)\). Try \(\lambda\bar q\): the return \(1+\lambda\bar d/(\lambda\bar q)\) is unchanged, and the equation holds. By uniqueness, \(\bar q\) scales by \(\lambda\) and \(\bar r\) is unchanged.
12. \(\sigma=\beta\bar e\) for every \(R\), so \(\bar q=\beta\bar e\) and \(\bar r=\bar d/(\beta\bar e)\).

Exam-inspired 40 pts

Log example, endowments \((1-\alpha,\alpha)\), \(p_1=1\).

  1. (10 pts, 1 line) Write \(p_t\) in terms of \(\bar r\).
  2. (10 pts, 2 lines) Compute \(p\cdot\sum_i\bar x^i\) for each sign of \(\bar r\).
  3. (10 pts, 2 lines) For which parameters does the 1WT apply? What do you conclude in the other cases?
  4. (10 pts, 3 lines) When \(\beta>\alpha\), show that each generation's improved bundle \((1-\beta,\beta)\) costs more at CE prices than its endowment, and explain why that is no contradiction.

(a) \(p_t=(1+\bar r)^{-(t-1)}\).
(b) Aggregate consumption is 1 at each date, so the value is \(\sum_{t\ge1}(1+\bar r)^{-(t-1)}\): equal to \((1+\bar r)/\bar r\) if \(\bar r>0\), and \(+\infty\) if \(\bar r\le0\).
(c) The 1WT applies when \(\bar r>0\iff\beta<\alpha\): the CE is PO. When \(\bar r\le0\) the theorem is silent. Case 1 (\(\beta>\alpha\)) turns out inefficient and Case 2 (\(\beta=\alpha\)) efficient.
(d) The bundle costs \(p_t(1-\beta)+p_{t+1}\beta\) against an endowment worth \(p_t(1-\alpha)+p_{t+1}\alpha\). The difference is \(p_t(\beta-\alpha)\big(\frac{1}{1+\bar r}-1\big)>0\), since \(\bar r<0\). So every agent's improvement costs more, exactly as move (2) of the proof says. Summing gives \(\infty>\infty\), so no contradiction arises: the economy can afford it because the chain of transfers never ends.

Exam-inspired 50 pts

Growing economy: cohort size \(N_t=(1+n)^t\); generation \(t\) has endowment \((1+g)^t(1-\alpha)\) young, \((1+g)^t\alpha\) old; log utility with weight \(\beta\). Let \(G\equiv(1+g)(1+n)\). Normalize consumption by \((1+g)^t\): \(\hat c_y=c^t_t/(1+g)^t\), \(\hat c_o=c^t_{t+1}/(1+g)^t\).

  1. (10 pts, 1 line) Write the normalized resource constraint for symmetric allocations.
  2. (10 pts, 1 line) Find the best symmetric allocation.
  3. (15 pts, 3 lines) Show it Pareto-dominates the CE iff \(1+\bar r\lt G\).
  4. (15 pts, 2 lines) Show the value of the aggregate endowment is infinite under the same condition.
Hint for (a)

At date \(t\) there are \(N_t\) young and \(N_{t-1}\) old. Divide date-\(t\) feasibility by \(N_{t-1}(1+g)^{t-1}\).

(a) \(G\hat c_y+\hat c_o=(1-\alpha)G+\alpha\equiv W\).
(b) Maximize \((1-\beta)\log\hat c_y+\beta\log\hat c_o\) on the line: \(\hat c_y^*=(1-\beta)W/G\), \(\hat c_o^*=\beta W\).
(c) Generations \(t\ge1\) weakly prefer the best point to their endowment, strictly unless the endowment is the best point. So domination comes down to the initial old: we need \(\hat c^*_o>\alpha\), i.e. \(\beta[(1-\alpha)G+\alpha]>\alpha\), i.e. \(G>\frac{\alpha(1-\beta)}{\beta(1-\alpha)}=1+\bar r\).
(d) Aggregate endowment at date \(t\) is proportional to \(G^t\), and \(p_t\propto(1+\bar r)^{-t}\). So the value is proportional to \(\sum_t\big(G/(1+\bar r)\big)^t\), which is infinite iff \(G\ge1+\bar r\): the same condition, up to the knife edge.
