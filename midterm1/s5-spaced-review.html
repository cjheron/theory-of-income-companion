2. Set A (about 20 min)

Exam-inspired Welfare theorems
"Finite exchange economy with \(X^i=\mathbb R^m_+\). If every consumer's utility is strictly increasing in at least one good (possibly a different good for each consumer), every competitive equilibrium allocation is Pareto optimal."

True. If \(u^i\) is strictly increasing in good \(k\), then from any \(x\) the bundle \(x+\varepsilon e_k\) is in \(X^i\), within \(\varepsilon\) of \(x\), and strictly better. So LNS holds for every consumer, and the 1WT applies. Monotonicity in every good is not needed.

Exam-inspired OLG
"In the log OLG example, the initial old always prefer the best symmetric allocation \((1-\beta,\beta)\) to the competitive equilibrium."

False. The initial old consume \(\alpha\) in the CE and \(\beta\) under the best symmetric allocation. They prefer it only if \(\beta>\alpha\) (Case 1, \(\bar r<0\)). When \(\beta<\alpha\) they lose, which is exactly why the CE is PO in Case 3.

Exam-inspired Diamond
"In the Diamond model with log utility (weight \(\beta\) on old age), \(F=AK^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}\) and no government, a higher \(\beta\) raises the steady-state capital stock and lowers the steady-state interest rate."

True. \(K_{t+1}=\beta(1-\alpha)AK_t^\alpha\), so \(\bar K=[\beta(1-\alpha)A]^{1/(1-\alpha)}\), increasing in \(\beta\). And \(\bar r=\alpha A\bar K^{\alpha-1}-\delta=\frac{\alpha}{\beta(1-\alpha)}-\delta\), decreasing in \(\beta\). More thrift moves the economy toward, and possibly past, the golden rule.

Exam-inspired Planner
"Two agents share the same strictly concave, additively separable utility \(u(x)=\sum_lv_l(x_l)\). At an interior solution of the planner's problem with \(\lambda_1>\lambda_2\), agent 1 gets more of every good."

True. FOC: \(\lambda_1v_l'(x^1_l)=\lambda_2v_l'(x^2_l)=\gamma_l\), so \(v_l'(x^1_l)\lt v_l'(x^2_l)\). Since \(v_l'\) is strictly decreasing, \(x^1_l>x^2_l\) for every \(l\). (Without separability, lower marginal utilities need not mean more of every good.)

Exam-inspired Blanchard–Yaari
"In Blanchard–Yaari, a higher death rate \(p\) raises the household's marginal propensity to consume out of total wealth."

True. \(c=(\theta+p)(v+h)\): the propensity is \(\theta+p\). A shorter expected horizon makes the household effectively more impatient (utility is discounted at \(\theta+p\)). Note that the growth rate of individual consumption, \(r-\theta\), does not depend on \(p\).

Exam-inspired Welfare theorems
"If every household satisfies LNS, there is no competitive equilibrium in which some household's budget constraint is slack."

True. If \(p\cdot\bar x^i\lt w^i\), a small ball around \(\bar x^i\) is affordable, and by LNS it contains a strictly better bundle, so \(\bar x^i\) was not optimal. (This is move (1) of the 1WT proof in another guise.)

Exam-inspired OLG
"In the log OLG endowment economy with \(\beta>\alpha\), a stationary equilibrium in which the initial old hold intrinsically useless fiat money achieves the same allocation as the optimal PAYG system."

True. With a constant price level the real return on money is 1, i.e. \(r=0\). The young hold real balances \(M/P=s(0)=\beta-\alpha>0\), consume \(1-\beta\), and consume \(\beta\) when old. The initial old consume \(\alpha+M/P=\beta\). This is exactly PAYG with \(\tau=\beta-\alpha\). It exists only if \(\beta>\alpha\), i.e. when \(\bar r<0\). (In a finite economy money could not be valued.)

Exam-inspired Negishi
"If the CE gives household \(i\) zero consumption of good \(l\) (a corner), the equation \(p_l=u^i_l/\mu_i\) can fail for that good."

True. The Kuhn–Tucker condition is \(u^i_l\le\mu_ip_l\), with equality if \(x^i_l>0\). At a corner the inequality can be strict: the good is too expensive for \(i\) at the margin. That is why the Fall 2025 item assumed Inada conditions.

3. Set B (about 20 min)

Exam-inspired Welfare theorems
"The Second Welfare Theorem requires each firm's production set \(Y^j\) to be convex."

False, strictly speaking. Assumption FF requires only the aggregate production set \(Y=\sum_jY^j\) to be convex, because the proof separates \(A\) from \(B=Y+\sum_ie^i\). Convexity of each \(Y^j\) is sufficient for that but not necessary. (On the other side, the 1WT needs no convexity at all.)

Exam-inspired OLG
"With productivity growth \(g>0\) and no population growth, the OLG endowment equilibrium is Pareto-dominated by the best symmetric allocation whenever \(\bar r\lt g\)."

True. The condition is \(1+\bar r<(1+g)(1+n)\); with \(n=0\) that is \(\bar r\lt g\). Equivalently, the aggregate endowment grows faster than prices discount it, so its value is infinite. A positive interest rate is not enough for efficiency in a growing economy.

cf. PSET 1 P1 Note III
"When PAYG with tax \(\theta\) is privatized (same PV of taxes for each generation, paid when young), the young receive a net subsidy rather than pay a tax if \(\bar r<0\)."

True. The privatized tax is \(\tilde\tau^t_t=\theta-\frac{\theta}{1+\bar r}=\frac{\theta\bar r}{1+\bar r}\), which is negative when \(\bar r<0\). Under PAYG they receive \(\theta\) when old, worth more than the \(\theta\) they paid young (a negative interest rate makes the future valuable). The PV-equivalent of that is a subsidy now. The debt is still constant at \(\theta/(1+\bar r)\).

cf. PSET 1 P5 Note III
"In the \(N\)-period OLG model, the \((\tau,Y)\) transfer (give \(\tau\) at ages \(1..Y\), receive \(Y\tau/(N-Y)\) at ages \(Y+1..N\)) raises generation \(t\)'s utility at \(\tau=0\) iff the average price of its receiving dates exceeds the average price of its paying dates."

True. By the FOCs, \(dU^t/d\tau|_0=\lambda^t\big[-\sum_{a\le Y}p_{t+a-1}+\frac{Y}{N-Y}\sum_{a>Y}p_{t+a-1}\big]=\lambda^tY\big[\bar p_{\text{late}}-\bar p_{\text{early}}\big]\). Rising prices (negative interest rates) make this positive for every generation.

Exam-inspired Blanchard–Yaari
"In Blanchard–Yaari, Ricardian equivalence holds if labour income does not decline with age (\(\alpha=0\)), even when \(p>0\)."

False. What breaks equivalence is \(p>0\): part of the future tax falls on people not yet born, so \(dH=-dT[1-e^{-ps}]\neq0\). Equivalence holds iff \(p=0\) (infinitely lived households), whatever \(\alpha\) is.

Exam-inspired Planner
"If every \(u^i\) is concave, the representative agent's utility \(U(x;\lambda)=\max\{\sum_i\lambda_iu^i(x^i):\sum_ix^i=x\}\) is concave in \(x\)."

True. Take optimal splits \(\{x^i\}\) of \(x\) and \(\{y^i\}\) of \(y\). The mixture \(\{tx^i+(1-t)y^i\}\) is a feasible split of \(tx+(1-t)y\), and by concavity of each \(u^i\) it gives at least \(tU(x)+(1-t)U(y)\). The planner can do no worse with averages.

Exam-inspired Welfare theorems
"Lump-sum taxes and transfers break the 1WT, because they change households' budget sets."

False. Lump-sum transfers only change each household's wealth. Everyone still faces the same prices, and the proof never uses the wealth levels, only optimality at prices \(p\), profit maximization and feasibility. (That is exactly why the 2WT can use them.) Distorting taxes break it, because households and firms then face different prices.

Exam-inspired OLG synthesis
"In the Note II log economy (no growth), the CE allocation is Pareto optimal if and only if \(\bar r\ge0\)."

True. \(\bar r>0\): finite value, 1WT applies. \(\bar r=0\) (\(\alpha=\beta\)): the CE is the best symmetric allocation and is PO, even though the value is infinite. \(\bar r<0\): the best symmetric allocation Pareto-dominates it. So the cutoff is exactly at 0, and the 1WT alone gets you only the \(\bar r>0\) part.

4. Set C (about 20 min)

Exam-inspired Welfare theorems
"Arrow's remark (a cheaper point turns expenditure minimization into utility maximization) uses continuity of the utility function."

True. The proof mixes a strictly better affordable bundle \(\hat x\) with a little of the cheaper point \(\tilde x\): \(x^\theta=\theta\tilde x+(1-\theta)\hat x\) is strictly cheaper than \(\bar x\), and continuity keeps it strictly better than \(\bar x\) for small \(\theta\). That contradicts expenditure minimization.

Exam-inspired OLG
"With population growth \(n\) and no productivity growth, contributing \(\tau\) to PAYG when young is like holding an asset with gross return \(1+n\)."

True. Each old person receives the contributions of \(1+n\) young: \((1+n)\tau\). So PAYG beats private saving iff \(1+n>1+\bar r\), which is the same condition as the CE being inefficient.

cf. PSET 1 P2 Q4 Diamond
"In the Diamond model with a government holding assets \(B_t\) (Note III sign convention), Walras' law implies \(B_t+s_t=K_{t+1}\) at every date, given the initial condition."

True. Adding the young's, old's and government's budgets, using CRS (\(F=F_KK+F_LL\)), \(v_t=r_{t-1}+\delta\) and goods-market clearing, gives \(B_t+s_t-K_{t+1}=(1+r_{t-1})(B_{t-1}+s_{t-1}-K_t)\). This is zero by induction from \(B_0+s_0=K_1\).

Exam-inspired Blanchard–Yaari
"In Blanchard–Yaari with capital, a higher death rate \(p\) lowers the steady-state capital stock."

True. The steady state solves \(\frac{\mathcal F(\bar K)}{\bar K}=\frac{(p+\alpha)(p+\theta)}{\mathcal F'(\bar K)+\alpha-\theta}\). The left side falls in \(K\), the right side rises in \(K\), and a higher \(p\) raises the right side, so they cross at a lower \(\bar K\). Shorter horizons mean more generational turnover, so less wealth is accumulated.

Exam-inspired Blanchard–Yaari
"In the Blanchard–Yaari endowment economy, a faster decline of labour income with age (higher \(\alpha\)) raises the equilibrium interest rate."

False: \(r=\theta-\alpha\) falls. People want to save for old age, when their income will be low, but in the aggregate nobody can save (no capital, no debt). The interest rate falls until they are content to consume their declining income. It is the continuous-time analogue of Note II, where a lower old-age endowment share \(\alpha\) lowers \(\bar r\).

Exam-inspired cf. Fall 2005 Ex. 1 Q2
"With log utility and subjective beliefs \(\pi^i_s\), the Pareto optimal allocation with weights \(\lambda\) gives agent \(i\) the share \(\frac{\lambda_i\pi^i_s}{\sum_j\lambda_j\pi^j_s}\) of the aggregate endowment in state \(s\)."

True. FOC \(\lambda_i\pi^i_s/x^i_s=\gamma_s\) gives \(x^i_s=\lambda_i\pi^i_s/\gamma_s\); feasibility gives \(\gamma_s=\sum_j\lambda_j\pi^j_s/\bar e_s\). The share varies with \(s\) whenever beliefs differ: agent \(i\) gets more in the states it considers relatively likely. With common beliefs the share is \(\lambda_i/\sum_j\lambda_j\), constant.

Exam-inspired Welfare theorems
"With incomplete markets, a competitive equilibrium is always constrained Pareto optimal."

False, because of "always". In the simple case of a missing market for one good with fixed endowments, the CE is constrained PO (the planner must respect the same trading restrictions). But with several goods per date or state, prices on the open markets affect how valuable the incomplete trades are, and even constrained efficiency typically fails (Geanakoplos–Polemarchakis 1986).

Exam-inspired Negishi
"Smooth exchange economy with concave, differentiable utilities and interior solutions. If \(\bar x\) solves the planner's problem with weights \(\lambda\gg0\) and multipliers \(\gamma\), then \(\bar x\) is a CE allocation with prices \(p=\gamma\) and endowments \(e^i=\bar x^i\)."

True. Set \(\mu_i=1/\lambda_i\). The planner's FOC \(\lambda_iu^i_l=\gamma_l\) becomes \(u^i_l=\mu_ip_l\), the household FOC. With \(e^i=\bar x^i\), \(\bar x^i\) is affordable with the budget binding, and concavity makes the FOC sufficient, so \(\bar x^i\) is optimal. Markets clear because \(\bar x\) is feasible.
